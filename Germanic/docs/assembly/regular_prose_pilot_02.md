The regular item here is not the better-known masculine dual _bēġen_ but the inherited neuter dual _bū_. Kroonen's paradigm under _\*ba-_ includes precisely that unextended _\*bō_ form alongside the wider dual set, and the Old English grammars likewise preserve neuter _bū_ beside masculine _bēġen_ and feminine _bā_ [@Kroonen2013, 47; @SieversBrunner1965, 324; @Campbell1959, 295; @Fulk2018, 223]. That makes _bū_ the cleanest regular comparator for this entry: it is attested Old English, and it does not depend on the more debated extended formation behind _bēġen_. Campbell and Brunner both treat final stressed _ō > ū_ as an ordinary West Germanic-to-Old-English development, with _bū_ among the standard examples [@Campbell1959, 122; @SieversBrunner1965, 69].

Comparison note. The historical discussion of _bēġen_ and the continental forms behind Modern English _both_ belongs to a related but different problem. Orel still cites an older _\*bō-j-_ explanation, while Fulk reports Seebold's preference for _\*bō-þ-_ [@Orel2003, 45; @Fulk2018, 223]. Those formations matter for the wider history of the numeral, but they do not displace the regular comparison _\*bō > bū_ used here.

Orel and Kroonen both place the verb in the ordinary class-II show-family, and Brunner's Old English evidence _scēawian_, _scāwian_ confirms that the entry is dealing with the standard lexeme rather than a special paradigm cell [@Orel2003, 339; @Kroonen2013, 482; @SieversBrunner1965, 148]. The regularity lies in the treatment of _aw_ before a following vowel: the inherited form develops to Old English _ēaw_, while the class-II suffix preserves _ō_ between _w_ and _j_, yielding the expected _sċēawian_ [@Campbell1959, 138; @Orel2003, 339]. The remaining wrinkle is orthographic, not philological.

Form note. The difference between dictionary _scēawian_ and the book spelling _sċēawian_ is only normalization of initial <sc> [@Campbell1959, 19; @HoggPhonology1992, p. 90].

Kroonen's double-stem noun _\*wēbna- ~ wēpna-_ and Campbell's discussion of cluster nouns both show why this entry cannot be reduced to the familiar late West Saxon headword _wǣpen_ [@Kroonen2013, 617; @Campbell1959, 150; @Campbell1959, 226]. Old English evidence preserves both broken and unbroken forms: Bright contrasts broken nominative _wǣpen/wapen_ with unbroken _wǣpnes_, and Clark Hall likewise records the headword under _wapen_ while preserving unbroken forms elsewhere [@BrightCassidyRingler1971, 29; @ClarkHall1960, 355]. The point of the book form _wǣpn_ is to represent the regular unbroken cluster word-finally, which the trace derives cleanly from _\*wḗpną_.

Form note. The common simplex headword _wǣpen_ is real Old English, but it reflects later breaking in the simplex. The unbroken forms remain essential evidence for the regular line modeled here [@Campbell1959, 150; @Campbell1959, 226; @BrightCassidyRingler1971, 29; @ClarkHall1960, 355].
