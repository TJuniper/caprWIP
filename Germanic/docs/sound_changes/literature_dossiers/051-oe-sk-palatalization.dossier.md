## Historical problem

`SC051` is the cleanest palatalization candidate exposed by the
`SC049-SC052` split review. Unlike the mixed four-change parent scaffold, it has
interpretable two-sided evidence and a fairly tight lexical profile: it must
follow `SC046` OE A Restoration on the left and precede `SC056` OE Ws Palatal
Diphthongization on the right.

That makes `SC051` a plausible standalone production candidate. But the dossier
needs to answer a narrower question than a general Old English palatalization
chapter: whether the handbook tradition treats `sk > sc` as a distinct enough
process to support a self-contained report, while still acknowledging its
entanglement with the broader velar-palatalization complex around `SC052` and
with later West Saxon palatal diphthongization.

The practical task, then, is to align three levels without overstating any one
of them:

1. the local CAPR card relation `SC046 < SC051 < SC056`;
2. the broader handbook description of OE `sc` development;
3. the unresolved structural question of whether `SC051` should remain
   standalone or later be paired with `SC052`.

## Source dossier

### Campbell 1959

- source_key: Campbell1959
- locator: §170; §§440--441
- terminology: initial palatal consonants; palatalization and assibilation of `sk`
- short quotation: "`[sk]` is more prone to palatalization and assibilation than `[k]`."
- paraphrase: Campbell separates two useful layers. In §170 he treats initial `sk` alongside the fronting of initial `k` and `g`, first under the same front-vowel conditions and then as a later generalized initial palatalization. In §§440--441 he gives the fuller `sc` system: initial `sc` becomes widely palatalized, medial `sc` palatalizes before front-vocalic conditions, but medial and final `[sk]` remain after back-vowel environments. [@Campbell1959, §170; §§440--441]
- conditioning: initially `sk` patterns with fronted `k`/`g` before front vowels; later, "perhaps about 800", every initial `sk` is palatalized. Internally, palatalization and assibilation occur before front vowels and after umlauted vowels, but not before back vowels in forms like `wascan`/`waxan` or in back-vowel-final environments. [@Campbell1959, §170; §§440--441]
- chronology: Campbell's organization implies that the basic palatalization of `sk` belongs to the earlier palatal-consonant zone, while later glide-vowel and diphthong effects belong to subsequent sections. This supports treating `SC051` as earlier than the West Saxon palatal-diphthongization material reflected in `SC056`. [@Campbell1959, §170; §§179--184; §§440--441]
- examples: `scip`; `scieppan`; `sceal`; `wasce`; `asce`; `fisces`; `flǣsc`; `wascan`/`waxan`. [@Campbell1959, §170; §§440--441]
- cautions: Campbell does not isolate `sk` palatalization as a wholly separate chapter from the rest of OE palatalization. His treatment is strong support for the rule itself, but only partial support for a fully isolated production chapter.

### Hogg 1992

- source_key: HoggPhonology1992
- locator: pp. 107--108; pp. 111--112
- terminology: palatalisation; assibilation; cluster `*/sk/`; palatal diphthongisation
- short quotation: "The cluster `*/sk/` underwent a parallel change to `/ʃ/`."
- paraphrase: Hogg gives one of the clearest direct descriptions of `*/sk/` as a special case within OE palatalization. He says the cluster change is more widespread than plain velar palatalization because the alveolar `s` reinforces movement toward the palate, and he explicitly distinguishes medial cases like `waste` from back-vowel-preserving forms like `ascad`. He then treats palatal diphthongization separately, after palatalization, as a further effect of the new palatals on following stressed vowels. [@HoggPhonology1992, pp. 112--113]
- conditioning: word-initial `*/sk/` palatalizes broadly; medially it palatalizes only under the same conditions as `*/k/`, so `waste` is palatalized but `ascad` is not. Later palatal diphthongization affects following vowels in forms such as `sceap`. [@HoggPhonology1992, pp. 112--113]
- chronology: Hogg's chapter order is particularly useful for CAPR. He treats `sk` palatalization before palatal diphthongization, which fits the local card claim that `SC051` must precede `SC056`. [@HoggPhonology1992, pp. 112--113]
- examples: `waste`; `ascad`; `ascian`/`axian`; `scip`; `disc`; `æsc`; `sceap`. [@HoggPhonology1992, pp. 112--113]
- cautions: Hogg strongly supports the distinctness of the `sk` development, but he still embeds it in a larger palatalization-and-assibilation chapter rather than treating it as fully independent from `k/g` palatalization.

### Ringe and Taylor 2014

- source_key: RingeTaylor2014
- locator: §§6.4.1; 6.5.1
- terminology: palatalization of velars; cluster `*sk`; West Saxon diphthongization by initial palatals
- short quotation: "The cluster `*sk` developed somewhat differently."
- paraphrase: Ringe and Taylor are especially useful because they separate the processes very cleanly. In §6.4.1 they describe `*sk` as partly parallel to, but not identical with, the palatalization of plain velars: initial `*sk` first palatalizes before front vowels, later generalizes word-initially before all vowels, while medial and final cases remain sensitive to neighboring back vowels. In §6.5.1 they then treat West Saxon diphthongization as a separate later effect after initial palatals, including `scield`, `scieppan`, `scieran`, `sceaft`, and `sceap`. [@RingeTaylor2014, §§6.4.1, 6.5.1]
- conditioning: initial `*sk` is first palatalized before front vowels, then generalized before all vowels; medial `*sk` is palatalized unless followed by a back vowel; final `*sk` is palatalized unless preceded by a back vowel. Diphthongization later affects stressed non-high front vowels after initial palatals in West Saxon. [@RingeTaylor2014, §§6.4.1, 6.5.1]
- chronology: this source gives the strongest literary support for the local CAPR right boundary. It places the `sk` palatalization process before West Saxon diphthongization, not as the same event. That matches the direction of `SC051 < SC056` even if the CAPR card relation is narrower than the whole textbook chronology. [@RingeTaylor2014, §§6.4.1, 6.5.1]
- examples: `scield`; `scieppan`; `scieran`; `sceaft`; `sceap`; `sceal`; `sceatt`. [@RingeTaylor2014, §6.5.1]
- cautions: Ringe and Taylor also emphasize the broader palatalization complex, so a future SC051 report should not imply that handbook history consists of a single isolated `sk` rule with no overlap into neighboring palatal processes.

### Luick 1914

- source_key: Luick1914
- locator: §§168; 171--174
- terminology: frühe Verschiebungen in palataler Richtung; anlautende Palatale; Diphthongierung
- short quotation: "die Folge `sk` (`sc`) ... vor allen Vokalen palatal wurde"
- paraphrase: Luick frames the whole zone as a sequence of "early shifts in a palatal direction". Within that larger field, he states that OE `sc` became palatal before all vowels, earlier before front vowels, and then treats the diphthongal effects of initial palatals in later sections. This is useful for explaining why `SC051` is a real rule but still historically embedded in a broader palatal environment. [@Luick1914, §168; §§171--174]
- conditioning: Luick explicitly says that the `sk > sc` development is earliest before bright/front vowels and later generalized more broadly; the ensuing diphthongization by initial palatals is a separate development with West Saxon forms such as `scield`, `scieran`, `sceaft`, and `sceap`. [@Luick1914, §168; §§171--172]
- chronology: Luick's ordering supports a narrow historical distinction between palatalization proper and later diphthongization. That helps keep `SC051` from being swallowed either by `SC052` on the left or by `SC056` on the right. [@Luick1914, §168; §§171--174]
- examples: `scield`; `scieran`; `sceaft`; `sceap`; `sceal`; `sceatt`. [@Luick1914, §172]
- cautions: Luick is best for large-scale framing, not for the exact CAPR witness set. His presentation argues for contextualizing SC051 inside a larger palatalization chapter even if the report itself stays narrow.

### Fulk 2018

- source_key: Fulk2018
- locator: p. 28; §4.13
- terminology: palatal or velar `sc`; diphthongization by initial palatal consonants
- short quotation: "`sc` represents `[ʃ]` everywhere except internally before or finally after a back vowel"
- paraphrase: Fulk gives a concise summary of exactly the contrast SC051 needs. In his phonological description, OE `sc` is normally palatal but remains `[sk]` internally before or finally after a back vowel. Later, in §4.13, he treats diphthongization by initial palatal consonants as a distinct source of OE diphthongs and lists `scieran` among the examples. [@Fulk2018, p. 28; §4.13]
- conditioning: the `sc` outcome is palatal almost everywhere, except in the back-vowel environments that preserve `[sk]`; diphthongization after initial palatals affects West Saxon front vowels in forms like `scieran`. [@Fulk2018, p. 28; §4.13]
- chronology: Fulk's compact formulation also keeps the two processes distinct. The palatal cluster behavior belongs to the phonological setup; diphthongization is a later vowel effect. [@Fulk2018, §4.13]
- examples: `scieran`; `ceaster`; `geaf`; `giest`. [@Fulk2018, §4.13]
- cautions: Fulk is excellent for concise comparative framing, but he does not by himself decide whether `SC051` should be promoted alone or paired later with `SC052`.

### Sievers-Brunner 1965

- source_key: SieversBrunner1965
- locator: §91.a; §50; §105 Anm. 2
- terminology: palatale Aussprache des `k` in anlautendem `sc`; Stellung nach Palatalen
- short quotation: "bei anlautendem `sc` hat sich vor allen Vokalen ... palatale Aussprache des `k` entwickelt"
- paraphrase: Sievers-Brunner gives an older but still useful grammar anchor for the same structural picture. The grammar states that initial `sc` develops palatal `k` before all vowels in Old English and then lists the resulting West Saxon outcomes among the vowel developments after palatals. Elsewhere it preserves the contrastive back-vowel material in forms like `wascan, waxan`. [@SieversBrunner1965, §91.a; §50; §105 Anm. 2]
- conditioning: the old grammar treats initial `sc` as palatalized across vowel contexts, while also preserving back-vowel counterevidence in forms such as `wascan`/`waxan`; the diphthongized West Saxon reflexes belong to the later vowel developments after palatals. [@SieversBrunner1965, §91.a; §50]
- chronology: this source supports the same layered reading as the later handbooks: palatalization first, then the West Saxon vowel effects. [@SieversBrunner1965, §91.a]
- examples: `sceaft`; `scieppan`; `wascan`; `waxan`. [@SieversBrunner1965, §91.a; §50]
- cautions: because it is an older grammar, it is most useful as corroboration of the traditional analysis, not as the sole basis for chapter architecture.

## CAPR formulation versus literature

CAPR's `OESkPalatalization` corresponds to a real and easily recognizable
handbook process. Every major source above treats OE `sc` as a genuine
palatalization development and also distinguishes it in some way from the
plain-velar palatalization story.

But the sources do **not** all treat it as a cleanly isolated chapter. Most of
them embed `sk > sc` inside a larger palatalization complex:

1. plain velar palatalization and assibilation;
2. the special breadth of `sk` palatalization;
3. later West Saxon diphthongization after the new palatals.

That is exactly where CAPR sharpens the picture. `SC051` names the `sk`
palatalization rule itself, while `SC052` and `SC056` hold adjacent parts of the
broader complex. This is analytically useful for order-testing, but the eventual
report will need to say plainly that CAPR is separating phenomena the handbooks
often discuss together.

The clearest positive alignment is:

1. `SC051` corresponds to a historically real `sk > sc` development.
2. `SC046` on the left and `SC056` on the right are both historically
   intelligible neighbors.
3. `SC051 < SC056` is well supported by handbook sequencing that places
   palatalization before West Saxon palatal diphthongization.

The main caution is:

1. the handbooks do not automatically imply that `SC051` should absorb `SC052`,
   `SC056`, or the whole `SC049-SC052` scaffold into a single promoted chapter;
2. nor do they imply that `SC051` must remain forever standalone.

## Chronology implications

The chronology cards and the literature fit together reasonably well here.

1. `SC051` must follow `SC046`. The earlier-side witnesses `flask` and `wash`
   are historically interpretable because the sources consistently preserve
   back-vowel environments as resistant to palatal `sc`.
2. `SC051` must precede `SC056`. The later-side witnesses `shaft`, `shear`,
   `sheath`, `sheep`, and `shield` fit the literary picture in which
   palatalization precedes the West Saxon vowel effects of initial palatals.
3. Both boundaries are fairly local. This is not a case of runner-bounded
   overreading or of projecting a very distant handbook chronology onto the
   card.
4. The report should therefore not absorb all of `SC049-SC052`. `SC049` remains
   residual, while `SC050` and `SC052` remain structurally related but not yet
   settled.
5. `SC052` and `SC057` remain unresolved neighboring material. They may later
   need pairing or cross-reference, but that decision is still separate from the
   narrow case for `SC051`.
