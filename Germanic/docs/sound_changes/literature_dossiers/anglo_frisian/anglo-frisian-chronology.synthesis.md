# Anglo-Frisian chronology: comparative synthesis

**Single authority for comparative chronology in this research area.**
Research only: no adjudication, implementation, corpus addition or registry
verdict follows. Source cards preserve the detailed readings; the
palatalization note and witness queue defer here rather than maintaining
independent chronologies.

## 1. Governing test and notation

**Governing CAPR topology:** a strictly tree-like history **with an
Anglo-Frisian ancestral node is required**. Its existence is not the
question this dossier adjudicates. The questions are which precisely
conditioned events belong on its stem, which belong on daughter branches,
and what phonological state must be reconstructed at the node. A failed
shared-event assignment does **not** remove that node. Authors' rejection
of a node or preference for linkage is source interpretation, not CAPR's
topological conclusion. No shared innovation is guaranteed merely by
requiring the node.

An inherited shared change X must fit the common stem **before the split**.
If daughter-specific Y precedes X in B, and A lacks Y, one inherited X
fails under those premises. Either the histories are independent, the
ordering or specificity premise is wrong, or the apparent event identity
is false. CAPR cannot evade this test through wave/diffusion/linkage.
Authors' alternative frameworks remain accurately recorded: Campbell's
contact account (pp. 91–92), Bremmer's continuum (pp. 284, 292–293),
Laker's reservations (p. 166), and Versloot's areal arguments
(2017 pp. 317–319; 2021 pp. 346, 362).

**S:** surface agreement only. **H:** compatible with an inherited stem,
**not proof**. **P:** parallel/distinct under stated premises, conditional
when a load-bearing edge is disputed. **U:** unresolved identity/order.
`<` means precedes, not an absolute date. `H*`/`P*` emphasize conditional
classifications. Wider distributions prevent an **exclusive** AF diagnosis,
but do not logically invalidate stem compatibility by themselves
([@Bremmer2008, pp. 288–294; @Versloot2021, pp. 345–346]).

Evidence levels: **O** observed spelling/reflex; **D** diagnostic ordering
argument; **R** reconstructed/systemic or numbered order; **A** absolute
anchor through interpretation. D does not certify every premise.
Stiles1995 and Fulk1998 remain unavailable direct works, not acquisition
prerequisites. All thirteen specialist works now have direct source cards,
including Bremmer2009, Nielsen2001, Goblirsch1991 and Hogg1979 (cards
10–13). An earlier author's report of another work remains a report even
after that work is separately reviewed. Hogg1992, Luick,
Campbell1939, Bremmer2008 and Kortlandt2008 are held; holdings alone do not
verify every quoted passage or corrupted glyph.

**Hogg holdings identity:** the locally named
`hogg_fulk_2011_grammar_old_english_vol1_phonology.txt` and associated PDF
are Hogg alone, *A Grammar of Old English*, Volume 1, *Phonology*
(original 1992; reissue 2011), not Hogg & Fulk's Volume 2.
`hogg_vol1.txt` is the edited *Cambridge History of the English Language*
volume (1992), not that grammar. Neither filename establishes a holding
of Hogg & Fulk's Volume 2.

**Direct Luick evidence:** local adjacent printed headings establish only
the mappings sheet 177→p.129, 178→p.130, 179→p.131 and 180→p.132 in
`docs/references/luick_historische_grammatik.txt`. These are not a
whole-book offset or certification of the extract's vowel glyphs.
The following §§118–120 citations are direct readings, not reports
through Laker. Luick's §637 palatalization passage remains unlocated and
unverified; no page image has confirmed the corrupt intermediate notation.

## 2. Central competing histories

### Campbell versus Kortlandt

| Proposition | Campbell1939 | Kortlandt2008 |
|---|---|---|
| English *ai/fronting | long-ā fronting < *ai > ā < short-*a fronting (p. 90) | shared AF4 *ai > ā < AF6 restricted fronting < AF7 palatalization (p. 270) |
| Frisian *ai/fronting | short-*a fronting < *ai > **(æi)** > open long front reflex; intermediate **“may well”**, not demonstrated (pp. 90, 104) | inherited ā from AF4 later fronts in F11, except before following back vowel (pp. 268, 271) |
| Frisian *au | *au > ā **before** short fronting: steps 2–3; aa mechanism “Probably” (pp. 91, 104) | F11 ā-fronting < F12 *au > ā < F13 mutation < F14 breaking (p. 271) |
| Palatalization layers | possible common/contact-period onset, different completion histories (pp. 106–107) | early AF7 versus later English E14 (pp. 267, 271, 273) |

Campbell's reversed *ai/fronting order is central, but its Frisian
intermediate remains hedged. His independence conclusion (p. 91) is
conditional for CAPR on that reconstruction, not directly observed history.
**Earlier Frisian-only *au contraction followed by supposedly shared
fronting explains surface distribution but fails inherited-node compatibility.**
It places B-specific Y before shared X. To regain H a premise must change.

There is **no internal ai/au contradiction in Kortlandt p. 267**:
he explicitly assumes *ai had monophthongized to ā and *au remained
unchanged before early fronting/palatalization. This matches AF4 < AF6 <
AF7 (pp. 267, 270). Do not revive the earlier misreading that both
diphthongs were unchanged.

Campbell's two long front vowel strata must not be collapsed: following
card 01, `ǣ(open)` labels his difficult open glyph; close ē derives from
WGmc ā. That editorial label is not permission to normalize other
authors' notation (pp. 90, 95, 100–101, 104 n. 1).

### Direct Luick alternative to Campbell's earlier Frisian contraction

Luick calls fronting an apparently common Anglo-Frisian process:
“Offenbar liegt ein gemein-anglofriesischer Vorgang vor,” with nasal
darkening already begun before non-nasal fronting. He places its beginnings
at least in the period of continental neighborhood
([@Luick1914, p. 130, §118]). He explicitly connects the first component
of au with that fronting: “Hierauf nahm die erste Komponente an der
Aufhellung des a-Lautes teil”; subsequent English second-component
unrounding is assigned to the seventh century
([@Luick1914, pp. 130–131, §119]).

For Frisian au→ā, with ō also in North/East Frisian, he tentatively
suggests regressive assimilation: **“Vielleicht”**, not demonstrated
([@Luick1914, pp. 131–132, §120]). The intermediate glyph in that
sentence is corrupt; it is deliberately neither reproduced as a phonetic
form nor normalized. The legible prose permits a **shared-fronting
possibility followed by Frisian assimilation**, contrasting with
Campbell's Frisian contraction **before** fronting (pp. 91, 104).
These are rival histories, not interchangeable surface explanations.
Luick's possibility is H-compatible only if the common-stem placement and
subsequent daughter sequence hold; it does not prove inheritance, supply a
verified intermediate, or establish the palatalization behavior needed to
explain every surviving consonant. Campbell's earlier-Frisian-contraction
configuration still fails that shared-fronting tree test.

### Direct Goblirsch alternative: shared diphthong, daughter continuation

Goblirsch's own proposal is shared `ai > ā` and `au > ēa`, with the
Frisian continuation explicitly printed as `ēa > eā > ā`
([@Goblirsch1991, pp. 17, 21]). English/Kentish comparisons supply a
possible stress-shift/glide-loss mechanism; Frisian eu-derived jā supplies
an analogue, **not attestation of a Frisian au-derived intermediate**
([@Goblirsch1991, pp. 20–21]).

```text
Proposed common events: ai > ā; au > ēa (their mutual order unspecified)
Frisian: au > ēa < stress shift ēa > eā < contraction eā > ā
```

This reverses the relevant placement problem in Campbell's account:
Frisian contraction is a **follower** of Goblirsch's proposed shared event,
not a daughter-only predecessor. The sequence is H-compatible under its
premises, but no independently attested Frisian ēa/eā or dated pre-split
event establishes inheritance. Kentish's comparable later development
must not be erased by saying all English retained ēa unchanged
([@Goblirsch1991, pp. 17, 20–21]).

Goblirsch does **not** establish SC030 = SC043. His tentative p. 18
explanation literally prints `ā > æ`, with unmarked æ; p. 19 entertains
a diphthong-system account independent of short-a fronting. Neither
licenses silently correcting the notation into a particular general
fronting law. He supplies no own obligatory order between plain-a
fronting, velar palatalization, mutation and the au shift/contraction
([@Goblirsch1991, pp. 18–21]). His Campbell and Krupatkin chronologies
remain **reported alternatives**, not components to splice into his
preferred shared-diphthong proposal.

### Direct Frisian controls: Bremmer's handbook versus Nielsen

These are incompatible source packages, not a consensus Frisian chain:

```text
Bremmer2009, §44-preferred:
long/short fronting < palatalization < ai-monophthongization < mutation < breaking
long-ā fronting < au-monophthongization < mutation

Nielsen2001, preferred structural account:
ai/au-monophthongization ≤ long-system restructuring < short fronting
short fronting < palatal/affricated treatment < mutation
ai-derived gravity split < mutation
```

Here `≤` expressly allows co-occurrence; it is **not a strict chronology
edge**. Bremmer's initial-palatalization argument also permits the weaker
`palatalization < unrounding`, rather than securely fixing it before
mutation. Nielsen does not give a Frisian breaking/mutation order
([@Bremmer2009, pp. 27–35; @Nielsen2001, pp. 514–517, 519–521]).

Bremmer's handbook itself requires alternatives: §42a describes initial
palatalization after ai/au monophthongization, whereas §44 and its
Remark 1 explicitly place it **before ai**, using unpalatalized initials
including gēr and kēi. Do not combine these opposed edges into one chain.
Nor substitute Bremmer2008's contraction-before-short-fronting numbering
for the handbook's fronting-before-palatalization-before-ai sequence
([@Bremmer2008, pp. 286–288; @Bremmer2009, pp. 29–33]).

Nielsen's long-system restructuring follows or co-occurs with
daughter-local monophthongization, contrary to Bremmer's inherited
long-ā-fronting-before-contraction order. His short split is structurally
secondary to that long contrast. Under **those causal and daughter-only
premises**, common-stem identification of the consequent fronting and
fronting-dependent palatal treatment fails the tree test. Neither the
causal reconstruction nor its event identity is forced by final
correspondences alone; earlier nasal/oral differentiation remains
shared-compatible and must not be conflated with the later independent
long-system restructuring ([@Nielsen2001, pp. 514–517, 520–521]).

### Direct Hogg: operative palatalization versus earlier sensitivity

Hogg retains `breaking/restoration < operative palatalization < West
Mercian second fronting`. His initial breaking argument concerns **at
least phonologization**, not a proved date for the first articulatory
tendency. A reversible earlier allophonic onset is discussed, although
he prefers the simpler later chronology
([@Hogg1979, pp. 89–97, 111]).

His source does **not** settle `palatalization < mutation`: unrounded
negative witnesses favor that order, while cǣġe/dæġe under his
early-merger and syllabification premises demand the opposite. He calls
the relation unresolved and rejects proposed repairs. These are rival
diagnostic arguments, not two edges to merge into a historical chain.
Nor does he supply a matching Frisian chain establishing comparative
scope ([@Hogg1979, pp. 102–110]).

Under independently established English-only breaking/restoration,
Hogg's **operative** event cannot be inherited on the common stem.
That conditional consequence does not exclude an earlier shared
sensitivity or prove that all palatalization layers were independent.
His West Mercian second fronting is a **different event** from
English au-fronting; the phrase “second fronting” cannot identify SC030
by itself ([@Hogg1979, pp. 90–91, 96–97, 111]).

## 3. Proposition-by-proposition comparison

### 3.1 *ē1 / long ā outside nasal environments

**Outputs:** WS ǣ versus non-WS/Frisian ē, with dialect distinctions.
Campbell reconstructs WGmc ā-fronting, then raising before Frisian
restoration; Kortlandt regards WS ǣ as retention, not a new fronting
([@Campbell1939, pp. 89–90, 96, 104; @Kortlandt2008, pp. 265–266, 271]).
Bremmer's closed-syllable raising precedes contraction in his adopted
Stiles-attributed numbering; *skēp < *skāp* illustrates output, not every
edge (pp. 286–288). Repanšek's shared *ē1 split is systemic, with
later Frisian raising independent of Anglian (pp. 79–81, 84–85).
Versloot2021 dates fronting fifth century and raising after 700
(p. 345); Versloot2025 does not test long *ē1 (p. 107).
**Diagnostics/tree:** distinct later long-vowel strata constrain mergers
but do not choose retention versus backing/fronting uniquely. Early
components H-compatible; event identity overall U. Campbell supplies no
Frisian calendar date; Bremmer's before/about 500 is runic inference
(p. 288), not direct vowel measurement.

### 3.2 Nasal short a and long ā

**Outputs:** rounded low/back vowels, often o/ō, with short a/o variation.
Campbell places nasal developments earliest, distinguishing rounded
short o from old o and later branch mergers (pp. 86–89, 104–105).
Bremmer argues `rounding < nasal-spirant loss` from *tanþ > tōth*,
leaving phonemic status open (pp. 287–289). Kortlandt instead decomposes
AF1 centralization < AF2 loss < AF3 long rounding (pp. 269–270).
Repanšek separates long phonemicization from short nasal allophony
(pp. 79–81, 86 n. 9). Versloot2021's *kabu/kobu* emphasizes variability
(pp. 365–366); 2025's Épinal a versus Erfurt a/o are manuscript
distributions, not a demonstrated phoneme boundary (pp. 109, 125, 133).
**Dates/tree:** fifth-century rounding in Versloot2025 is reconstructed
(p. 135); Waxenberger's Undley 450–500 /ɔ̃:/ interpretation concerns a
long split, not short fronting (pp. 66–67). Early components H-compatible;
the combined package U until length, allophony and merger identities agree.

### 3.3 Nasal-spirant loss

**Outputs:** OE tōþ/gōs/fīf/ūs and Frisian tōth/gōs/fīf/ūs illustrate
loss/lengthening, with a distribution wider than this pair
([@Bremmer2008, pp. 287–289; @Kortlandt2008, pp. 265, 269]).
Repanšek counts extension of older nχ-lengthening to mf/ns/nþ as one
shared innovation, before long phonemicization and the *ē1 split
(pp. 79–80, 85); this is not the same decomposition as every later
rounded-vowel response. Versloot2021 gives 400–600 for loss (pp. 345–346);
2025 does not independently test it (p. 107).
**Diagnostics/tree:** tōth tests survival of rounding after lost
conditioning; fīf/ūs are non-a controls. H-compatible broad early
innovation, not proof of an exclusive AF node or every intervening stage.

### 3.4 Ordinary a-fronting and SC030/SC043

**Outputs:** OE dæġ/þæt and Frisian dei/thet versus unfronted contexts
([@Repansek2012, pp. 78–79; @Versloot2021, p. 343]).
Campbell has independent daughter frontings after different contractions
(pp. 90–91). Bremmer asserts closed-syllable fronting and argues
`fronting < g-palatalization` from -dei (pp. 287, 289–290).
Kortlandt has restricted shared AF6, then WS11/A11 extensions
(pp. 267, 270–271). Repanšek places short fronting after the long split
but before symmetry-driven ai-contraction; independence is possible,
not compelled by “could equally well” (pp. 81–82).
Versloot2025 has conditional fronting before mutation/late breaking and
retains lengthening versus selectively fronted-rC alternatives
(pp. 123, 125–128); late-fifth-century fronting is reconstructed (p. 135).
Waxenberger dates a later **phonemic split**, not initial [æ] allophony
(pp. 69–74).

**Identity stays U:** one fronting affecting ordinary a and au inside
English (Campbell p. 91); shared restricted fronting plus English
extension (Kortlandt pp. 267, 270–271); independent diphthong restructuring
(Repanšek p. 82; Laker's reported scenario 4, pp. 179–180).
Luick directly links ordinary fronting and the first au component,
with a tentative later Frisian assimilation route
([@Luick1914, pp. 130–132, §§118–120]); this is an additional
shared-process candidate, not corroboration of Campbell's reversed order.
SC030/SC043 remain provisional separate identities; no merger follows.
Goblirsch's direct account strengthens the need to separate **shared au
development** from **one English plain-a/au fronting process**: he argues
for the first but does not establish the second
([@Goblirsch1991, pp. 17–19, 21]).

### 3.5 *ai

**Outputs:** OE ā, mutated ǣ; Frisian long front and ā reflexes.
Campbell's reversed-order histories (§2) compete with Kortlandt's
shared ā then Frisian F11 fronting (pp. 270–271). Bremmer's
stēn/ēk/klāth illustrate outcomes, not individually demonstrated
intermediates (pp. 287–288). Repanšek favors late symmetry-driven
English contraction and Proto-Frisian twofold contraction before later
raising (pp. 82–84). Versloot2017 instead has early **velar-context**
Frisian ā, mutation, delabialization, then remaining ai > ē; English
waves are inseparable, yielding ā throughout (pp. 292–293, 302, 305–309).
Versloot2021 affirms early Frisian differentiation including Insular
North Frisian (pp. 346, 348–349); 2025 defers diphthongs (p. 107).
**Diagnostics:** kēi/kāi versus tsetel/tziake distinguish vowel strata at
palatalization, not uniquely ā versus retained-ai intermediate
(Kortlandt pp. 267–268, 274). Shortened ai must be controlled separately
(Campbell p. 99; Versloot2017 pp. 284–285, 302).
**Dates/tree:** Versloot2017 brackets English contraction about 450–600;
Frisian early subset before 600, remaining subset eighth/ninth century
(pp. 295–297, 309). Waxenberger retains two English scenarios and a
conditional skanomodu terminus (pp. 63, 68, 72–74).
H* for Kortlandt's shared event; P* for Campbell's daughter events;
Versloot's velar subset only H-compatible. Frisian late contraction
after Frisian-specific delabialization cannot be one inherited
all-context event also found in English. Overall U.
Goblirsch advocates common ai > ā, but records non-umlaut Frisian ē
that cannot all be explained by i/j. His contact/structural alternatives
do not provide a complete exceptionless conditioner or an independently
demonstrated contraction/mutation order
([@Goblirsch1991, pp. 17–19]).

### 3.6 *au

**Outputs:** OE ēa versus Frisian ā. Laker prints **ċeapian/kapia**
without macrons (pp. 177–178); Kortlandt prints **cēapian/kāpia**
(p. 267). Preserve each author's notation.
Campbell contracts Frisian au before fronting, with OE first-component
fronting alongside short a (pp. 91, 104). Kortlandt keeps au unchanged at
AF7, then English second fronting/breaking/E14 versus Frisian F11 < F12
(pp. 267–268, 271). Repanšek separates English long-diphthong
restructuring from short fronting (pp. 82–84, 86 n. 5).
Laker reports four alternatives: shared-fronted diphthong then Frisian
contraction; reversal/depalatalization; early ā then English
rediphthongization; late independent English fronting. He favors 1 and 4
without choosing, leaving the argument unresolved (pp. 178–180, 184).
Versloot2017 decouples ai/au and places Frisian au-ā before 600
(pp. 307–309); 2021 brackets possible English contraction (pp. 345–346).
Versloot2025 suggests an au > long a > ēa route as potentially
supportive but **deferred and not crucial** (p. 107).
Direct Luick instead offers participation of au's first component in
fronting, English offglide unrounding in the seventh century and tentative
Frisian regressive assimilation; the corrupt Frisian intermediate remains
unusable as an implementation input
([@Luick1914, pp. 130–132, §§119–120]).
Direct Goblirsch supplies a separate shared-ēa/stress-shift alternative,
not a claim of common plain-a fronting or independently evidenced
consonant depalatalization (pp. 17–21; §2 above).
**Diagnostics/tree:** ċeapian/kapia establishes initial velar contrast,
not uniquely the vowel at one shared palatalization moment if subsequent
depalatalization is possible. Intermediate evidence is needed (Laker
pp. 178–180). U; Campbell's earlier-Frisian-contraction explanation
cannot license inherited shared fronting.
**Dates:** Laker reports eighth-century names or possibly sixth-century
skanomodu **if Frisian** (p. 178); Waxenberger's 575–610 coin dates au
only under skaun- and relevant attribution (pp. 63, 68).

### 3.7 Palatalization / phonemicization / assibilation

**Outputs:** English palatal/affricate outcomes versus Frisian ts/j/dz;
final k and dialect distributions differ. Onset, productive cutoff,
phonemicization, assibilation and analogy are separate events
(Laker pp. 166–168, 180–184).
Campbell allows common onset but different completion after vowel changes
(pp. 106–107). Kortlandt separates AF7 and E14, both before mutation
(pp. 267–268, 271, 273). Laker argues productive palatalization precedes
mutation from kyn/kening; later British names may reflect sound
substitution, not continued productivity (pp. 168–172).
He rejects three **reported Stiles** arguments: ċeorl proves breaking
before palatal **diphthongization**, not necessarily velar palatalization
(pp. 174–176); h/χ may have a separate history (pp. 176–177);
assibilation/leveling differences need not mean different original
conditioning (pp. 180–184). Au remains open (pp. 177–180).
**2025:** palatalization is explicitly outside its test; some digraphs
admit back mutation instead (pp. 107, 120–121).
**Dates/tree:** Laker's continental onset is an inference before
mid-fifth-century settlement (pp. 169–173), not direct proof of sharing.
Early event H-compatible; exact inherited identity U; completed daughter
distributions cannot all be inherited from shared onset alone.

### 3.8 Breaking

**Outputs:** English ea/eo/io versus Frisian iu/io, with different inputs
and contexts (Campbell p. 105; Bremmer p. 287; Versloot2021
pp. 342, 348–349). Conventional English breaking before mutation
contrasts with Frisian after mutation; Kortlandt separately numbers
English breaking before E16 and Frisian F14 after F13 (pp. 270–271).
Campbell's siucht argument supports post-mutation breaking and
mutation < syncope < loss of intervocalic h, **not a securely established
breaking-versus-h-loss edge** (p. 105).
Versloot2021 proposes late English breaking after mutation, retaining
Hines's disagreement (pp. 343–345, 364); 2025 directly argues the
alternative with two input mechanisms (pp. 123, 125–128, 131–135).
Modern namme/neame and Faroese tak/taks support phonetic possibility,
not inherited OE–Frisian breaking (2025 pp. 115–116).
**Dates/tree:** seventh-century Frisian and seventh–ninth-century English
in 2021 (p. 345); seventh-century English onset in 2025 (p. 135).
P for a unitary identity across these different laws; removing a
reversed-order obstacle does not make different breakings H.

### 3.9 Restoration / retraction

Campbell reconstructs a > æ > a and long-front retraction before second
raising/mutation; gard retains velar g (pp. 94–98, 104). Kortlandt
restricts initial fronting, while retaining limited long retraction AF5
and **analogical** back restoration AF8 after AF7 (pp. 267, 270, 273).
Repanšek places divergent restoration after the shared boundary
(pp. 78, 86 n. 6). Versloot2025 removes broad front-and-restore from its
short-a history but retains unexplained forms/limited second fronting
(pp. 123, 127–129, 135).
**Diagnostic/tree:** spadan has identical finals under “restore” and
“never front”; hebuc fails both (2025 p. 123). U for broad event identity;
H* only for precisely limited Kortlandt candidates. No calendar date

### 3.11 i-mutation

**Outputs:** fronted/raised vowels, subsequent unrounding, trigger/input/
paradigm differences. Campbell has Frisian second raising < mutation <

(pp. 287, 291). Kortlandt separates daughter E16/F13 (p. 271): P
under that reconstruction. Repanšek places mutation within an ongoing
raising system; bræm(b)el tests quality, not date (p. 88 n. 22).
Versloot2017 places Frisian mutation between ai subsets (pp. 306–309);
2021 puts about 600 at the linkage boundary (pp. 344–346).
2025 has mutation before breaking with graded i versus ī/j, input,
weight and manuscript effects (pp. 119–120, 123–128, 139–141).
**Dates/tree:** Laker reports sixth-century English versus sixth–eighth-
century Frisian (pp. 168–169). Waxenberger distinguishes allophonic
umlaut by about 550 from phonemicization by 610–650 (pp. 66, 71–73).
U overall; typological similarity and statistical fit do not prove
single inherited identity.

## 7. Absolute evidence and unresolved questions

Waxenberger's ladder is inscription → object date → phonetic
interpretation → phonemic status → chronology. Undley about 450–500
supports her long /ɔ̃:/ split; Caistor astragalus about 425–475 preserves a
diphthong; skanomodu about 575–610 has a monophthong conditional on
etymology/provenance; Chessell/Watchfield old rune uses distinguish
allophony from later Caistor brooch phonemic contrasts about 610–650
(pp. 62–73). Her two ai scenarios remain open, with the later merely
preferred (pp. 72–74). Rune correspondence is S, not proof of inherited
sound change.

Versloot2025's manuscript dates also require source-layer control:
Épinal about 700, Erfurt first-quarter ninth century copied from earlier
material; manuscript date is not one homogeneous vowel-stage date
(pp. 108–110). Categorical prediction is ST 84% versus AF 82% in Épinal,
71% versus 72% in Erfurt; probabilistic mutation improves AF to 87%/80%.
Both analyses predict the same outcomes in 92%/91% of tokens
(pp. 124–128). Better fit or fewer laws does not prove intermediate states
or inherited-node identity.

**Exact unknowns:** Campbell's Frisian æi intermediate; all-context ā
versus context-dependent ai paths; one versus extended versus independent
au-fronting; productive palatalization versus later reversal/assibilation/
leveling; English breaking before or after mutation and its input;
skanomodu etymology/provenance; allophonic-to-phonemic timing; direct
arguments in the missing specialist sources (Campbell p. 90;
Kortlandt pp. 267–271; Laker pp. 178–184; Versloot2017 pp. 307–309;
Versloot2025 pp. 107, 126–128, 131–135; Waxenberger pp. 63–74).
For directly read Luick, the missing evidence is specifically the
intermediate glyph/page image and unlocated §637, **not** the held
grammar or the locally verified §§118–120 prose
([@Luick1914, pp. 130–132]).
These unknowns constrain **stem/branch allocation and the state of the
required AF node**; they are not a proposal to delete it. No single
consensus node-state reconstruction or categorical shared conditioning
has been established in this documentation pass.
No newer or simpler account receives automatic priority.

For Goblirsch, the localized gaps are the unobserved Frisian au
intermediate, the completeness of the ai conditioner and independent
verification of the Bennett/Gysseling mechanisms and dating he reports.
Gysseling's eighth-century onomastic dating is **reported evidence**,
not a date independently established here from the named documents.
Neither gap turns a feasible common-diphthong model into proof or makes
the missing Stiles/Fulk papers prerequisites for evaluating it
([@Goblirsch1991, pp. 17–21]).

**Changed by the four direct reviews:** Nielsen supplies a daughter-local
causal chain opposed to the handbook's long-fronting-before-contraction
account; Bremmer supplies a much fuller Frisian inventory but also
opposed ai-order passages and qualified h chronology; Goblirsch supplies
a positively shared-compatible diphthong alternative without proving
plain-a/au identity; Hogg supplies English component diagnostics and an
unresolved P/U contradiction, not a ready consensus palatalization chain.
No author is adopted wholesale, and none of these changes in research
coverage constitutes an executable-science decision
([@Bremmer2009, pp. 27–40; @Nielsen2001, pp. 514–521;
@Goblirsch1991, pp. 17–21; @Hogg1979, pp. 90–111]).
