## 2. Author's own thesis

Versloot argues *sympathetically and explicitly* for an areal, non-cladistic
model. His central claim is that there was **no Anglo-Frisian node** on the
West Germanic tree, but that between roughly **AD 450 and 650** the varieties
that later became English and Frisian (together with coastal Dutch/Flemish
down to Calais and the North-Sea-oriented, especially Eastphalian, varieties
of Old Saxon) formed a single, only minimally differentiated **speech
community / *Sprachbund* / linkage** that shared most of its innovations
(pp. 344–346, 349, 362). His formulation of the tree question is decisive
for CAPR: "Is there an Anglo-Frisian node in the West Germanic family tree?
Not in a strict sense, just as little as there was a West Germanic node …
But there was a period in the history of Germanic, roughly between AD 450
and 650, when varieties that later evolved into Frisian and English shared
most of their innovations" (p. 346). He describes the object as a "linkage
model" combining "shared, areal phenomena as well as simultaneous gradual
differentiation" (p. 346, citing Schulte 2018), and concludes that "the
North Sea region witnessed the formation of the Anglo-Frisian idiom by a
series of shared innovative linguistic changes"; "The question about a
separate branch on the Germanic language tree or a matter of convergence is
actually superfluous under a more subtle interpretation of the developments"
(p. 362). English and Frisian are the "heirs of an 'Anglo-Frisian' sub-group
of West Germanic which encompassed a much wider geographical area than only
England and Friesland" (p. 347). In England the idiom is characterized as
"rather a sociolect or ethnolect" of a militarily/politically dominant,
ethnically self-identifying settler class (p. 363).

A secondary but load-bearing thesis is a **reform of early Old English
phonology** (argued in Versloot forthcoming a/b, summarized here): OE
breaking is *late* (late seventh century onward, deploying into the eighth
and ninth centuries) and fronting of PGmc *\*a* is only *partial*, under
"nearly identical conditions as in Frisian" (pp. 343–344). This "slimmed"
OE chronology deletes Table 12.2's changes 6, 7, 8 and 11 (combinative
breaking */æ/ > /a/*, restoration of */a/*, partial restoration of */a:/*,
and smoothing) and is then said to "fit very well" with the reconstructed
Frisian chronology (p. 344).

**Evidential status Versloot claims for the "North Sea Germanic idiom":**
it is *not* a reconstructed protolanguage and *not* a family-tree node; it
is a **bundle of areally-shared innovations** ("a set of features that
spread at different times and different pace over varying regions around
the North Sea", reported approvingly from Stiles at p. 341) whose overlap
was strong enough (in his revised chronology) to constitute a single speech
community for ~200 years, but whose geographical scope "differed per
feature" (p. 362) and whose members he declines to place under one exclusive
common relative chronology. This is precisely the configuration that, under
CAPR's formalism, **cannot** be entered as a NODE (see §10).

## 3. Positions reported, not held

- **Schwarz 1951** (*Goten, Nordgermanen, Angelsachsen*): the "old-school"
  view that the common Frisian–English traits "had been established already
  on the Continent" before the migrations. Versloot quotes it as the
  paradigm he rejects (p. 340; again p. 348 on the North Frisian
  "autochthonous" corollary).
- **Kuhn 1955**: a "fairly undifferentiated North-West Germanic" prior to
  the Migration Period, with the common language forming *during and after*
  c. 400. Versloot adopts the spirit of this (pp. 340–341, and endorses it
  again at p. 366: "This confirms Kuhn's idea"), but it is Kuhn's position,
  not original to Versloot.
- **Chadwick 1889**: no major Scandinavian/Anglo-Frisian/West Germanic
  distinctions go back much before the fifth century; quoted at length
  (p. 340).
- **Stiles 1995**: the evidence "does not support the notion of an 'original
  Anglo-Frisian unity' or sub-protolanguage … it is not possible to
  construct the exclusive common relative chronology that is necessary in
  order to be able to establish a node on a family tree" (quoted p. 341).
  **This is Stiles's argument as Versloot reports it, not Versloot's own
  demonstration** (CAPR does not hold Stiles 1995; see §13 and
  `../missing-direct-sources.md`).
- **Stiles 2013 / Nielsen 1985–2010**: language-continuum, gradual
  differentiation into North Gmc / North Sea WGmc / Continental WGmc
  (pp. 340, 346).
- **Kortlandt 1999; 2008**: a common basis with "many developments actually
  shared in chronology and time by English and Frisian." Versloot says some
  of his own suggestions reflect Kortlandt's but "differ from it in other
  points" (p. 342). Reported, partly endorsed, not fully adopted.
- **De Vaan 2017** (*The Dawn of Dutch*): many alleged North Sea Germanic
  features of coastal Dutch are better explained as local developments;
  only a residue are genuine NSG relics (pp. 344, "conclusions … sobering
  for the Anglo-Frisian hypothesis"). Versloot relies on De Vaan's residue
  list as his "only access" to coastal-Dutch NSG (p. 344).
- **Campbell 1959 / Hogg 2011 ("the current theory")**: general fronting of
  PGmc *\*a* + near-exceptionless breaking before *\*rC, \*lC, \*x*, patched
  by retraction/smoothing. Versloot reports this as the theory he is
  replacing and criticizes it as "extremely complicated" (p. 343).
- **Higham & Ryan 2015 / current archaeology**: rejection of Bede's
  Angle/Saxon/Jute tripartition as "too late to be a useful primary
  source"; Versloot reports this mainstream view (pp. 358–359) and then
  argues *against* it from runic distribution (p. 359).
- **Peter Schrijver's Celtic-substrate hypothesis**: reported and partly
  resisted in the Discussion — Versloot finds the arguments "a bit
  far-fetched" but concedes "breaking in English could well be
  Celtic-influenced" (p. 364).

## 4. Languages and evidence actually used

- **Old English** — all four dialect groups distinguished and used: West
  Saxon (WS), Mercian (Mc), Kentish (Ke), Northumbrian (Nh); Anglian
  (Mc+Nh) treated as the diagnostically primary group for his reform, WS
  treated as an aberrant western contact zone (pp. 343, 364). Dialect
  detail does real work in Tables 12.2–12.3.
- **Old Frisian** — split into "South Frisian" (all varieties incl. North
  Frisian coast/Halligen) vs **Insular North Frisian** (Sylt, Helgoland,
  Föhr, Amrum); the North Frisian origin question is a whole section
  (pp. 347–349). Modern West Frisian and modern Insular North Frisian
  reflexes (Föhr, Sylt) are cited as witnesses.
- **Old Saxon** — Eastphalian (EF) vs Westphalian (WF); the North-Sea-
  oriented Weser/Elbe-basin (Eastphalian) varieties participate in the
  early changes (pp. 344–346, citing Versloot & Adamczyk 2017).
- **Coastal Dutch/Flemish** — Flanders (F), Zeeland (Z), Holland (H) relic
  words and place-names, via De Vaan 2017 (pp. 344–346).
- **North Germanic / Old Norse** — as an outer comparandum sharing several
  changes (p. 346, citing Haugen 2004).
- **Runic material** — used *two ways*: (a) as **distributional** evidence
  (find-spots of 3rd–8th-c. inscriptions correlated with place-names,
  brooches and Bede's tripartition: Figures 12.1–12.8); (b) as
  **phonological** evidence in a few forms (the Tune stone *arƀijō-*,
  p. 343; the Frisian comb spellings *kabu/kobu*, p. 365; the ending *-u*,
  p. 366).
- **Onomastics** — place-name suffixes *\*haim* and *\*ing* (settler names),
  plus *leben/-lev*, *\*stadi-*, *villa*, *court*; Celtic *dun/don* in
  England (pp. 350–357, Appendix p. 363).
- **Archaeology** — cruciform and "Saxon" brooches, furnished cemeteries
  (pp. 355–359, citing Nicolay, Higham & Ryan).
- **Loanwords** — *not* a primary strand here; contact is argued
  demographically, not through a loanword layer.

## 5. Proposed relative chronology

Versloot presents chronology mainly through two tables. **Table 12.2**
(p. 341) is the *conventional* ordering he is replacing; **Table 12.3**
(p. 345) is *his* combined North-Sea-Germanic ordering with absolute dates
(see §6). Most edges below come from Table 12.3's ordinal sequence and the
prose reform on pp. 343–344. Ordinal position in Table 12.3 is Versloot's
own claimed relative order.

### English (Versloot's reformed chronology)
```
fronting *a (partial) < i-mutation < OE breaking
```
- edge `fronting *a < i-mutation`: (pp. 343–344; Table 12.3 nos. 3, 8) —
  fronting is fifth-century, i-mutation c. 600. Diagnostic: the Frisian-style
  derivation *\*harwian- > \*hærwian > herwan* is *rejected*; instead *\*a*
  is fronted directly by i-mutation, "the same could be assumed for pre-OE"
  (p. 343).
- edge `i-mutation < OE breaking`: (pp. 343–344; Table 12.3 nos. 8, 11) —
  **this is the reform's crux and reverses the textbook order.** Versloot
  dates OE breaking to the late seventh century onward, i.e. *after*
  i-mutation (c. 600). Hines objects in the Discussion that broken
  diphthongs are attested i-mutated, which forces breaking *before*
  i-mutation; Versloot answers that his usable early evidence is
  non-West-Saxon, that in Northumbrian/Mercian "the i-mutation product of a
  so-called broken vowel is a monophthong", and that there is no evidence
  for an early Anglian breaking later undone (pp. 364). **This edge is
  contested and rests on Versloot forthcoming a/b, which CAPR does not
  hold.**
- Table 12.3 also implies, for OE: rounding/fronting (5th c) < monophth.
  *\*ai*, *\*au* (early 6th) < palatalization (6th c) < i-mutation (c. 600)
  < OE breaking (7th–9th c) < raising ǣ (>700). See §6 for the dated series.

### Frisian
```
monophthongization *ai (differentiated ē / ā) < i-mutation < OFris breaking (eht- / w-)
```
- edge `monophth. *ai < i-mutation`: (p. 348 no. 1; Table 12.3 nos. 4, 8) —
  the *\*ai* split into *ē* and *ā* is dated c. 500 (p. 348) / early sixth
  century (Table 12.3), before i-mutation c. 600.
- Frisian fronting is by **i-mutation directly**: PGmc *\*a > e* with no
  breaking stage, e.g. *arƀijō- > erva* "heir" (p. 343). Frisian has no
  OE-type breaking (p. 342).
- edge `i-mutation < OFris breaking`: (Table 12.3 nos. 8, 10; p. 366) —
  OFris *eht*-breaking (*reht > riucht*) and *w*-breaking (*singwan >
  siunga*) are placed in the seventh century, "just after 600" per the
  Nijdam exchange (pp. 365–366); these are *unique to Frisian* in
  conditioning/lexical distribution and are shared by Insular North Frisian
  (pp. 348–349 no. 2, p. 366).
- Generalization of fronted *\*a* in the feminine *ō*-stems (*\*talō- > tele*)
  is a further South-Frisian innovation shared by Insular North Frisian
  (p. 349 no. 3).

### Shared / undifferentiated stem
- The starting point is **"a fairly undifferentiated North-West Germanic at
  the beginning of the fifth century"** (p. 342), reached before any of the
  NSG changes.
- The "strong linkage remains intact up to i-mutation" (p. 344): Versloot
  places rounding, ā-fronting, *\*a*-fronting, *\*ai*/*\*au* monophthong.,
  loss of nasals before spirants, palatalization and i-mutation on the
  *shared* NSG stem (Table 12.3 nos. 1–8), i.e. before the English/Frisian
  divergence in breaking. But he stresses the "shared chronology is not
  categorical": *\*ai* (and possibly *\*au*) monophthongization already
  differentiates English and Frisian **before 600** (p. 346).

Kept strictly apart from §5, with the inferential distance from *dated

Independently dated *objects / attestations* (as opposed to reconstructed
changes):

- **Tune stone (Norway), 3rd–4th c.**: attests the form *arƀijō-* "heir"
  (p. 343). This dates *the inscription and the word-shape*, **not** the
  Frisian i-mutation (*erva*), which is later and inferred; ≥2 inferential
  steps.
- **Earliest Early-OE records** (Cædmon's Hymn, Bede's Death Song, Franks
  Casket, Épinal/Erfurt Glossaries, ~1,100 tokens): late seventh / early
  eighth century (p. 342). These bound the *terminus ante quem* for changes
  visible in them; the mapping from spelling to a specific completed sound
  change is itself several steps (reading → phonetic value → phonemic status
  → identification with a change).
- **Common reconstructable ancestor of all Frisian (incl. Insular North
  Frisian): 650–850** (p. 349). Dated "based on runic inscriptions and names
  in early medieval Latin texts (Gysseling 1962; Versloot 2014a)" and matched
  to archaeological resettlement of the North Frisian Islands. This dates a
  *reconstructed common stage*, inferred from runic + onomastic evidence.
- **Runic finds**: bulk of West Germanic inscriptions fifth–sixth century
  (p. 361); used to date *settlement/literate populations*, not sound
  changes.
- **Charles Martel vs Radbod, 716** (p. 361): a historical event framing the
  Excursus, not a linguistic datum.
- **i-mutation ~600** is the single most load-bearing absolute anchor, but
  it is a *reconstructed* date relative to the earliest records, not an
  object date (p. 344).

## 7. Proposed shared innovations

Developments Versloot treats as genuinely common (shared NSG stem), Table
12.3 nos. 1–8 up to i-mutation, "a largely shared chronology, both relative
and absolute, for English and Frisian" (p. 344):

1. **Rounding of /a/, /ā/ > /ɔ/** — 5th c.; *month/moanne* (p. 345).
2. **Fronting of ā < PGmc ē1** — 5th c.; *sheep/skiep* (p. 345).
3. **Fronting of PGmc /a/** — 5th c.; *tail/teil* (p. 345). NB Versloot's OE
   reform makes this only *partial* in OE (p. 343).
4. **Monophthongization of PGmc /ai/ > ā** — early 6th c.; *toe/tean*
   (p. 345) — but already differentiating before 600 (see §8).
5. **Monophthongization of PGmc /au/ > ā** — early 6th c.; *beam* (p. 345).
6. **Loss of nasals before spirants** — 400–600; *us/ús < \*uns* (p. 345).
7. **Palatalization of initial /g/, /k/ before front vowels** — 6th c.;
   *yield, church / jilde, tsjerke* (p. 345).
8. **i-mutation ("secondary")** — c. 600; *green/grien* (p. 345). Marked off
   from the *restricted* "primary i-mutation" of Continental WGmc (p. 344,
   citing Salmons 2018). This is the **outer limit of the strong linkage**
   (p. 344).
9. **Rising diphthongs < PGmc eu** — early 7th c.; *steer/stjoere* (p. 345);
   OE bracketed (weaker in OE).

Versloot explicitly frames these as *waves*, not a node: "This is very much
in line with the concept of waves rather than a discrete node on the language
tree" (p. 346).

## 8. Proposed independent developments

Superficially "Anglo-Frisian" but treated as separate/divergent:

- **OE breaking (no. 11, 7th–9th c.) vs OFris breaking (no. 10, 7th c.)** —
  distinct changes, not one. Old Frisian "did not have this type of
  breaking"; its cognates keep the PGmc vowel (*ward, werk* vs OE *weard,
  weorc*) (p. 342). OFris *eht*-/*w*-breaking is unique in conditioning and
  lexical distribution across all Frisian (pp. 348–349 no. 2; p. 366). Under
  the tree test these are **parallel/independent** breakings.
- **Monophthongization of *\*ai* (and possibly *\*au*)** — the shared
  chronology breaks "already before 600": English and Frisian differentiate
  the *\*ai* outcome (Frisian into *ē* vs *ā*, e.g. *tāne, stēn*; English
  uniform *ā*) (pp. 346, 348 no. 1, 366). So even this "shared" change
  diverges early.
- **Coastal Zeeland/Flemish** ceases to track Frisian "perhaps already in
  the case of PGmc *\*ai*, but certainly from the seventh century onwards
  with the lack of Frisian breaking" (p. 346; Discussion p. 365, at Table
  12.3 point 9 / early seventh c.).
- **Later changes (nos. 12–14)**: shortening ai > æ (late 7th c.), raising
  ǣ > ē (>700), delabialization (8th–10th c.) show restricted, divergent
  distributions.

**⚠ Features shared with Old Saxon and/or coastal Dutch — evidence AGAINST a
specifically *Anglo-Frisian* node.** Per Table 12.3's distribution columns
(p. 345), the following "AF" changes extend beyond English+Frisian and so
cannot diagnose an exclusive A–F subgroup:

- **Rounding (1)**: OE-AK(S), OF, **FZH (coastal Dutch)**, **EF (Eastphalian
  OS)**, WF.
- ***\*a*-fronting (3)**: OE, OF, **FZH**, **[EF]** — shared with coastal
  Dutch and (restricted) Eastphalian.
- **Loss of nasals before spirants (6)**: OE, OF, **FZH**, **EF, WF**.
- **Palatalization of /g/, /k/ (7)**: OE, OF, **[FZH]**, **EF** — shared with
  coastal Dutch (weakly) and Eastphalian. *A palatalization isogloss is
  therefore not A–F-exclusive on Versloot's own map.*
- **i-mutation (8)**: OE, OF, **FZH** (Old Saxon has it "much more
  restricted", p. 344).
- Versloot states outright: several changes "spread wider than the North Sea
  Germanic area, including sometimes (parts of) North Germanic, sometimes
  also parts of the Franconian regions" (p. 362); nos. 4, 6, 7(?), 8, 9 and
  (in a North Gmc form) 10, 11 "are also found in North Germanic" (p. 346).

The Gallo-Romance parallel (author's footnote 13, p. 364): fronting of a/ā,
palatalization of velars before front vowels, retraction æ > a before w/n,
and palatal-conditioned diphthongization occur in Gallo-Romance AD 400–700
too (Kerkhof 2018) — Versloot raises this to question whether the "North Sea
Germanic developments" are even Germanic-internal in causation. This further
undercuts any of these as A–F-diagnostic isoglosses.

## 10. Tree-test implications

Applying the CAPR criterion in `../README.md`:

- **If Versloot's chronology is correct, which alleged A–F innovations could
  still occupy a shared ancestral node?** In principle the changes he places
  on the *shared stem before the E/F split* (Table 12.3 nos. 1–3, 6, 7, 8 —
  rounding, ā-fronting, *\*a*-fronting, loss of nasals before spirants,
  palatalization, i-mutation) are ordered identically for English and
  Frisian in his account and would be candidates for a shared node. **But
  each of these is, on Versloot's own distribution map, also shared with
  coastal Dutch and/or Eastphalian Old Saxon (and several with North
  Germanic).** A change shared with Old Saxon or coastal Dutch cannot
  diagnose a specifically *Anglo-Frisian* node (§8). So under the CAPR tree
  test these survive at most as **S (surface isogloss)** / broad NSG areal
  features, not as **H (A–F historical isogloss)**: they place, if anywhere,
  on a much larger and vaguer North-Sea-Germanic region, not on an exclusive
  English+Frisian stem.
- **Which correspondences must be independent parallel changes (P) under a
  tree model?** **Breaking**, unambiguously: OE breaking (7th–9th c.) and
  OFris breaking (7th c., *eht-/w-* type) have different conditioning,
  different lexical distribution, and — critically for the test — Frisian
  reaches its breaking without ever passing through the OE breaking stage,
  while OE (in the reformed chronology) breaks *after* i-mutation. The
  identity of "breaking" in the two languages is therefore **false** in the
  sense of the README's clause 4 (similar outcomes, historically distinct
  changes) → **P**. The *\*ai* monophthongization likewise differentiates
  before 600 (Frisian ē/ā split vs OE uniform ā) and is at best **U/P**.
- **Where the author invokes wave/continuum/linkage in place of an ordering
  argument (recorded neutrally — these mark the absence of a tree-usable
  edge, not an error):**
  - p. 341: features "spread at different times and different pace over
    varying regions" (reported from Stiles) — no exclusive relative
    chronology.
  - p. 346: "the concept of waves rather than a discrete node"; adopts the
    "linkage model" (Schulte 2018).
  - p. 362: "Anglo-Frisian *Sprachbund*"; the branch-vs-convergence question
    is "actually superfluous."
  - Discussion pp. 365–367: "The wave model allows for variability … The
    model is flexible"; "'Frisian' is imposed from hindsight."
  Each of these is a place where Versloot **declines to supply the exclusive
  ordered edge** the CAPR node test requires. That is a statement about what
  CAPR's tree formalism can and cannot consume from this source — **not** a
  criticism of Versloot, whose declared model is explicitly areal.

**Net for CAPR:** This source's *tree-usable* output is almost entirely
**negative/constraining**: (i) it supplies a strong daughter-specific edge
`i-mutation < OE breaking` (contested) and `monophth. \*ai < i-mutation`
that, if accepted, force OE and OFris breaking apart → **breaking is P, not
H**; (ii) it supplies areal statements (waves/linkage/Sprachbund) that CAPR's
tree formalism cannot ingest as edges. It provides **no** positive, exclusive
A–F relative-chronology edge that could license a shared A–F node; on the
contrary, it argues no such node exists.

## 11. Uncertainty grading

- **Firmly demonstrated (data compel):**
  - Old Frisian lacks OE-type breaking of *\*a/\*e* before rC/lC/x; the
    cognates keep the vowel (*ward, werk*) (p. 342). This is an attested
    contrast.
  - Insular North Frisian shares the diagnostic South-Frisian innovations
    (*\*ai* differentiation, *eht-/w-*breaking, ō-stem *\*a*-fronting, *-ar*
    plurals, *tiuche*), so its closest relatives are the South-Frisian
    dialects (pp. 348–349). Well evidenced.
  - The distributional correlation of runic finds with *\*haim/\*ing*
    place-names, brooches, and Bede's Anglian/Jutish territories (Figs
    12.1–12.8) is a genuine statistical pattern (69% of runic finds against
    these name-types, p. 351).
- **Author's preferred reconstruction (consistent; alternatives exist and he
  knows it):**
  - The **whole reformed OE chronology** — partial *\*a*-fronting + *late*
    OE breaking placed *after* i-mutation. He himself calls it a "suggested
    … reform" resting on Versloot forthcoming a/b (pp. 342–344); Hines
    presses the standard counter-argument in the Discussion (p. 364).
  - The c. 450–650 "single speech community" reading of Table 12.3
    (pp. 344, 349).
  - The 650–850 date for the common Frisian ancestor (p. 349).
- **Plausible but non-unique inference (evidence compatible, but also with
  rivals):**
  - The identification of the runic-writing population with Migration-period
    settlers, and the resurrection of Bede's tripartition from runic
    distribution (pp. 359, 361) — explicitly a re-interpretation against
    current archaeological consensus.
  - The Redbad "fan Danemercum" echo of a real sixth/seventh-c. Frisian
    Scandinavian link (Excursus II, p. 362) — hedged ("may … not only …
    reflect … but just as well echo").
  - The Amay inscription as "Anglo-Frisian" but "not necessarily Frisian in
    any narrower … sense" (p. 356).
- **Explicitly unresolved (author says it is open):**
  - Why runic use is restricted to newly settled regions / to *\*haim/\*ing*
    names — "not easy to answer" (p. 353).
  - The large onomastic/cemetery mismatches in England (Oxfordshire,
    Warwickshire) — "it is not possible to give an explanation" (p. 357).
  - Inland/central-river-area boundaries — "We have very little data";
    "practically nothing there" (Discussion p. 365).
  - The mechanism of the parallel fifteenth-century Frisian internal change
    vs Groningen language-shift — "I don't have clear answers yet" (p. 368).

**Do not upgrade the hedges:** the reformed OE chronology is offered
programmatically ("I recently suggested", "the alternative scenario,
suggested by myself", pp. 342–343) and much of its support is in unpublished
work. Its "i-mutation < OE breaking" edge must be recorded as *the author's
contested proposal*, not a CAPR fact.

## 12. Implementation-ready extracts (Frisian)

Preparation only — **not** an implementation. Frisian developments where
Versloot gives enough to sketch a rule. Dates are Versloot's reconstructed
absolute dates (Table 12.3, p. 345); ordering constraints from §5.

1. **Monophthongization/differentiation of PGmc *\*ai***
   - input: PGmc *\*ai*
   - output: OFris *ē* and *ā* (conditioned split, two outcomes)
   - conditioning: lexically/phonologically split (Versloot does not fully
     specify the conditioning here; e.g. *\*klaiþ- > clāth*, *\*staina- >
     stēn*, and *tāne/stēn* p. 366)
   - scope: all Frisian incl. Insular North Frisian
   - approx. stage: c. 500 / early sixth century
   - ordering: before i-mutation (c. 600); differentiates from English
     already before 600
   - evidence: p. 348 no. 1; p. 366
   - uncertainty: outcome split firmly attested; exact conditioning
     under-specified in this source
2. **i-mutation (Frisian, incl. direct fronting of *\*a > e*)**
   - input: back/low vowels + following *-i-/-j-*; specifically PGmc *\*a*
   - output: front vowel; *\*a > e* directly (no breaking stage)
   - conditioning: following *i/j* (e.g. earlier *-ij-*)
   - scope: general in Frisian
   - approx. stage: c. 600, "perhaps with a slight delay" vs English (p. 344)
   - ordering: after *\*ai* monophthongization; before OFris breaking
   - evidence: *arƀijō- > erva* (p. 343); *grōni- > grien* (p. 345)
   - uncertainty: the *direct* *\*a > e* path is Versloot's preferred analysis
3. **Generalized fronting of *\*a* in feminine ō-stems**
   - input: PGmc *\*a* in fem. ō-stem paradigms
   - output: fronted vowel (OFris *e*)
   - conditioning: morphological generalization across the ō-stem paradigm
   - scope: South Frisian + Insular North Frisian
   - approx. stage: within the fifth-c. *\*a*-fronting complex, generalized
   - ordering: presupposes *\*a*-fronting
   - evidence: *\*talō- > tele > WFris tel*, INFris (Sylt) *tial* (p. 349 no. 3)
   - uncertainty: firmly attested as Frisian-specific generalization
4. **OFris *eht*-breaking (*\*e* before *\*xt*)**
   - input: PGmc *\*e* before *\*xt*
   - output: rising diphthong *iu* (OFris *riucht*)
   - conditioning: before *\*xt*
   - scope: all Frisian incl. INFris (Föhr *rocht*)
   - approx. stage: seventh century, "just after 600" (p. 366)
   - ordering: after i-mutation; Frisian-internal, no English equivalent
   - evidence: *\*rexta- > riucht* (pp. 348–349 no. 2; p. 366)
   - uncertainty: conditioning/lexical spread called unique across Frisian
5. **OFris *w*-/*u*-breaking**
   - input: vowel before *\*u/\*w* (e.g. *\*nigun*)
   - output: rising diphthong (*niugen*, *siunga*)
   - conditioning: following *\*u/\*w*
   - scope: Frisian incl. INFris (Föhr *njüügen*)
   - approx. stage: seventh century
   - ordering: Frisian-internal
   - evidence: *\*nigun > niugen*; *singwan > siunga* (pp. 348–349 no. 2;
     p. 366)
   - uncertainty: as no. 4
6. **Rounding of *\*a* before nasal (> o) — weak/variable**
   - input: *\*a* + nasal
   - output: *o* (variable)
   - conditioning: following nasal; explicitly a "gradual/reluctant"
     tendency, not categorical
   - scope: Frisian (and OE as a tendency: *long/hang*, *comb/lamb*)
   - approx. stage: within 400–600 nasal complex
   - ordering: n/a (weak)
   - evidence: comb runes *kabu/kobu* (pp. 365–366)
   - uncertainty: Versloot stresses weak phonological status → **do not**
     encode as a categorical rule
7. **Rounding of /a/, /ā/ > /ɔ/** (shared NSG, listed for completeness)
   - input: *\*a, \*ā*; output: *ɔ*; stage: fifth century; scope: OF (+ OE,
     coastal Dutch, Eastphalian) (p. 345). *Shared beyond Frisian — not
     Frisian-diagnostic.*

Morphological items (not sound changes, recorded for the future Frisian
chain): *-ar* nom.pl. of masc. a-/s-stems (p. 349 no. 4); *-u* nom/acc.sg.
of masc. a-stems interpreted as a schwa-like reduction (p. 366, citing
Nedoma 2018).
