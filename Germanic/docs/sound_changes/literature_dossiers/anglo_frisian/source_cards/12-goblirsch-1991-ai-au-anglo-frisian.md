# Goblirsch 1991, “Germanic ai and au in Anglo-Frisian”

## 1. Bibliographic identity

- **Citation:** Kurt Gustav Goblirsch, “Germanic ai and au in Anglo-Frisian,” *Amsterdamer Beiträge zur älteren Germanistik* 33 (1991), 17–23.
- **BibTeX key:** `Goblirsch1991`.
- **Local reference file:** `docs/references/goblirsch_1991_germanic_ai_and_au_in_anglo_frisian.vision.txt`; same-stem ignored PDF.
- **Printed pages / pages actually used:** entire article, pp. 17–23; argument pp. 17–21, bibliography pp. 21–23. Seven PDF sheets, printed folio = sheet + 16. Byline is **Kurt Gustav Goblirsch**; “Minneapolis” is his affiliation/location, not surname (p. 17).
- **Genre / venue character:** short research article advocating an explanation of Anglo-Frisian unity, largely through reinterpretation of published comparisons; not a newly edited inscription or manuscript corpus (pp. 17–23).

Footnote says a version was presented at the 1989 MLA conference, not that any change is dated to 1989 (p. 17).

## 2. Author's own thesis

Goblirsch argues for Anglo-Frisian unity through both `ai > ā` and `au > ēa`: Frisian `ā < au` is a further development of the same diphthong retained in English, rather than evidence that the initial developments differed (pp. 17, 21). The abstract explicitly gives **`ēa > eā > ā`**, calling stress shift the only explanation of the divergence, while saying `ēa` and `ā` “seem” to have been prosodic variants; the conclusion says common `au > ēa` “must be added” to the innovation list (pp. 17, 21). His intervening argument is more hedged: the Frisian mechanism “apparently” operated, Bennett “may well” have identified it, and the English digraph evidence “seems” to show diphthongs (pp. 20–21). **He does not establish a single sound law jointly fronting plain short *a and the first element of *au**: the suggested fronting explanation is conditional, and he finds plausible an alternative diphthong-series account not dependent on short-*a fronting (pp. 18–19).

## 3. Positions reported, not held

- **Sweet versus Luick on *ai:** smoothing versus `ai > æe > æ > æa > ā`; Goblirsch says the mechanism “cannot be ascertained,” not that Luick's intermediate stages are observed (p. 18). Those intermediate æ symbols are **unmarked** in the printed sequence (p. 18).
- **Siebs:** common `ai > ǣ`, `au > ā`; subsequent Frisian front/back outcomes and English `ǣ > ā`, `ā > ēa`. This is an alternative he reports, not Goblirsch's shared `au > ēa` proposal (p. 18). Print has an unmarked æ in “Frisian shifted æ > ē,” but `ǣ > ā` elsewhere in that passage; preserve the inconsistency rather than normalizing it (p. 18).
- **Campbell 1939:** English `ā > ǣ` before `ai > ā`, short `a > æ` after it; Frisian short `a > æ` before `ai > æi > ē`; English short fronting before `au > æa`, Frisian `au > aa > ā` before short fronting (pp. 18–19). These are **Goblirsch's report of Campbell**, not independent direct evidence from Campbell here.
- **Krupatkin:** common `ai > ā`, then umlaut in both languages and Franconian influence in Frisian; prior changes to inherited long ā remove the old back vowel and occupy the front-long space, structurally favouring new ā rather than open ē. *Au may instead belong to the long front–back “timbre gliding” series, without depending on short-a fronting. Goblirsch calls this plausible but insufficient to reconcile OE ēa and OF ā (p. 19). The printed series is **`īu, ēo, ǣa`**, not `īu, ēo, ēa` (p. 19).
- **Hockett, with Antonsen compared:** OE digraphs as monophthongs, with retraction before [w], subsequent unrounding and contraction; Goblirsch considers the resulting structural unity hypothetically, then argues against this reconstruction through stress-shift evidence (pp. 19–20).
- **Contact accounts:** Saxon/Franconian influence might explain non-umlaut Frisian ē < ai, with Karstien/Miedema/Morsbach and Heeroma/Markey cited; this is explicitly conditional, and Saxon itself has ā variants (p. 18). Bennett's Norse–Frisian–Kentish contact explanation is separable from his proposed stress-shift mechanism: Goblirsch retains the mechanism even if the contact explanation is incorrect (pp. 20–21). Gysseling's eighth-century monophthongization and Kuhn's sixth–tenth-century North Sea innovation span are reported claims (p. 21).

## 4. Languages and evidence actually used

- **OE / OF:** Gothic and OHG comparanda establish the different *ai/*au outcomes; OF umlaut and non-umlaut ē matter to whether a common ai > ā can be reconstructed (pp. 17–18). No OF manuscript dialect-by-dialect census is supplied (pp. 17–21).
- **English dialects:** WS versus Old Kentish *au reflexes are the closest comparator for the proposed Frisian mechanism; Northumbrian and early WS rare iú/ió cases show other alleged stress shifts, expressly distinguished from inherited eu (p. 20).
- **Middle English:** front/back reflex variants and southwestern place-name spellings argue for earlier diphthongs and variable stress, not specifically for a recorded Frisian ēa stage (p. 20).
- **Old Norse:** breaking and eu > jú/jó provide a phonetic analogy; their historical relationship to OE breaking “cannot be proven” (p. 20). OF jā < eu provides an internal Frisian analogue, but is not *au evidence (p. 21).
- **Old Saxon / Franconian:** contextual alternatives for OF ē < ai; Low German, East/West Norse, OHG and Gothic supply the wider Germanic contrast (pp. 17–18). Frisian names *Datnesta* and *Franlo* do chronological work through Gysseling's reported analysis; no runic witness is independently analysed here (pp. 20–21).

## 5. Proposed relative chronology

`X < Y` means change X precedes change Y, not that both are directly attested. **OWN** = Goblirsch's reconstruction; **REPORTED** = another author's account; **CONDITIONAL** = depends on the stated phonetic/structural premises.

### English

```text
OWN, preferred exemplar: au > ao > æo > æa [represented later by ēa]
OWN, Kentish: ea formation < stress shift ea > ja < glide weakening/loss
OWN, post-initial-palatal class: rising diphthong < falling diphthong < ME front reflex
REPORTED Campbell: long ā > ǣ < ai > ā < short a > æ < au > æa
```

- In the exemplar, `au > ao` (second-element lowering) precedes `ao > æo` (first-element fronting), which precedes `æo > æa` (further lowering); Goblirsch immediately allows stages in a different order. These are proposed phonetic intermediates, not dated spellings or compulsory independent edges. `æ > e` in later orthography may involve **no phonetic change**, so do not add a phonetic raising edge (p. 18).
- Kentish shift precedes glide loss; longer glide retention after dental stops and **l**, including *djāf*, *-lyās*, supports the proposed intervening glide, whereas *gecās*, *brād* illustrate loss. This is his interpretation of outcomes, not a complete attested derivation (p. 20).
- Initial-palatal examples *gēar*, *scēap* allegedly require rising-to-falling shift before their ME front-vowel outcomes; this is **not the same direction** as falling-to-rising stress shift in the Kentish/Frisian *au account (p. 20).
- Campbell's English chain is reported explicitly: long fronting < ai monophthongization and ai monophthongization < short fronting (p. 18); short fronting < au development (p. 19). It is **not adopted as Goblirsch's own chronology**. The article supplies no lexical feeding/bleeding derivation proving these long/short-fronting edges (pp. 18–19).
- No own edge orders general consonant palatalization, i-mutation, short fronting and inherited *au-fronting against one another. Breaking/velar umlaut are discussed in the debate about digraph interpretation, not used to establish such an edge (pp. 19–20).

### Frisian

```text
OWN: au > ēa < stress shift ēa > eā < monophthongization eā > ā
REPORTED Campbell: au > aa > ā < short a > æ < ai > æi > ē
REPORTED Krupatkin: ai > ā < umlaut of its reflex
```

- `au > ēa < ēa > eā`: the abstract's literal sequence requires a diphthong before stress can move to its second element; the conclusion identifies that predecessor as common Anglo-Frisian. This is **premise-entailed in his reconstruction**, not independently witnessed OF ēa (pp. 17, 21).
- `ēa > eā < eā > ā`: also explicit in the abstract. Body argument compares Kentish stress shift plus glide loss and OF *dāf, lās, kās, brād*; it calls the OF process “apparently” applicable (pp. 17, 20–21). The article does not supply an attested OF *jā < au stage: OF jā spellings on p. 21 descend from **eu**.
- Campbell's `au > aa > ā < short a > æ` is a reported competing mechanism with a different au input to fronting; `short a > æ < ai > æi > ē` is also reported, not shown through a Frisian witness derivation here (pp. 18–19).
- `ai > ā < umlaut` is Krupatkin's reported chain (p. 19). Goblirsch's own descriptions identify ē in i/j environments (*dēla*) and ē < au under umlaut (*dēpa, hēra*), but do **not** uniquely choose contraction-before-mutation over diphthong mutation followed by contraction (pp. 17–18).
- No own ordering places short-a fronting, inherited long-ā fronting, *ai change, *au stress shift/contraction, and i-mutation in one obligatory sequence (pp. 17–21).

### Shared / undifferentiated stem

- **OWN proposed common outcomes:** `ai > ā` and `au > ēa`; no relative edge between these two changes is established (pp. 17–18, 21). Common au development precedes the Frisian secondary shift/contraction **if that ancestry is accepted** (pp. 17, 21).
- **CONDITIONAL structural premise, reported from Krupatkin and called plausible:** nasalized Gmc ā > ō and WGmc ā > ō before nasals / ǣ otherwise < new `ai > ā`; long-ā fronting occupies the front-long space and leaves a back-long vacancy. These are structural reconstruction edges, not demonstrated lexical feeding edges (p. 19).
- **Identity of fronting is unresolved:** p. 18 says `au > ēa` “could” result from Anglo-Frisian **`ā > æ`** plus second-element lowering; the printed æ lacks a macron. Do not silently turn this into either `short a > æ` or `ā > ǣ`, or an established simultaneous plain-a/au law. On p. 19 a short-a-independent diphthong-series account is explicitly entertained. The conclusion claims shared **au > ēa**, not a proven identity of this process with plain-a fronting (pp. 18–19, 21).

## 6. Absolute chronology

- **Mid ninth century:** date of the Vespasian glosses in the Hockett discussion; Hockett's inferred long-vowel monophthongization at that time or soon after depends on his rejected interpretation of the digraphs. Object date → phonetic interpretation → sound-change date; not Goblirsch's accepted date for au contraction (p. 19).
- **Late thirteenth century:** date assigned to written OF records; under the Bennett/Goblirsch reconstruction, glide loss occurred before those records. This is a terminus conditional on the proposed lost-glide analysis, not an attestation of the glide (p. 20).
- **Eighth century:** Goblirsch reports Gysseling dating OF ai and au monophthongization from onomastics (*Datnesta*, *Franlo*). The article does not give the individual documentary dates or establish the intermediate vowels: names → identification as inherited au words → reading of a as a contracted vowel → dating (pp. 20–21).
- **Sixth–tenth centuries:** Kuhn's cited span for the North Sea common development, with innovation centres on both sides of the sea. It dates a package/contact-area account, not separately the au-fronting, stress-shift or plain-a changes (p. 21). No split date or independent absolute date for Goblirsch's common ēa is established (pp. 17–21).

## 7. Proposed shared innovations

- **Central proposals:** `ai > ā` and `au > ēa`, latter masked in OF by secondary stress shift/contraction. Goblirsch explicitly adds the latter to the Anglo-Frisian list (pp. 17, 21). Shared outputs and a feasible phonetic bridge are his arguments; neither establishes a dated pre-split event uniquely (pp. 18–21).
- **Supporting list, not individually demonstrated:** unrounding `ö, ü > e, i (e)`; `eg (æg) > ei (æi)` word-finally/before consonants; lengthening before *mb, nd, ld, rd, rn*. These correspondences are listed without their own chronology or witness derivations (p. 21).
- **Broader North Sea parallels:** pronoun and verbal-plural mergers, pronoun r loss, nasal loss before f/s/þ, short/long fronting and pre-nasal retraction. This is a regional list, not proof that every member is one Anglo-Frisian stem innovation (p. 21).

## 8. Proposed independent developments

- **OF secondary development:** common ēa → eā → ā, absent from the general OE retention account; Old Kentish has a comparable stress-shift/glide-loss outcome, so “English unchanged” must not erase that dialect exception (pp. 17, 20–21).
- Goblirsch does **not** positively decide that OF and Kentish stress shifts were independent: he separates the mechanism from Bennett's possibly incorrect contact history. Their similarity permits parallel mechanisms or contact, without establishing event identity (pp. 20–21).
- Non-umlaut OF ē < ai may reflect Saxon/Franconian influence, unlike OE ā; this is a conditional regional explanation, not an established exceptionless native dental-conditioned sound law (p. 18).
- He does not argue that initial English and Frisian *au developments were independent; that is precisely the alternative his common-ēa account opposes (pp. 17–21).

## 10. Tree-test implications

**CAPR premises for this review:** strict tree, required Anglo-Frisian node; a daughter-only predecessor Y before candidate shared X excludes X from the common stem under those premises, not the node. Compatibility does not prove historical identity.

- Under Goblirsch's **own preferred** `common au > ēa < Frisian stress shift < Frisian contraction`, the shared au development is tree-compatible: its daughter-only follower is not a daughter-only predecessor. Common ai > ā is also not excluded by any independently proved own edge here. Neither permission proves that the changes happened once on the stem (pp. 17–21).
- **Stress premise:** if OF ā really comes from fronted ēa through `ēa > eā > ā`, an earlier fronted diphthong is required in OF. Its existence still does not decide whether OF and OE inherited one innovation or separately produced compatible diphthongs. If stress shift is rejected, the article loses its principal bridge to common au > ēa; it does not thereby demonstrate independence (pp. 17, 20–21).
- **Same-fronting premise:** identifying plain short-a fronting F with au-fronting X is an additional hypothesis, not Goblirsch's established conclusion (pp. 18–19). If one instead accepts his **report of Campbell's Frisian** `au contraction < F`, and independently establishes that contraction as daughter-only, F cannot be on the stem. If F = X is additionally required, common-stem X is excluded too; with corresponding fronting in both sisters it must then be parallel under the fixed tree. Dropping F = X removes that particular obstruction for a separate diphthong process. None of those premise changes abolishes the required node (pp. 18–19).
- **Long-fronting premise:** the structural account orders long-ā fronting before ai > ā. If that predecessor is independently shown daughter-only, common ai > ā is excluded; if long fronting is itself stem-wide, the ordering is compatible with common ai > ā. Goblirsch's cited structural argument supplies neither the daughter-only diagnosis nor proof of shared event identity (p. 19).
- No demonstrated edge from OE consonant palatalization or post-palatal stress shift to initial au-fronting can be extracted here; p. 20's palatal examples cannot manufacture a daughter-only predecessor.
- **Contact/diffusion reported, not a CAPR escape hatch:** p. 17 suggests spreading monophthongization in Low German/East Norse; p. 18 entertains imported OF ē; p. 21 treats Bennett's contact cause as dispensable and reports North Sea innovation centres. These claims supply no substitute tree ordering. Non-umlaut lexical variation is not licensed as lexical diffusion or grammatical conditioning masquerading as a sound law (pp. 17–21).

## 11. Uncertainty grading

- **Firmly demonstrated at this article's level:** the compared final reflex contrasts, the distinction between umlaut and non-umlaut OF ē examples, and the exact sequences Goblirsch prints. The article presents these data rather than re-editing their underlying attestations (pp. 17–21).
- **Author's preferred reconstruction:** inherited common au > ēa followed by OF stress shift/contraction; common ai > ā despite problematic OF ē. His forceful abstract/conclusion exceed the observational reach of the intervening witnesses (pp. 17–21).
- **Plausible but non-unique inference:** common versus separate event identity; lost OF glide inferred from ā; earlier vowel quality/stress inferred from later ME outcomes; structural pressure selecting ā; onomastic spelling as a date of contraction (pp. 18–21).
- **Explicitly unresolved / hedged:** ai mechanism “cannot be ascertained”; distribution of OF ā/ē has no consensus; contact explanation is possible/conditional; fronting/lowering “could” explain au and intermediate order can vary; orthographic æ/e may not be phonetic; rare OE stress shifts are disputed; OE–ON historical relationship is unproven; Bennett's contact claim may be wrong while his mechanism may be right (pp. 18–21).
- **Not supplied:** a directly recorded OF au-derived ēa/eā stage, independently established fronting/stress/mutation partial order, exhaustive conditioning/blocker inventory, or a demonstrated identity of plain-a and au fronting (pp. 17–21).
