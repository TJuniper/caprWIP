# Hogg 1979, “Old English Palatalization”

## 1. Bibliographic identity

- **Citation:** Richard M. Hogg, “Old English Palatalization”, *Transactions of the Philological Society* 77.1 (1979), 89–113. DOI: `10.1111/j.1467-968X.1979.tb00855.x`.
- **BibTeX key:** `Hogg1979`.
- **Local reference file:** `docs/references/hogg_1979_old_english_palatalization.vision.txt`; same-stem ignored scanned PDF, 25 sheets, printed folio = sheet + 88.
- **Printed pages / pages actually read:** **all pp. 89–113**, including notes and bibliography; all 25 retained page images inspected. Load-bearing notation, diagrams and diagnostic forms checked against images, not inferred from OCR agreement.
- **Genre:** argumentative research article exposing unresolved phonological and chronological problems, not a comparative Frisian handbook; Hogg expressly warns against expecting a major resolution (p. 89).
- **Notation:** `ȝ` / `ȝ'` encode Hogg's printed **3-shaped** velar/palatal fricative signs, preserving his declared analytical neutrality; they are not digit 3 or IPA postalveolar `ʒ`. Modern descriptive equivalents **[ɣ] / [ʝ]** are an editorial explanation, not replacements in author notation; `[j]` remains the approximant whose merger is argued, not assumed by transcription (p. 103; p. 111 n. 1). Dotted `ċ`, `ġ` mark palatal consonants in orthographic examples; a prime marks palatal phonetic notation; neither silently means an affricate (p. 111 n. 1; p. 112 n. 2).

## 2. Author's own thesis

Hogg's eventual positive conclusion is that Germanic *g was **most probably** a voiced velar fricative `[ȝ]`, palatalized to `[ȝ']`, which quickly merged with inherited `[j]`; this explicitly excludes geminate *gg and *g after nasals (p. 111). He retains `breaking < palatalization < second fronting`, but the failure of `[ȝ]` to induce breaking and the uncertain chronology of second fronting weaken the usefulness of that bracket (pp. 96–97, 111). Palatal diphthongization is phonetically defensible, not decisively settled by new evidence here (pp. 97–100, 111). **The relation of palatalization to i-umlaut remains “quite unsolved”: his diagnostics demand opposite orders, and he rejects the proposed repairs rather than adopting either order as the solution** (pp. 105–110).

## 3. Positions reported, not held

- **Luick:** Hogg quotes a chronology putting Anglo-Frisian fronting, breaking and restoration before palatalization, with umlaut afterwards. Hogg initially interprets this as phonologization, as opposed to Campbell's phonetic onset; the quotation does **not** certify a directly checked locator in Luick's original (pp. 89–90).
- **Campbell:** continental sensitivity of velars, lasting through fronting/breaking/restoration and ending before second fronting/umlaut; Hogg first shows how reversible allophony could reconcile this with Luick, then prefers Luick's simpler chronology and challenges the shared-origin inference (pp. 90–91, 96). **Different issue:** Hogg prefers Campbell's retention of initial fricative *g over an early initial stop (pp. 92–94).
- **Moulton / Kuhn:** `[g]` and `[ȝ]` as allophones with phoneme `/g/`; Kuhn makes the initial allophone decisive and posits early `/ȝ/ > /g/`. Hogg disputes both the naming criterion and the necessary initial hardening, without denying the allophonic relationship (pp. 93–94).
- **Lass and Anderson:** underlying `/g/ > /g'/ > [ȝ']`; Hogg finds their labial/velar parallelism and separate fricativizations inadequate. **Wagner:** underlying fricative phonemes, but arbitrarily excludes `/ȝ/` from breaking; neither solves the problem (pp. 95–96).
- **Daunt; Stockwell and Barritt; Lass and Anderson:** palatal diphthongization as scribal diacritics rather than a sound change. Hogg rejects the alleged phonetic impossibility, but regards Samuels's combined palatal-plus-umlaut explanation of ‘cheese’ as a plausible alternative (pp. 98–100).
- **Second-fronting dates:** d'Ardenne/Ball before or no later than restoration; Luick/Campbell immediately before umlaut; Girvan/Kuhn after umlaut. Hogg cites his own 1977 preference for the last, without repeating its demonstration (p. 97).
- **Lass and Anderson's late merger:** challenged by early Mercian/Kentish i-spellings (p. 104). **Penzl's phonemic argument:** Hogg invokes it against a proposed late phonemicization of umlaut; this is not Hogg's adoption of the whole early-umlaut solution (p. 110).

## 4. Languages and evidence actually used

OE lexical forms and paradigms do the principal work: West Mercian second fronting; West Saxon palatal diphthongization; Northumbrian differences before æ; Kentish ǣ₂ > ē and early spellings; earliest Mercian glosses; possibly the Mercian Rushworth Gospels and runic consonant distinctions (pp. 96–104). Latin loans supply ‘cheese’, ‘halter’, ‘wine’ and ‘cup’; Middle/Modern English *key* helps diagnose the otherwise ambiguous initial c of cǣġ (pp. 99, 102–103). Modern Dutch and Standard Frisian are **typological** controls against treating a fricative phoneme without a voiced velar-stop phoneme as impossible, not witnesses to shared prehistoric palatalization (p. 94). OS *sitjan > sittjan* and OE *bæddja > bedd* establish inherited *j's geminating/umlauting activity; French *cul* is only a non-unique analogy for rounding inhibiting palatalization (pp. 101, 103). OFris extensive palatalization and Frisian second fronting are discussed comparatively **without a Frisian lexical derivation or full chain** (p. 91; p. 110).

## 5. Proposed relative chronology

### English

Here **P** means the operative palatalization discussed in a derivation, **Pₐ** a possible reversible allophonic onset, **Pφ** phonemic establishment, **M** merger/loss of friction, **U** i-umlaut, **Uφ** its phonemicization. These are analytical labels for separating Hogg's arguments, not his named single universal sequence (pp. 90–91, 103–110).

| Ordered statement | Premise / strength / diagnostic |
|---|---|
| `breaking < P` | Hogg's retained conclusion: *feh* `[fex]` must retain a velar trigger for breaking to *feoh*, whose final `[x]` stays nonpalatal. The initial argument expressly concerns **at least phonologization**, not an independently dated first articulatory tendency; breaking before `[ȝ]` remains unexplained (pp. 91–96, 111). |
| `restoration of a < P` | *calan*, *galan* lack palatalization of an earlier fronted vowel, now restored to a; assumes regular front-vowel sensitivity. Hogg prefers this rather than Campbell's more complex reversible analysis (pp. 90–91, 96). |
| `P < West Mercian second fronting` | Restored a becomes æ in *gætu*, *cæfurtūn* without new palatalization. Retained daughter edge; second fronting's own relation to U is uncertain (pp. 96–97, 111; p. 112 n. 5). |
| `U < second fronting` | Hogg's **“most probably”** preference citing Hogg 1977, not demonstrated anew; not a secure bridge for deriving P/U order (p. 97; p. 100). |
| `P < palatal diphthongization` | Feeding relation **if** the latter is a genuine sound change: palatalized consonants condition following e/æ > ie/ea. WS, plus Northumbrian æ; not Mercian (pp. 96–99). |
| `palatal diphthongization < U` | Conditional ‘cheese’ derivation: *ǣ₁ > ēa > īe*, whereas U first leaves ǣ then gives ēa. The one discriminating loan is insufficient for an unconditional global order; status left open (pp. 99–100, 110). |
| `P < unrounding of ø` | *ċēn* versus *cēne* establishes only this weaker order if front rounded vowels inhibit P. Do **not** upgrade to `P < U` (pp. 100–101). |
| `P < U` | Stronger unrounded ǣ₂ negatives: cǣġ and gǣlan; further short-æ examples. Depends on consonant identification and regular conditioning, with traditional gǣlan classification explicitly admitted as potentially circular (pp. 102–103, 105, 110). |
| `U < P` | Opposite inference from dæġe: if medial `[ȝ] > [ȝ'] > [j]` occurred while U was active, *dæjæ* should give *dejæ*, then *deġe*. Actual dæġe lacks that umlaut. Requires sufficiently early merger/umlauting equivalence and the parallel syllabification of cǣġe/dæġe (pp. 104–108, 110). |
| `P of fricative g < M < final postvocalic vocalization` | Early i-spellings support final loss of friction; extension of M to **all positions** is Hogg's a priori syllable-structure inference, not equally direct positional attestation (pp. 103–104, 111). |
| `P < initial [ȝ] > [g] hardening` | Preferred simplest account avoiding `[ȝ] > [g] > [ȝ] > [ȝ']`; applies to surviving velars, not geminate/postnasal stops (pp. 93–94). |
| `Uφ < Pφ` | Hogg reports Penzl's claim that phonemic umlaut establishes the palatal phonemic contrast, against the fifth attempted solution. Not a resolution of the contradictory **operative** P/U derivations (p. 110). |

**No single merged chain containing both `P < U` and `U < P` is licensed.** Hogg calls their relative chronology contradictory and unresolved; criticism of an order is not proof of the reverse (pp. 105–110).

### Frisian

No derived Frisian palatalization/breaking/restoration/umlaut chain. Hogg states that Frisian and West Mercian second fronting occurred after their unity was severed, but supplies no Frisian form or intermediate stages here (p. 91). The remark that the fifth hypothetical English solution “would not agree with the parallel shift in Frisian” is unelaborated and supplies no independently usable Frisian edge (p. 110).

### Shared / undifferentiated stem

No positively demonstrated pre-split palatalization edge. Campbell's continental/shared-onset placement is **reported and challenged**, not Hogg's conclusion; near-identity of outcomes does not require an onset during unity (pp. 90–91). Inherited Germanic *j's activity is distinguished from newly palatalized g's later merger, but is not a demonstrated common Anglo-Frisian merger event (pp. 103–105).

## 6. Absolute chronology

No calendar dates for the changes, dated inscription inventory or independently dated split are supplied. “Earliest recorded texts” bounds the described OE reflexes; earliest Mercian glosses/early Kentish spellings bound final vocalization by textual period, without numerical dating here (pp. 89, 104). “Continental period” belongs to the reported Campbell argument, not a date established by Hogg (pp. 90–91). October 1977 dates presentation of §5, not palatalization (p. 111 author note).

## 7. Proposed shared innovations

Hogg recognizes almost identical extensive OE/OFris palatalization as the phenomenon Campbell seeks to explain, while explicitly denying that similarity **necessarily** establishes common onset; he does not demonstrate a genuinely shared ancestral innovation (pp. 90–91). His own OE fricative-to-palatal-to-j conclusion is not accompanied by matching Frisian inputs, contexts or chronology (pp. 103–111).

## 8. Proposed independent developments

Second fronting in Frisian and West Mercian is his counterexample to “similar outcome ⇒ unity-period onset”: he explicitly puts it after unity was severed, without providing a comparative derivation (p. 91). He does **not** thereby prove separate origins for every component of palatalization (pp. 90–91). Within OE, second fronting is West Mercian while palatal diphthongization apparently did not occur in Mercian; their treatment order in the article has no chronological implication (p. 96).

### Failed or disputed resolutions

Unordered simultaneity incorrectly favours feeding; its synchronic formalism does not solve the historical mutual counterfeeding (p. 106). Delaying merger, or making `[ȝ']` incapable of umlaut while `[j]` can, conflicts with Hogg's early-merger inference and phonetic expectations; his later southern vowel raising before palatal fricatives is an **analogy**, not proof that both triggers were historically identical during U (pp. 106–107). Splitting initial/medial P is rejected for unity/simplicity and analogical cost; syllable reassignment is rejected through final vocalization and the key/day comparison (pp. 107–108). Morpheme restriction fails under *i* distribution, the assumed single thematic *-oj-* and possible jō-stem reanalysis; early phonetic U with late Uφ fails under Penzl and immediate long-vowel phonemic contrast (pp. 109–110). None authorizes a CAPR lexical, grammatical or diffusion exception as the solution.

## 10. Tree-test implications

**CAPR inference, not Hogg's adjudication:** keep the **required Anglo-Frisian node**. Test component events, not a single label encompassing all onset, phonologization, merger and assibilation (pp. 90–91, 103–111).

- **Conditional failure:** if breaking and/or a-restoration in the relevant derivation is independently established as **English-daughter-only**, Hogg's retained `breaking/restoration < effective P` excludes **that effective event** from the common stem. Matching Frisian effective palatalization would then require a separate daughter event under the strict tree. The article itself supplies the English order but does **not** establish the necessary comparative scope premise; this is conditional, not a proved parallelism verdict (pp. 91–97, 111).
- **Conditional compatibility:** a common ancestral reversible phonetic tendency could still precede daughter-specific breaking/restoration if later daughter effective P and reversal are kept distinct. Hogg discusses this reconciliation but prefers the simpler later chronology; it is not a directly demonstrated common event. Conversely, if the supposed predecessors were themselves shared, this paper alone supplies no daughter-only predecessor blocking common-stem P. Compatibility is **not inheritance proof** (pp. 90–91).
- `P < daughter-only second fronting` does not itself block ancestral P: a daughter-specific **successor** is not the relevant failure configuration. Frisian/West Mercian second fronting itself is placed after severed unity by Hogg and so is parallel on that premise (pp. 91, 96–97).
- The contradictory P/U edges cannot both enter a historical ordered tree; do not select one because the other was criticized, or extend the English merger chronology to Frisian without evidence (pp. 105–110).
- Hogg invokes similar developmental tendencies, not a demonstrated wave/continuum history, to reject necessity of common onset. Record his alternative comparative interpretation accurately; it is **not** a CAPR diffusion/lexical/grammatical escape hatch (p. 91; pp. 107–110).

## 11. Uncertainty grading

- **Firm within the stated derivations:** restored/secondary vowels and the key/day contrast demand genuinely different conditioning histories; Hogg demonstrates the failure of a single simple P/U ordering under his assumptions, not the correct replacement chronology (pp. 96–97, 102–110).
- **Author's preferred reconstruction:** original non-geminate/non-postnasal fricative, simple initial history, quick merger; `breaking/restoration < P < second fronting`; phonetic plausibility of diphthongization (pp. 93–100, 111).
- **Plausible, non-unique:** all-position loss of friction inferred from final vocalization; rounding blocker; ‘cheese’ order; post-umlaut second fronting imported from Hogg 1977; rejection of split P primarily on structural economy (pp. 97, 99–104, 107–108).
- **Explicitly unresolved:** why voiced velar fricative fails to cause breaking; precise second-fronting date; ‘cheese’ status and diphthongization evidential force; P/U order. Comparative common-stem placement lacks Frisian diagnostics rather than being decided negatively (pp. 91, 96–100, 110–111).

## 12. Implementation-ready extracts (Frisian)

**None:** no Frisian rule has a source-local input/output/context/chronology package here (pp. 91, 110). The following **English-only analytical inventory** prevents borrowing an underspecified “palatalization” into Frisian:

| Component | Input → output / condition / blocker / scope |
|---|---|
| Reversible phonetic onset | Velar → palatal allophone in front-vowel environments, potentially reversed when a is restored; Campbell reconciliation, not Hogg's final preferred chronology (p. 90). |
| Operative OE P | *g (fricative) → `[ȝ']`, *k → `[k']`, *h → `[x']`; initially before, finally after, medially between front vowels. This introductory summary requires the later rounding/low-vowel and chronology qualifications; *feoh* supplies the broken-vowel negative (pp. 89, 91, 100–102). |
| Voiced velar distribution | Hogg prefers `[ȝ]` except after nasals/in gemination; initial hardening later than P. Neither *ng nor *gg follows the simple `[ȝ] > [ȝ'] > [j]` account (pp. 92–94, 111). |
| Phonologization | Must be distinguished from articulatory onset; relation to Uφ is discussed through Penzl, not given a complete resolved chain (pp. 90–91, 110). |
| Merger and vocalization | `[ȝ'] > [j]`; final postvocalic vocalization/diphthong formation is syllable-sensitive; general friction loss is inferred rather than separately attested in every position (pp. 103–105). |
| Later palatal effects | Southern palatalized *xs, xt, xþ* and raising of eo/io/e provide a later comparison with umlaut; not the same demonstrated early g event (p. 107). |
| Assibilation / *sk | Assibilation of `[k']`, `[g'g']` and palatalization of *sk expressly outside investigation; `[k']` later `[tʃ]` is mentioned, not dated or fully conditioned. Do not manufacture a Frisian affrication or *ng rule (p. 105; p. 112 n. 2). |
