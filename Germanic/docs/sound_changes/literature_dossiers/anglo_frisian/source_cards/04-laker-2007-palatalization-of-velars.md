## 2. Author's own thesis

Laker argues that "older scholarship was right to place the beginnings of
palatalization in the continental period" (p. 166), i.e. before the
Anglo-Saxon settlement of Britain (mid-5th c.), siding with Siebs, Luick
and Campbell against Hogg, Stiles and Nielsen. His opening sentence frames
the whole debate: "Opinions are divided on whether palatalization of
*-k(k)- and *-g(g)- in Old English and Old Frisian resulted from a shared
development or arose independently" (p. 165).

His two positive moves are: (a) an **absolute-chronology / place-name
argument** (pp. 169–173) that Romano-British place-names were palatalized
on adoption into Old English, which he reads as evidence that a phonemic
palatalized/non-palatalized velar contrast *already existed* before
i-umlaut, so palatalization was pre-Conquest and probably continental
(pp. 171–173); and (b) a **critical demolition** of the four
relative-chronology arguments used to prove independent palatalization:
"three out of four of the main arguments against a shared process of
palatalization carry no weight while a fourth is inconclusive" (p. 166;
restated p. 184).

Crucially, Laker **hedges the whole method**. He writes that "ultimately it
is questionable whether recourse to relative chronology is the ideal
approach for assessing whether palatalization was a shared development or
not, especially since the origins of palatalization are unknown"; the
"genealogical (or Stammbaum) method based on relative chronology cannot
adequately deal with sound-change resulting from dialectal diffusion within
language continua", and he endorses Stiles' arguments "that we are dealing
with a language continuum", so "the genealogical approach is bound to have
its limitations" (p. 166). He therefore does **not** claim to have *proved*
shared palatalization — he claims to have removed three of the four
obstacles to it while leaving the fourth (*au) open, and to have shown the
absolute chronology is compatible with a continental origin. This is a
concessive/probabilistic thesis, not a demonstration.

## 3. Positions reported, not held

This section is long by necessity: §4 of the paper is a point-by-point
critique of others.

- **Siebs, Luick, Campbell — "link" / continental origin (reported
  approvingly, p. 165).** Siebs: the beginnings of palatalization "liegen in
  der englisch-friesischen, das heißt der kontinentalen Periode"; Luick:
  palatalization initiated "noch zur Zeit der anglofriesischen
  Gemeinschaft"; Campbell: palatalization "doubtless began in the
  continental period, for it is a major link of Old English and Old Frisian"
  (p. 165). Laker agrees with the *conclusion* but notes their judgement
  "was based primarily on the inference that the close similarities with
  Frisian bespoke a continental origin" (p. 172) — i.e. he thinks their
  *grounds* were thin and supplies his own place-name argument.
- **Hogg, Stiles, Nielsen — independent palatalization (reported,
  attacked; pp. 165–166, 174).** They argue English and Frisian "do not
  share the same relative chronology of sound changes prior to
  palatalization", concluding the two had separated before palatalization
  initiated (p. 165). They do not question a general OE palatalization but
  are "unspecific about whether it initiated before or after the settlement
  of Britain" (p. 166).
- **Fulk — defends shared chronology (reported, partly endorsed; pp. 166,
  174).** In the forerunner volume Fulk "presented an alternative chronology
  ... which aimed to bring English and Frisian back to a common dialect at
  the time when velars were palatalized" (p. 166). Laker also uses Fulk's
  *reductio*: "if we look at Anglo-Saxon through the same chronological
  spectacles as we do Anglo-Frisian, then we are forced to conclude that
  palatalization was an independent change in Old English too" (p. 174) —
  i.e. the relative-chronology method proves too much. Fulk's quoted
  chronology (p. 174 n. 23) has WGmc *ā* fronted to *ǣ* (kept in West
  Saxon), then raised to *ē* in other OE dialects and OFris, with the
  raising *ǣ > ē* preceding breaking (Kentish *nēor*, Mercian *nēolǣcan*,
  *nēowest*).
- **Kortlandt — revises Fulk but agrees on Anglo-Frisian unity (reported;
  pp. 166, 179).** Kortlandt criticized many points of Fulk's chronology
  while "agreeing with the main thrust of it, namely that palatalizaton of
  velars initiated when Old English and Old Frisian formed a linguistic
  unity" (p. 166); he objected that Fulk's *au proposal "relies on
  pendulum-developments which, though not impossible, are uneconomical"
  (p. 179).
- **Stiles — the four independence arguments (reported, attacked across
  §§4.1–4.4).** Detailed in §7–§9 below. Stiles is Laker's principal target
  throughout §4.
- **Hans Kuhn — later cross-sea contact (reported, rejected; p. 173).**
  The alternative that palatalization emerged in OE only after settlement,
  by cross-sea contact, which Laker finds less likely than continental
  origin.
- **"Palatalization continued after i-umlaut because rounded front vowels
  did not palatalize" (reported, judged "most doubtful"; pp. 169, 171).**
  Laker notes scholars who favour this "have a tendency never to cite living
  languages which attest such a typology", whereas Salmons (this volume) and
  Bhat show many languages palatalize before both rounded and unrounded
  front vowels (p. 171).
- **Cercignani, Liberman — pre-i-umlaut phonemic palatalized/velar contrast
  (reported, adopted by Laker; p. 171 n. 15).**
- **Luick / Goblirsch, Fulk, Page, Krupatkin et al. — four *au scenarios
  (reported; pp. 178–180).** See §9.
- **Watson, Pak, Gevenich — Northumbrian final *k (reported; pp. 180–182).**
  Laker sides with Watson (palatalization general, outcomes differ) against
  Gevenich (northern non-palatalization due to Scandinavian) and against
  Pak's attempted rebuttal of Watson.
- **Campbell — palatalization straddles later changes (reported, used;
  p. 174).** "sensitivity of k and g 'continued through the periods of
  Anglo-Frisian fronting, breaking, and retraction before consonant groups
  and before back vowels'" (p. 174, quoting Campbell 1959 §426).

## 4. Languages and evidence actually used

- **Old English** and **Old Frisian** — the core comparison. Within OE,
  **dialect** matters heavily: palatal diphthongization is limited to West
  Saxon and "North" Northumbrian (p. 175 n. 30); assibilation of final *k
  after *i is absent in Northumbria and parts of Mercia (p. 180); West Saxon
  uniquely assibilates *k both before *and* after front vowels (p. 183).
  Within OFris, North vs East/West Frisian differ on *h palatalization
  (p. 177).
- **Romano-British / Late British place-names in England** — the paper's
  main new evidential contribution (pp. 169–173): *kɛ̄d- 'forest', *kevn-
  'ridge', *gaßr- 'goat', Map 1.
- **Runic material** — used for *dating only*, and always hedged: Franks
  Casket *wylfi* (i-umlaut, late 7th c., p. 168); Folkstone Coin *æniwulufu*
  (OFris i-umlaut, c. 600–650, "usually thought to be Frisian", p. 168);
  *skanomodu* (OFris *au monophthongization, "if it is Frisian", p. 178);
  Ruthwell/Northumbrian runic evidence for palatalization without
  assibilation (p. 180 n. 47).
- **Onomastic/charter material** — Gysseling's OFris names (*Hrēðgǣr;
  Midochi, Fiuilga, Vronlo) for dating i-umlaut and *au monophthongization
  (pp. 168–169, 178).
- **Old Saxon, Old/Middle Dutch** — mentioned as sporadic palatalization
  evidence but explicitly set aside as "less useful" due to Franconicization
  / High German influence (p. 173).

## 5. Proposed relative chronology

Ordering edges below are tagged **[Laker]** (his own commitment),
**[reported]** (an edge he attributes to others and assesses), or
**[rejected]** (an edge he argues is *not* established). CAPR must not
collapse these.

### English
```
palatalization(productive) < i-umlaut          [Laker]
breaking < palatal-diphthongization            [Laker]
palatalization ?? breaking                     [UNDECIDED by Laker]
```
- edge `palatalization(productive) < i-umlaut` — **[Laker]**, p. 168.
  Diagnostic: velars are *not* palatalized before front vowels arising from
  i-umlaut (OE *kyn* 'kin' < *kunni; OFris *kening* 'king' < *kuning* —
  genuine unpalatalized *k*). So the productive change had ceased before
  i-umlaut created new front vowels. Laker adds that apparent post-i-umlaut
  palatalization (British place-names) is **sound-substitution**, not a live
  change (pp. 171–172).
- edge `breaking < palatal-diphthongization` — **[Laker]**, p. 175. This is
  the *only* thing Laker grants Stiles' *ċeorl* data proves: "while Stiles'
  analysis shows convincingly that palatal diphthongization follows
  breaking, it does not prove that palatalization follows breaking either as
  a phonemic change or not" (p. 175). Palatal diphthongization is
  dialect-restricted (WS + N-Northumbrian only), so it is later and
  OE-dialect-specific, "differentiated from an earlier process of velar
  palatalization which is found in all Old English dialects" (p. 184).
- edge `palatalization ?? breaking` — **left open by Laker.** He offers
  chronology (1c) `palatalization < breaking` (input *kerl → Palataliz.
  *ċerl → Breaking *ċeorl → OE *ċeorl*) as a viable alternative to Stiles'
  (1a) `breaking < palatalization` (input *kerl → Breaking *ceorl →
  Palataliz. *ċeorl → OE *ċeorl*) (p. 175). He does **not** decide between
  them; he only denies that breaking *must* precede palatalization.
- **[reported]** `breaking < palatalization(phonemic)` — Stiles, pp.
  174–176; Laker judges it **unproven** (§7).
- **[reported/used]** palatalization *straddles* fronting, breaking and
  retraction — Campbell, p. 174: "continued through the periods of
  Anglo-Frisian fronting, breaking, and retraction before consonant groups
  and before back vowels." Laker uses this to argue palatalization has a
  long life-span and so can overlap other changes, which "complicates any
  analysis" (p. 174). This is co-temporality, not a strict edge.

### Frisian
```
palatalization(productive) < i-umlaut          [Laker]
```
- edge `palatalization(productive) < i-umlaut` — **[Laker]**, pp. 168–169.
  Same diagnostic as English (OFris *kening* < *kuning*). OFris i-umlaut
  dated 6th–8th c.
- **[reported]** OFris *au monophthongization `*au (> *æa/*æu >) ā`** is a
  **Frisian-specific late** development that Laker treats as *not* having
  palatalized preceding *k (hence *kapia*, not †*tsapia); see §9. Its
  ordering relative to palatalization is exactly what §4.3 leaves open.
- **[reported]** A *second*, much later West Frisian palatalization/
  assibilation of *k* (and *t, d*) "re-occurred ... in about the fifteenth
  century" (p. 174) — a warning that palatalization recurs and must not be
  conflated across strata.
- OFris **breaking** is taken to be **independent** of OE breaking (p. 174,
  citing Stiles and, implicitly, Boutkan) — this is a *shared premise* of
  the debate, not Laker's finding.

### Shared / undifferentiated stem
Laker's positive claim is that **productive palatalization of *k, *g began
in the continental (Anglo-Frisian) period, before the mid-5th c.
settlement** (pp. 166, 172, 184), i.e. on the shared stem. But he presents
this as *probable and unrefuted*, resting on (a) the place-name argument and
(b) the failure of the four independence arguments — **not** as a proven
shared node. He explicitly doubts that the Stammbaum/relative-chronology
method can settle it and prefers a language-continuum picture (p. 166).

## 6. Absolute chronology

Kept separate from §5 per template rule. Every date here is separated from
the sound change it bears on by inferential steps; Laker's own hedges are
preserved.

- **i-umlaut, English: no later than 6th c.** Basis: Luick's 6th-c. dating +
  Coates' place-name study, which argues i-umlaut "cannot have been
  operative long after AD 500" (p. 168 and n. 9). Terminus from runic
  *wylfi* (Franks Casket, < Pre-OE *wulßi 'she-wolf', late 7th c.): i-umlaut
  already present in earliest attestations (p. 168). Steps: rune reading →
  phonemic interpretation → identification with i-umlaut.
- **i-umlaut, Frisian: 6th–8th c.** Basis: Folkstone Coin *æniwulufu*
  (< *auni-, c. 600–650, "usually thought to be Frisian"); failing that
  Gysseling's *Hrēðgǣr* (< *Hrōþi-; *Hredgaerus* AD 793, *Hredgerus* AD
  796, 805) in documents ultimately of late 8th c. (pp. 168–169).
- **Palatalization still active until at least the early 7th c. (English).**
  Basis: Romano-British place-names taken in during the 5th–6th c.
  Anglicization show palatalization; areas absorbed after the mid-7th c.
  (Lothian 638; west of Pennines after mid-7th c.) (pp. 169–171, restated
  p. 183). Note the tension Laker flags: Coates puts i-umlaut ≤ AD 500 yet
  velars still palatalized after that — resolved (per Laker) by
  sound-substitution, **not** a live post-500 change (pp. 169, 171–172).
- **Palatalization initiated before the mid-5th c. settlement (continental
  period).** This is an *inference*, not a dated attestation (pp. 166, 172,
  184). Luick's estimate "end of the fourth or the beginning of the fifth
  century" is *reported*, with Laker noting Luick "knows no proof" that
  palatal diphthongization immediately followed (p. 175 n. 29).
- **OFris *au monophthongization `*au > ā`: 8th c. (Gysseling, place-names)
  or possibly ~6th c. (skanomodu runic, *if* Frisian)** (p. 178). Heavily
  hedged. Bears on §4.3.
- **Later West Frisian re-palatalization of *k/t/d*: ~15th c.** (p. 174).

## 7. Proposed shared innovations

Developments Laker regards (or argues are defensible) as genuinely common
to English and Frisian:

- **Palatalization of *k and *g before front vowels (not those from
  i-umlaut) and *j** — the core candidate, argued to be pre-Conquest /
  continental (pp. 165–166, 172, 184). Presented as *probable and
  un-refuted*, not proven.
- **Paradigmatic levelling of palatalized ~ non-palatalized doublets** —
  Laker adopts Luick's account (p. 182) and states the Frisian data
  "essentially prove" it, "in turn ... confirmed by the English" (p. 184).
  OFris doublets: *wigge ~ widze* 'horse' (< Gmc *wegja-), *rīke ~ rīze*
  'rich(es)' (< *rīkja-), *wreke ~ wretse* 'revenge' (< *wrakja-), *breke ~
  bretse* 'breach' (< *brukja-), *egge ~ edze* 'edge' (< *egjō-) (p. 183).
  This is offered as a *shared mechanism* explaining variation in both
  languages, i.e. an argument *against* Stiles' "different conditioning
  factors" claim.
- **Similar conditioning of palatalization overall** — "the conditioning
  factors for palatalization in Old Frisian and Old English are very
  similar", the differences being *later, dialect-specific outcomes*
  (assibilation), not the palatalization itself (p. 183).

Note the level: these are Laker's *arguments that sharing is defensible*. He
does not upgrade them to demonstrated shared nodes (§2).

## 8. Proposed independent developments

Developments Laker (or those he reports) treat as parallel / language- or
dialect-specific:

- **Palatal diphthongization** — later, OE-dialect-specific (WS + N-
  Northumbrian), postdates breaking; must be "differentiated from an earlier
  process of velar palatalization which is found in all Old English
  dialects" (pp. 175, 184). **[Laker]**
- **Palatalization of the voiceless fricative *h/χ*** — Laker argues it is
  plausibly *independent* of *k, *g palatalization and possibly separated by
  centuries, exactly as it is in modern Frisian dialects (some North Frisian
  have [ç], East/West Frisian do not): "there seems to be no conclusive
  evidence that in pre-Old English the palatalization of *χ and the velars
  *k and *g were parallel developments, instead of independent innovations,
  occurring perhaps several centuries apart" (p. 177). **[Laker]**
- **Assibilation** (as distinct from palatalization) — later, with dialect
  variation in both languages; West Saxon uniquely assibilates *k after
  front vowels too; Northumbrian palatalizes but does not assibilate final
  *k after *i (pp. 168, 180–183). **[Laker, following Watson/Luick]**
- **OFris *au monophthongization `*au > ā`** — Frisian-specific and late; on
  Stiles' reading it is a change *unique to Frisian* that fed *kapia*
  without palatalization (pp. 177–178). **[reported; contested]**
- **Late OE *au fronting `*au > ǣa`** (scenario 4) — a later Anglo-Saxon
  change independent of short-*a fronting (Krupatkin et al.), which would
  make OE palatalization *before ēa* an OE-specific late event while leaving
  the older shared palatalization intact (p. 180). **[reported]**

### The *ċeapian ~ kapia* diagnostic (handle with care)

This is the CAPR-critical lexeme. **Laker prints it *ċeapian* (with the
palatal dot, i.e. palatalized) ~ OFris *kapia* (plain *k*, unpalatalized),
both without macrons** (p. 178). What makes it a diagnostic is that the
**reflex of the *initial* velar makes the following-vowel stage
independently visible without reconstructing the vowel directly**: in OE the
initial *k is palatalized (*ċeapian*), which is only possible if a *front*
vowel followed it at palatalization time; in OFris the initial *k is *not*
palatalized (*kapia*), implying a *back* vowel there at palatalization time
(pp. 177–178).

Stiles (quoted p. 178) draws the *independence* conclusion: the OE/OFris
contrast "demonstrates that palatalization took place in each language after
changes which are unique to it." Laker does **not** accept this as
demonstrated. His counter is that the contrast is entangled with the
development of Gmc *au and its relationship to the fronting of short Gmc *a:
"It is more economical if the development of Gmc *au to OE æo, and later æa,
can be linked with a contemporaneous fronting of Gmc *a > OE æ which also
caused palatalization of preceding velars; otherwise two separate changes
must be posited. By doing so, however, it seems that the fronting of Gmc *a
must also be seen as yet another coincidence common to Old English and Old
Frisian, which is likewise uneconomical" (p. 178).

**Chronological proposition it establishes, and uniqueness:** the
*ċeapian/kapia* contrast establishes *only* that, **at palatalization time,
OE had a front vowel and OFris a back vowel after the initial velar**. It
does **not** uniquely fix whether (i) palatalization postdates
Frisian-specific *au changes (Stiles' independent reading), or (ii)
palatalization is shared and OFris *later* backed/monophthongized *æu/*æa to
ā, depalatalizing the velar (Fulk's shared reading), or (iii) OE *au
fronting is a late OE-specific change decoupled from short-*a fronting
(Krupatkin's reading, leaving older shared palatalization intact). All three
are compatible with the same *ċeapian/kapia* data. Hence the diagnostic is
**compatibly, not uniquely, decisive** — which is exactly why §4.3 is left
open (p. 180).

### The four *au scenarios (all reported; Laker declines to choose)

1. **Luick & Goblirsch** (p. 178): Gmc *a fronted *contemporaneously inside
   the diphthong *au in Anglo-Frisian → *æa, later monophthongized to *ā in
   Pre-OFris (Goblirsch: via *Akzentumsprung*, with Frisian velar
   palatalizations then lost). Needs only two stages: *au > *æa > ā.
   **Short-*a fronting and *au-fronting are ONE process here.**
2. **Fulk** (pp. 178–179): Gmc *a fronted in *au (and *ai); all three
   induced Anglo-Frisian palatalization; but *æu reverted to *au in
   Pre-OFris (depalatalizing the velar) before ā arose:
   Anglo-Fris *kau- > *cæu- → (1) OE *tʃæa-*, (2) > PreOFris *kau- > OFris
   *kā-*. Kortlandt: "pendulum-developments ... uneconomical" (three stages
   *au > *æu > *au > ā vs. Luick/Goblirsch's two).
3. **Page** (p. 179): Gmc *au > *ā in earliest OE too (not only OFris),
   later raised/diphthongized before the earliest OE texts (the resulting
   *ā "somehow distinct from *ā < Gmc *ai"); the OE diphthongization
   compared to post-medieval WFris ā > ModWFris *ea*.
4. **Late *au fronting** (Koch, Bülbring, Fourquet, Krupatkin, Hofmann,
   Kortlandt; pp. 179–180): Gmc *au not fronted in OE until late; the late
   *au > *ǣa "was not dependent on fronting of short *a at all" (Krupatkin,
   p. 180, who slots *ǣa into a general OE system of front-back long
   diphthongs *īu, *ēo, *ǣa; cf. Pilch's /æɑ/, p. 179 n. 45).

Laker's verdict: "the most likely of which seem to be (1) and (4), which are
perhaps not mutually exclusive if (4) preceded (1)" (p. 180). He then makes
the load-bearing hedge: **"Undoubtedly, the development of the *au is the
core problem for the interpretation of the Anglo-Frisian subgrouping, and
its interpretation remains very much a subjective one"** (p. 180); restated
in the conclusion: this argument "remains open to different interpretations,
and thus leaves some room open for future debate" (p. 184).

**Direct bearing on the open CAPR question** (whether *au-fronting and
ordinary short-*a fronting are one process): Laker does not decide it.
Scenario (1) makes them one process (favouring a shared node); scenario (4)
makes them separate (favouring a late OE-specific *au fronting). Laker holds
both "most likely" and does not rank them. CAPR must therefore record this
as **explicitly unresolved by Laker**, and must not read *ċeapian/kapia* as
settling it either way. (Note the printed vowel notation differs across
scenarios: the general statement and scenario 1 use *æa* / *ā*; Krupatkin in
scenario 4 uses *ǣa*. The p. 179 verified reading is "the development *au >
ǣa".)

## 10. Tree-test implications  *(CAPR analysis — not Laker)*

Applying the criterion in `../README.md`. This subsection is CAPR's own
reasoning about how Laker's material feeds the ordered-tree test; it is not
attributed to Laker.

- **If Laker's chronology is correct, what can occupy a shared node?** Laker
  removes three of the four *daughter-specific edges* that would force
  palatalization to be parallel (P). If his §4.1, §4.2 and §4.4 verdicts
  hold, then breaking, palatal diphthongization, *h-palatalization and the
  conditioning/assibilation differences are *not* secure B-specific edges
  ordered *before* palatalization — so on the CAPR test **palatalization of
  *k/*g could sit on the shared Anglo-Frisian stem (class H)** rather than
  being forced to P. But note Laker only shows the alleged edges are *not
  established*; he does not supply a positive shared-stem ordering edge.
  Under CAPR's rules this yields at best **class U (uncertain)**, not H:
  removing an obstacle to H is not the same as demonstrating H.

- **What would still have to be independent (P) under a tree model?**
  1. **Palatal diphthongization** — Laker himself makes it later and
     WS/N-Northumbrian-specific (pp. 175, 184); it is a *daughter-internal*
     OE change and cannot be shared.
  2. **Assibilation** (as opposed to palatalization) — dialect-variable in
     both languages, WS-specific post-vocalic *k assibilation, Northumbrian
     non-assibilation; P by Laker's own description (pp. 180–183).
  3. **Voiceless-fricative palatalization** — Laker argues it may be
     independent in OE and OFris "occurring perhaps several centuries apart"
     (p. 177); under the tree test it is *not* a shared innovation.
  4. **The *au reflex divergence itself** (OE ēa vs OFris ā) — regardless of
     scenario, the *later* OE/OFris outcomes diverge; only the *palatalizing
     stage* is a shared-node candidate.

- **The §4.3 *au question is the decisive CAPR gate.** The tree test asks
  whether a B-specific change (here: OFris *au > ā, or a late OE *au > ǣa)
  must precede palatalization. Laker leaves this ordering **undetermined**
  (p. 180). Therefore, on Laker's evidence alone, palatalization before
  *ēa/*au cannot be classed H *or* P — it is **class U**, and the
  classification hinges on resolving the very question CAPR has flagged (is
  *au-fronting = short-*a fronting?).

- **Where Laker invokes wave/continuum instead of an ordering edge (recorded
  neutrally).** Laker explicitly prefers a **language-continuum / dialectal-
  diffusion** model over the Stammbaum method (p. 166), endorsing Stiles'
  continuum arguments, and frames palatalization as a shared **areal
  feature** spread along the North Sea coast (p. 173). Per `../README.md`,
  "wave", "continuum", "linkage" and "diffusion" are **not admissible escape
  hatches** in CAPR's own analysis. This is not a defect in Laker — it marks
  the places where his source supplies **no tree-usable ordering edge**. The
  practical consequence: the parts of Laker's positive case that rest on
  areal/continuum reasoning (pp. 166, 173) contribute *no* H/P/U edge to the
  CAPR synthesis; only his *negative* results (the failed edges of
  §§4.1–4.4) and the absolute place-name dating are tree-usable, and they
  are usable only to *demote* alleged B-specific edges, not to *build* a
  shared node.

## 11. Uncertainty grading

- **Firmly demonstrated (Laker's strongest claims):**
  - Palatal diphthongization is later than breaking and dialect-restricted
    (WS + N-Northumbrian) (pp. 175, 184).
  - Northumbrian shows palatalization of final *k after *i without
    assibilation; assibilation outcomes are dialect-variable (Watson data,
    Lindisfarne *bǣc*; pp. 180–183).
  - Productive palatalization did not apply to front vowels from i-umlaut
    (*kyn*, *kening*; p. 168).

- **Author's preferred reconstruction (consistent, alternatives known):**
  - Palatalization began in the continental period / pre-Conquest (pp. 166,
    172, 184) — Laker's thesis, argued as *probable*, not proved.
  - British place-names were palatalized by **sound-substitution** against a
    pre-existing phonemic contrast, not by a live post-i-umlaut change
    (pp. 171–172).
  - *h-palatalization is independent of *k/*g palatalization (p. 177).
  - Luick's paradigmatic-levelling account of the doublets (p. 182), which
    Laker says is "essentially proved" by Frisian data (p. 184).

- **Plausible but non-unique inference:**
  - Stiles' *ċeapian/kapia* contrast (p. 178) — real datum, multiple
    compatible chronologies.
  - The absolute dates from runes (*wylfi, *æniwulufu, *skanomodu*) — each
    hedged ("if it is Frisian", "usually thought to be Frisian").
  - That Laker's rebuttals of §§4.1/4.2/4.4 *establish* a shared node — they
    remove obstacles but do not positively demonstrate sharing.

- **Explicitly unresolved (Laker says so — DO NOT upgrade):**
  - **The *au argument (§4.3):** "its interpretation remains very much a
    subjective one" (p. 180); "remains open to different interpretations,
    and thus leaves some room open for future debate" (p. 184). Whether
    *au-fronting and short-*a fronting are one process is **not decided**.
  - The *origin* of palatalization: "Unfortunately nobody knows" (p. 174);
    "the origins of palatalization are unknown" (p. 166).
  - Whether the whole relative-chronology method can settle the
    shared-vs-independent question at all (p. 166) — Laker doubts it.

## 12. Implementation-ready extracts (Frisian)  *(PREPARATION ONLY — do not implement)*

For a future Frisian cascade. Laker specifies conditioning fairly well but
ordering only partially; uncertainty is flagged per item. **No foma, no
corpus rows are to be written from this card.** Forms are Laker's printed
spellings.

1. **OFris palatalization/assibilation of *k**
   - input: *k
   - output: palatalized → assibilated *ts* (OFris orthography usually marks
     it, unlike OE); via *k > *cʲ > *tʲ > OFris ts (p. 168).
   - conditioning: (initial) before front vowels *not* from i-umlaut, and
     before *j; (medial) *-k(k)- before *i, *j; (final) *-k after preceding
     front vowel — **NB in OFris final *-k after front vowel is *not*
     regularly palatalized, unlike OE** (p. 166, §2, and Fig. 1: OFris *dik*
     vs OE *diċ*).
   - scope: general OFris (all attested dialects).
   - stage: after short-*a fronting was in place; before / up to i-umlaut.
   - ordering: `palatalization(productive) < i-umlaut` (p. 168). Relation to
     OFris breaking and *au monophthongization **unresolved** (see item 4;
     §4.3).
   - evidence: *tsin* 'chin', *tser(e)ke* 'church', *bretse* 'breach',
     *thritza* 'press (3sg)' (pp. 167, 180).
   - uncertainty: conditioning firm; internal ordering vs *au/breaking open.

2. **OFris palatalization of *g**
   - input: *g (two phonetic values: fricative *ɣ vs plosive [g] after *n
     and in geminate *gg).
   - output: fricative *ɣ → *j* (merges with palatal approximant); plosive
     [g] (in *ng, *gg) → assibilated *dz* (p. 167).
   - conditioning: (initial) before front vowels (not i-umlaut) and *j;
     (medial) *-g- between all front vowels, *-gg-/*-ng before *i, *j;
     (final) *-g after preceding front vowel (OFris *dei* 'day') (p. 166,
     Fig. 1).
   - evidence: OFris *ield* 'tax', *hei* 'mind', *widse* 'horse', *dei*
     'day' (p. 167); cf. OE *ġield, hyġe, wiċġ, dæġ*.
   - uncertainty: twofold *g outcome depends on the two-phonetic-values
     assumption (Laker reports this is held by "many scholars", p. 167) —
     flag as assumption, not datum.

3. **OFris i-umlaut** (context change, blocks palatalization)
   - input/output: back/low vowels → fronted (incl. rounded front) under
     following *i/*j.
   - ordering: **must postdate** productive velar palatalization (velars
     before i-umlaut front vowels are unpalatalized: *kening* < *kuning*),
     p. 168.
   - absolute: 6th–8th c. (Folkstone Coin *æniwulufu*, Gysseling *Hrēðgǣr*),
     pp. 168–169.
   - uncertainty: dating hedged; ordering vs palatalization firm.

4. **OFris *au monophthongization** `*au (> *æa/*æu >) ā`
   - input: Gmc *au; output: OFris *ā* (later ModWFris *ea*).
   - conditioning: unconditioned monophthongization (per the reported
     scenarios).
   - ordering: **UNRESOLVED relative to palatalization** — this is the whole
     of §4.3. Do **not** hard-code an order. Under Luick/Goblirsch it is
     shared-fronting-then-Frisian-monophthongization (*au > *æa > ā); under
     Fulk it involves a *æu > *au reversal + depalatalization (*au > *æu >
     *au > ā); under Krupatkin the OE side is a late independent *au > *ǣa
     (pp. 178–180).
   - absolute: 8th c. (Gysseling) or ~6th c. (skanomodu, if Frisian),
     p. 178.
   - evidence: OFris *kapia* (no palatalization) vs OE *ċeapian*, p. 178.
   - uncertainty: **explicitly unresolved**; carries the CAPR *au/short-*a
     open question. Flag prominently.

5. **OFris paradigmatic levelling of palatalized ~ non-palatalized doublets**
   - mechanism: after loss of *j, the (de)palatalized consonant is
     generalized across the paradigm from singular (front vowel) or plural
     (back vowel) forms (Luick, p. 182).
   - output: doublets *wigge~widze*, *rīke~rīze*, *wreke~wretse*,
     *breke~bretse*, *egge~edze* (p. 183).
   - scope: sporadic in OFris; parallel to English.
   - uncertainty: a *variation-generating* process, not a regular rule —
     implement (later) as optionality/lexical split, not a clean ordered
     rewrite.

6. **OFris voiceless-fricative *h/χ*** (treat as SEPARATE from *k/*g)
   - Laker: OFris *h palatalization is ambiguous/absent in most dialects;
     [ç] only in some North Frisian (Moring, Helgoland), not East/West
     Frisian (Saterland, Wangerooge) (p. 177). Do **not** bundle *h with the
     velar-palatalization rule. Cf. OFris *siuht(h)* < *sehiþ, *riucht* <
     *reht-, *nēi/nēy* < Gmc *nēhwō (pp. 176–177).
