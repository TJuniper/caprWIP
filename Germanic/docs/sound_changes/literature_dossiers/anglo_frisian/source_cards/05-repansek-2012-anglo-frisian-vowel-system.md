# Repanšek 2012, "Remarks on the Development of the 'Anglo-Frisian' Vowel System"

## 1. Bibliographic identity

- **Citation:** Repanšek, Luka. "Remarks on the Development of the
  'Anglo-Frisian' Vowel System." *NOWELE* 64/65 (2012), 77–90.
  doi:10.1075/nowele.64-65.04rep
- **BibTeX key:** Repansek2012
- **Local reference file:** `docs/references/repansek_2012_anglo_frisian_vowel_system.txt`
- **Printed pages:** 77–90 (14 printed pages; text extracted from a
  born-digital PDF via `pdftotext -layout` plus deterministic encoding repair
  documented in the reference header; no OCR).
- **Pages actually used for this card:** 77–90 (all of them, body and
  notes).
- **Genre / venue character:** Specialist research article in a Germanic /
  Frisian-studies journal. It is a *structuralist / systemic*
  reconstruction — the argumentation is driven throughout by long- and
  short-vowel-system symmetry and push-chain reasoning, not by an
  accumulation of lexical isoglosses. This affects evidential weight: much
  of the ordering it proposes is **systemically inferred** ("the system
  would otherwise have a gap / a merger") rather than lexically
  demonstrated, and the empirical scaffolding (rune-row history,
  Frisian monophthongization data, the diphthong reshaping) is largely
  taken over from Stiles 1995, Hofmann 1964/1995 and Krupatkin 1970, none
  of which CAPR holds (see §13).

## 2. Author's own thesis

Repanšek argues that the striking similarity of the Old English and Old
Frisian vowel systems is "indirect/secondary rather than … the result of a
period of common development" (p. 77), and he explicitly aligns himself
with the "discernible trend towards minimizing the number of similarities
of indisputably common provenance" (p. 77). His scare-quoted title is
programmatic: he does **not** accept an "Anglo-Frisian" mother dialect or a
shared Anglo-Frisian node. His central positive thesis is that the whole
set of traditionally adduced OE/OFris agreements can be reduced by
reconstruction to **"no more than two"** genuinely common innovations
(p. 78, p. 85): (1) a rule-extension of the Pan-Germanic
nasal-plus-voiceless-fricative lengthening to further homorganic clusters
(*mf, *ns, *nþ alongside *nχ), and (2) the allophonic split of *ē₁ into a
pre-nasal and a non-nasal allophone (p. 79). These two innovations belong
to a broad "North-Sea Germanic" or "Ingvaeonic" areal base that also
yielded Old Saxon and Old Low Franconian (p. 79), i.e. they are not even
uniquely English-Frisian. He then argues that these two "sufficiently
cataclysmic" changes set off *independent but parallel* system-internal,
symmetry-driven cascades in Primitive English and Proto-Frisian, "which
renders any contact between Frisian and English after this point
unnecessary" (p. 85). Crucially he insists on a labelling distinction:
Primitive English "was formed through convergence" whereas (Old) Frisian
"is a divergent linguistic system" (n. 16, p. 87).

Thesis statement quoted: "Early areal features that marked the ancestral
dialectal base of both linguistic systems must be efficiently reduced to no
more than two innovations. These two peculiarities, however, were
sufficiently cataclysmic to set off a wave of structurally motivated sound
changes directed by the principle of phonological symmetry." (p. 85)

## 3. Positions reported, not held

- **Stiles 1995 — chronological elimination criterion.** Repanšek reports
  (p. 77) Stiles 1995:181 as holding that "no phonological development
  transgressing the line that terminates the exactly parallel chronological
  ordering of the respective sound changes can be said to be concomitant,"
  which he paraphrases: "the chronological criterion eliminates change Y
  from the list of parallel developments if in language A change X is older
  than Y; the reverse is true of language B" (p. 77). *This is Repanšek's
  report of Stiles, not Stiles's own text* — CAPR does not hold Stiles 1995
  (§13).
- **Stiles 1995 — fuþork-row history.** Repanšek reports (p. 81) Stiles
  1995:185–86 for the renaming of the runes: *ansuz* rune renamed
  *ask(az) → *æsk(az)* (the *æsc* rune), and a new *a*-rune *ak-* (later
  *āc*) created. Repanšek adopts this as consistent with his phonology, but
  the reconstruction is Stiles's.
- **Stiles 1995 — *ai monophthongized before fronting of *a.** Repanšek
  reports (p. 82) Stiles 1995:199 as proposing that *ai was monophthongized
  *before* the fronting of *a. Repanšek **rejects** this: "This, however,
  seems the less likely scenario" (p. 82), preferring a later,
  symmetry-driven monophthongization. So this Stiles view is reported and
  argued against, not held.
- **de Vaan 2011.** Reported (n. 17, p. 87) as arguing that the Frisian
  twofold outcome of *ai monophthongization (/ǣ/ ~ /ā/) is "strictly
  predictable," one reflex regular and the other conditioned. Repanšek
  raises difficulties with this and does not adopt it.
- **Krupatkin 1970.** Cited approvingly for the incorporation of *au into
  the diphthong series (p. 82, p. 86 n. 5), the secondary nature of /æ/
  (n. 14, p. 87), the systemic "need for a fifth element" (n. 12, p. 86),
  and the systemic motivation of WS /iē/ (n. 20, p. 88). These are borrowed
  supporting analyses.
- **Hofmann 1964, 1995.** Reported/used for the "wider wave of
  monophthongizations" of *ai and *au shared with OS and OLF (p. 83), and
  for Old Insular North Frisian evidence (p. 84, n. 11).
- **Hogg 1992.** Used for the fronting-blocking environments (n. 6, p. 86),
  the low-front fixing of *ē₁ (n. 10, p. 86), and the *bræm(b)el* i-umlaut
  dating (n. 22, p. 88).
- **The anti-"Anglo-Frisian-node" consensus.** He lists Hofmann (1964,
  1995), Markey (1976), Nielsen (1981, 2001), Stiles (1995), Fulk (1998)
  and Kortlandt (2008) as authors who have "convincingly … contested" the
  Anglo-Frisian mother-dialect idea (p. 77, n. 1, p. 85).

## 4. Languages and evidence actually used

- **Old English** and **Old Frisian** are the two principal systems; the
  argument is genuinely bilateral, but note that OE and OFris dialects are
  distinguished where it matters.
- **OE dialects doing work:** West Saxon vs Anglian (esp. Northumbrian) vs
  Kentish. Fronting-environment differences (n. 6, p. 86: WS *eald*, ONth.
  *ald*, OFr. *ald*; WS *slēan* vs ONth./OFr. *slā* < *slahan*); Anglian
  close-mid /ē/ < *ē₁ (p. 84); "late Kentish raising of /ǣ/ > /ē/" (p. 85).
- **OFris dialects doing work:** Old Insular North Frisian vs West and East
  Frisian for dating the /ǣ/ > /ē/ chain and the monophthongization
  (p. 84, notes 11, 19, 23, 24). Also Weser East Frisian, Ems East Frisian,
  Riustring Frisian, Saterland (notes 19, 23, 24).
- **Runic material:** the "Anglo-Frisian" *ōs* rune and the fuþork-row
  reorganization (p. 81). One runic form is adduced: RunFr. ⟨cōbu⟩ 'a comb'
  (extract renders it "C)bu"), [kɒmbu] < *kambaz.
- **Old Saxon / Old Low Franconian:** used comparatively for the
  monophthongization wave and for Ingvaeonic membership (pp. 79, 83).
- **North Germanic / Old Icelandic:** used to argue NGmc's "marginal
  position" relative to Ingvaeonic (*gás*, *fífl*, *tǫnn* vs OE *tōþ*, OFr.
  *tōth*, p. 79).
- **Typological / comparative parallels** (not Germanic): Latin/Italic
  nasal-plus-fricative lengthening (n. 8, p. 86) and a Slovene (South-West
  Kras) dialect twofold-reflex parallel (n. 18, p. 87, and n. 21, p. 88)
  are used to make the "two competing waves in a transition area" argument
  typologically respectable. These are analogues, not Germanic evidence.

## 5. Proposed relative chronology

Each edge is tagged **[lexically demonstrated]**, **[systemically inferred]**
or **[asserted]** per the task. "Systemically inferred" = the ordering is
argued from vowel-system symmetry / gap-avoidance, not from a diagnostic
lexeme; these are weaker as tree-edges.

### Shared / undifferentiated (Ingvaeonic) stem

```
nasal+velar-fric lengthening (inherited)
   < rule-extension to N + any homorganic voiceless fric / *s   [shared innovation #1]
   < phonemicization of long nasalized *ā < *aNC
   < merger of *ā with the pre-nasal allophone *ē₁N
   < allophonic split of *ē₁  [shared innovation #2]  (≈ simultaneous with *ā entering the long system)
   < denasalization → low back /ɒ̄/ ; *ē₁(−nasal) fixed low-front → /ǣ/
   ≈ allophonic split of short *a (but [ɒ] not phonemicized)
   < fronting of short *a > *æ  (uppermost boundary for any shared change)
```

- edge `rule-extension` occurs **while the original *nχ development was
  still operative**: (p. 79) "every *a, *i and *u in front of the new
  clusters (i.e. *mf, *ns, *nþ) was included in the process." **[systemically
  inferred]** (distributional/structural, not a single diagnostic lexeme).
- edge `phonemicization of *ā < *aNC < merger with *ē₁N < *ē₁ split`:
  (p. 80) "after the long nasalized *ā … was phonemicized due to loss of
  the conditioning environment, it merged with the allophone of *ē₁ in the
  position before a nasal, causing *ē₁ to undergo a phonological split."
  Called by him "the most natural chronological ordering" (p. 80).
  **[systemically inferred].**
- edge `*ē₁ split ≈ entrance of *ā into the long system`: (p. 80) "the
  allophonic split of *ē₁ must have been almost simultaneous with the
  entrance of *ā into the long vowel system," argued "on the evidence of
  such treatment elsewhere" (WGmc/NGmc *ē₁ > /ā/; Gothic *ē₁ = *ē₂).
  **[systemically inferred].**
- edge `*ē₁ split → *ē₁(−nasal) > /ǣ/`: (p. 80) the emergence of the new
  open back vowel "forced /ē₁/ before [−nasal] to restrict its phonological
  territory and fixed it firmly in the low front position … in order to
  provide a symmetrically structured long vowel system." **[systemically
  inferred].**
- edge `short *a split ≈ *ē₁ split`, and `*ē₁/long-system development <
  fronting of short *a`: (p. 81) the short-*a split "came about at roughly
  the same time" as the *ē₁ split, and fronting of *a "must in turn be seen
  as the result of imitation, since through this secondarily achieved
  split, the short vowel system came to reflect fully the developments that
  had taken place in its long counterpart." **[systemically inferred]** (he
  labels the fronting's secondary/imitative nature explicitly, n. 14).
- **Anchoring lexical edge:** `*ē₁ > *ǣ < monophthongization of *ai`:
  (p. 78, crucial point 1) *ē₁ > *ǣ "occurred before the monophthongization
  product /ā/ arose (e.g. OE *stān*)"; Frisian support: "the ǣ-product of
  monophthongization of *ai must have occurred after PGmc. *ē₁ > *ǣ,
  because the former /ǣ/ fails to cause palatalization (e.g. *gēr* 'spear',
  *kēi* 'key')." **[lexically demonstrated]** — this is the one edge with a
  genuine lexical diagnostic (failure of palatalization).
- **Terminus edge:** `fronting of *a > *æ` is "the upper most boundary
  beyond which there is no possibility of shared developments" (p. 78,
  crucial point 3); he adds it "may be lowered and set immediately after
  *ē₁ > *ǣ" if differences in the extent of fronting count (p. 78). The
  divergence *after* fronting is **[lexically demonstrated]** via the
  dialect-split fronting environments (n. 6, p. 86: *eald*/*ald*,
  *slēan*/*slā*).

### English (Primitive English, after the split)

```
fronting of *a  <  monophthongization *ai > /ā/  <  push-chain raising /ɒ̄/ > /ō/  <  i-umlaut
                   (also: reshaping of *au > /æu/ > /ǣɑ/)
                   (Anglian /ē/ < *ē₁ and late Kentish /ǣ/ > /ē/ are post-divergence, separate)
```

- edge `fronting of *a < *ai monophthongization`: **[systemically inferred]**
  — Repanšek prefers a *late*, symmetry-driven monophthongization ("the
  clash between the already restored symmetry … and lack of symmetry in the
  low central vowel … provided the impetus," p. 82) and **rejects** Stiles's
  reverse ordering (*ai monophthongization before fronting), which he
  reports as Stiles 1995:199 and marks "the less likely scenario" (p. 82).
  Stiles's ordering is therefore [asserted, by Stiles] and not adopted.
- edge `*au reshaping is NOT the same event as short-*a fronting`: (p. 82)
  "we would also expect the *a element in *ai to have become fronted, which
  obviously did not happen" — the non-fronting of *ai's *a is the
  diagnostic. **[lexically demonstrated]** (from *ai behaviour); cf. n. 5,
  p. 86: OE *au > /ǣɑ/ "offers no convincing vindication" for connecting
  them.
- edge `push-chain raising /ɒ̄/ > /ō/ < i-umlaut`: (n. 22, p. 88) at
  i-umlaut time PrimEng /ɒ̄/ "was caught half-way to /ō/," shown by the
  æ-spelling in *bræm(b)el* (/ǣ/ < i-umlauted /ɔ/, not /ø̄/ < i-umlauted
  /ō/). **[lexically demonstrated]** (spelling diagnostic).

### Frisian (Proto-Frisian, after the split)

```
fronting of *a  <  monophthongization of *ai (twofold /ǣ/~/ā/) and *au (single /ā/)   [Proto-Frisian]
                <  push-chain raising /ɒ/ > /ō/ and /ǣ/ > /ē/   [post-Proto-Frisian]
                <  i-umlaut of *au (→ œ̄ > /ē/) and later lengthenings
```

- edge `*ē₁ > *ǣ < *ai monophthongization` (Frisian): as in the shared
  block, **[lexically demonstrated]** (*gēr*, *kēi* fail to palatalize,
  p. 78).
- edge `monophthongization is Proto-Frisian`: **[lexically demonstrated]** —
  "the lexical distribution of the /ā/ and /ǣ/ reflexes (< *ai) in Insular
  North Frisian … matches the state of affairs in West and East Frisian and
  secures the Proto-Frisian date for monophthongization" (p. 84).
- edge `monophthongization < push-chain /ǣ/ > /ē/`: **[lexically
  demonstrated / dialectal]** — the /ǣ/ > /ē/ coalescence "is exclusively
  Frisian and it appears to postdate the Proto-Frisian period on the
  evidence of Old Insular North Frisian, which coalesced the old and the
  new /ǣ/ … as opposed to West and East Frisian" (p. 84).
- edge `push-chain < i-umlaut of *au`: **[systemically inferred]** with a
  lexical anchor — the i-umlaut of *au yields a rounded open-mid front
  vowel before unrounding to /ē/: *baugijan > *bǣγ'an > OFr. *bēia* (= OE
  *bīeġan*), parallel nonWS *bēcnan* < *bauknijan (n. 20, p. 88).
- **Twofold *ai outcome (/ǣ/ ~ /ā/) is not regularly conditioned**: "alternates
  without any so far convincingly established regularity" (p. 83), diagnostic
  forms *stēn*, *ēre*, *rāp*, *tāne*. He explains it by two intersecting
  monophthongization waves in a transition area (p. 83) — **[asserted /
  wave-model]**, not an ordering edge.

### Shared / undifferentiated stem (summary)

Only the two "incontestable" innovations (rule-extension of nasal-fricative
lengthening; *ē₁ allophonic split) plus the systemically forced
consequences down to and *including the split of short *a* are placed
before any English/Frisian split. Fronting of *a itself is the last thing
that *could* be shared — and Repanšek expressly says it may not be (see
§7–§8).

## 6. Absolute chronology

**Repanšek gives essentially no calendar dates or termini.** The paper is
purely relative-chronological and areal. The one place he touches absolute
history he does so to *deny* a date can be extracted:

- **Anglo-Saxon migration ("the exodus").** (p. 81) The dialects that
  became Primitive English "must have left the continent after this point …
  at the latest," but brightening and fronting "could equally well have
  taken place independently (after or even before the exodus), seeing that
  they were systemically conditioned. Brightening therefore cannot be a
  reliable *terminus a quo* for Anglo-Saxon migration." So: the migration is
  the only external event mentioned, and he explicitly refuses to tie a
  sound change to it. Inferential distance from any dated object to a sound
  change is, in his own framing, unbridgeable here.
- **Runic material** (RunFr. ⟨cōbu⟩, the *ōs*/*æsc*/*āc* rune history,
  p. 81) is used for *relative* sequence (a new phoneme had entered the
  inventory), **not** with any archaeological date. No inscription is dated.
- Old Insular North Frisian, Middle English dialects "South/North of the
  Humber," Weser/Ems East Frisian etc. (notes 19, 23, 24) are invoked as
  *later relative* stages, again with no absolute dates.

## 7. Proposed shared innovations

Genuinely common to English and Frisian, per Repanšek — and note that even
these he assigns to a broader Ingvaeonic base, not to an exclusive
Anglo-Frisian node:

1. **Rule-extension of nasal-plus-voiceless-fricative lengthening** (p. 79):
   the Pan-Germanic rule V > V̄ / _ N + voiceless velar fricative extended so
   that "any homorganic voiceless fricative or *s followed the nasal"
   (*mf, *ns, *nþ added to *nχ). Reason: every *a, *i, *u before the new
   clusters was caught, so the extension happened while the original rule
   was live (p. 79). He labels this territory "North-Sea Germanic or
   Ingvaeonic" and warns against reading it geographically (p. 79); it also
   yielded OS and OLF.
2. **Allophonic split of *ē₁** into *[ē₁] and *[ē₁N] before [±nasal]
   (p. 79). Reason: the merger of denasalized *ā < *aNC with the pre-nasal
   *ē₁ allophone forced the split (p. 80). He calls this the one innovation
   "which was more or less indirectly responsible for *ē₁ (not before a
   nasal) to develop a front timbre, i.e. /ǣ/" (p. 80).

Everything downstream he treats as *system-internal parallel reaction*, not
as further shared innovation (p. 85: contact after the split is
"unnecessary").

## 8. Proposed independent developments

Repanšek regards the following superficially "Anglo-Frisian" agreements as
**parallel / independent**, driven separately by symmetry pressure in each
system:

- **Fronting of short *a / Anglo-Frisian brightening.** (p. 81) Systemically
  conditioned imitation of the long-vowel split; "could equally well have
  taken place independently (after or even before the exodus)." He accepts
  the term "brightening" only "if understood in the correct context (i.e.
  the phonemic stabilization of PGmc. *ē₁ in the low front position)"
  (n. 15, p. 87). Its conditioning already differs across OFr, WS, Anglian
  (n. 6, p. 86), which he takes as post-fronting divergence.
- **Monophthongization of *ai / *au.** English monophthongized *ai to
  restore short/long symmetry (p. 82); Frisian was "caught up in a wider
  wave of monophthongizations of *ai and *au" shared with OS and OLF, whose
  products *do not match* Frisian's (p. 83). Different motivations,
  different outcomes ⇒ independent.
- **Reshaping of *au** (> PrimOE /æu/ > /ǣɑ/; Frisian into the diphthong
  series then monophthongized): explicitly *not* to be connected with
  short-*a fronting (p. 82, n. 5 p. 86).
- **Raising of /ǣ/ > /ē/.** "Exclusively Frisian" and post-Proto-Frisian
  (p. 84); the Anglian close-mid /ē/ < *ē₁ and late Kentish /ǣ/ > /ē/ are
  "the result of a solidarity chain," i.e. separate again (p. 85). "It is
  fully impossible to connect close-mid front Anglian /ē/ < *ē₁ with the
  developments that took place in Frisian" (p. 84).
- **Breaking, restoration, retraction, i-mutation** — all placed *after*
  fronting and shown to "display divergent behaviour, even within Old
  English itself" (p. 78; n. 6, p. 86); hence not shared.

## 10. Tree-test implications  *(CAPR analysis — not the author's)*

Applying the criterion in `../README.md` to Repanšek's own chronology:

**Which alleged Anglo-Frisian innovations can still occupy a shared
ancestral node?** Under his reconstruction, essentially only the two he
accepts:
- the **rule-extension of nasal-plus-fricative lengthening**, and
- the **allophonic split of *ē₁**.

But note the CAPR consequence: Repanšek himself assigns *both* to a broader
**Ingvaeonic** stem that also feeds Old Saxon and Old Low Franconian
(p. 79). So even these two are **not** an *exclusive* English–Frisian
node — they are `S`/`H` at the *Ingvaeonic* level, not evidence for an
Anglo-Frisian subgroup. They pass the tree test only for a node higher than
"Anglo-Frisian."

**Which apparent correspondences must be independent (parallel) under a
tree model?** On his chronology, all of these are forced to `P`:
- **Fronting of short *a / brightening.** He states it is systemically
  conditioned and "could equally well have taken place independently"
  (p. 81). Under the tree test this is at best `U` and, given the
  dialect-divergent conditioning he documents (n. 6, p. 86), leans `P`.
  There is no lexical isogloss compelling a single shared event.
- **Monophthongization of *ai/*au.** English's *ai monophthongization is
  motivated by short/long symmetry (p. 82); Frisian's is a wider wave with
  OS/OLF and yields *different* products (p. 83). Distinct triggers +
  distinct outputs ⇒ `P`.
- **Reshaping of *au** (OE /ǣɑ/ vs Frisian series) ⇒ `P` (p. 82, n. 5).
- **Raising /ǣ/ > /ē/**: "exclusively Frisian" (p. 84); Anglian/Kentish
  raisings are separate "solidarity chains" (p. 85) ⇒ `P`.
- **Breaking, restoration, retraction, i-mutation**: all post-fronting and
  "divergent … even within Old English" (p. 78, n. 6) ⇒ `P`.

Notably, Repanšek's own structure *supplies* the decisive tree-test
configuration for brightening on the Frisian side: the Frisian
non-palatalization diagnostic (*gēr*, *kēi*, p. 78) shows a Frisian-specific
ordering (short-*a fronting isolated from the diphthongal *a; *ai
monophthongized late), which is exactly the kind of daughter-specific edge
that, if a comparable edge is absent in English, blocks a single shared
fronting event.

**Where he invokes wave / continuum / linkage instead of an ordering
argument** (these supply *no* tree-usable edge; recorded neutrally):
- Ingvaeonic as an areal isogloss, "dangerous to apply … in its strictly
  geographical sense" (p. 79).
- "a wider wave of monophthongizations" (Frisian + OS + OLF) (p. 83).
- "two competing monophthongization waves" intersecting Frisian territory
  (p. 83; typological parallel in notes 18, 21).
- Anglian developments as "the result of a solidarity chain" (p. 85).
- "Supra-dialectal areal features … can be concurrent with early
  diversification" (p. 84).
- The convergence/divergence contrast: Primitive English "formed through
  convergence," Frisian a "divergent linguistic system" (n. 16, p. 87).

Net CAPR reading: Repanšek is a *strong* source for the position that
almost nothing traditionally called "Anglo-Frisian" survives onto an
exclusive shared node, but the survival he does grant is systemic/Ingvaeonic
and the eliminations are mostly **systemically inferred**, so they are
comparatively weak as positive tree-edges and should be weighted below
lexically demonstrated edges from other cards.

## 11. Uncertainty grading

- **Firmly demonstrated (lexical diagnostic compels the conclusion):**
  - *ē₁ > *ǣ precedes the /ā/ (< *ai) product, from OFr. non-palatalizing
    /ǣ/ < *ai (*gēr*, *kēi*) and OE *stān* (p. 78).
  - Short-*a fronting did not affect the *a of biphonemic *ai (same
    diagnostic) (p. 78).
  - Frisian monophthongization is Proto-Frisian in date, from the lexical
    distribution of /ā/~/ǣ/ in Insular North Frisian (p. 84).
- **Author's preferred reconstruction (consistent; he knows alternatives
  exist):**
  - The whole systemic ordering of the two shared innovations
    (rule-extension < *ā phonemicization < *ē₁ split ≈ *ā entry; short-*a
    split ≈ *ē₁ split) — "the most natural chronological ordering" (p. 80),
    largely symmetry-driven.
  - Reduction of all agreements to "no more than two" innovations (pp. 78,
    85).
  - Late, symmetry-driven monophthongization of *ai (against Stiles) — he
    marks his own preference with "seems the less likely scenario" applied
    to the rival (p. 82).
- **Plausible but non-unique inference:**
  - The two-intersecting-waves account of the Frisian /ǣ/~/ā/ split (p. 83)
    — directly contested by de Vaan 2011's "predictable" reading (n. 17).
  - The quality of the denasalized back vowel (/ɒ̄/ vs /ɑ̄/): "it is possible
    that the vowel was /ɑ̄/ rather than /ɒ̄/" (n. 9, p. 86) — hedged.
  - The rounded value of the pre-nasal *a: "If rounding was indeed
    involved, which is very likely" (n. 9) — hedged.
- **Explicitly unresolved / left open (do not upgrade):**
  - Whether brightening/fronting happened before or after migration:
    "could equally well have taken place independently (after or even
    before the exodus)" (p. 81); brightening "cannot be a reliable *terminus
    a quo*."
  - The exact locus of the Ingvaeonic "innovatory centre" — declared
    "irrelevant to the following discussion" (p. 79).
  - Regularity of the Frisian *ai twofold reflex — "without any so far
    convincingly established regularity" (p. 83).

## 12. Implementation-ready extracts (Frisian)  *(PREPARATION ONLY — not implemented)*

> These are staged for a future pre-Old-Frisian cascade. They are Repanšek's
> reconstruction only; ordering is largely systemic and must be reconciled
> with other cards before any FST work.

**F1 — Ingvaeonic nasal + voiceless-fricative lengthening (rule-extension)**
- input: short *a, *i, *u
- output: long /ō/, /ī/, /ū/ (nasalized, later denasalized)
- conditioning: _ N + voiceless homorganic fricative or *s (*nχ, *mf, *ns,
  *nþ)
- scope: shared Ingvaeonic (OE, OFr, OS, OLF); NGmc only in reduced form
- stage: earliest; on the shared stem
- ordering: extension while original *nχ rule still operative; < *ā
  phonemicization
- evidence: OFr./OE *gōs* < *gans-; tōth vs OIcel. tǫnn (pp. 78–79)
- uncertainty: firm for the change; the areal delimitation is systemic

**F2 — Phonemicization of *ā < *aNC and *ē₁ allophonic split**
- input: nasalized *ā (< *aNC); *ē₁
- output: denasalized low back /ɒ̄/ (raisable → /ɔ̄/ → /ō/); *ē₁ splits, the
  non-nasal allophone fixed low-front → /ǣ/
- conditioning: *ē₁ split by [±nasal]; merger of denasalized *ā with *ē₁N
- scope: shared stem
- stage: on the shared stem, ≈ simultaneous with *ā entering the long system
- ordering: *ā phonemicization < merger < *ē₁ split < /ǣ/ fixing
- evidence: *mōna* < *mē₁nō; systemic symmetry argument (pp. 79–80)
- uncertainty: systemically inferred ordering; back-vowel quality hedged
  (n. 9)

**F3 — Fronting of short *a (Frisian side)**
- input: short *a
- output: /æ/ (~/e/); [ɒ] retained before nasal / velar / back-vowel context
- conditioning: fails in *_rC, before covered *l and *χ, and if a back vowel
  follows in the next syllable (OFr, n. 6, p. 86)
- scope: Frisian (parallel to, not necessarily shared with, English)
- stage: last possibly-shared change; Repanšek treats it as independent
- ordering: after F2; **isolated from** the *a in biphonemic *ai (does not
  feed *ai)
- evidence: OFr *dei* vs *land*; non-palatalizing /ǣ/ < *ai (*gēr*, *kēi*)
- uncertainty: shared-vs-parallel explicitly open (p. 81)

**F4 — Monophthongization of *ai and *au (Proto-Frisian)**
- input: *ai, *au
- output: *ai → /ǣ/ ~ /ā/ (irregular); *au → /ā/ (single)
- conditioning: none securely established for the *ai twofold reflex; wave
  overlap invoked
- scope: Proto-Frisian, part of a wider wave with OS/OLF (different products)
- stage: after F3; Proto-Frisian (secured by INF lexical distribution)
- ordering: after *ē₁ > *ǣ; before the /ǣ/ > /ē/ push-chain
- evidence: *stēn*, *ēre*, *rāp*, *tāne* (p. 83); INF distribution (p. 84)
- uncertainty: regularity disputed (de Vaan 2011, n. 17)

**F5 — Symmetry push-chain: /ɒ/ > /ō/, /ǣ/ > /ē/ (post-Proto-Frisian)**
- input: /ɒ̄/, /ǣ/
- output: /ō/ (coalesces with old *ō), /ē/ (coalesces with old *ē₂) via /ɔ/,
  /ɛ̄/
- conditioning: system-internal (push chain)
- scope: /ǣ/ > /ē/ exclusively Frisian; post-dates Proto-Frisian (INF
  keeps old+new /ǣ/ distinct)
- stage: after F4; before/around i-umlaut of *au
- ordering: onset simultaneous with entry of the trigger
- evidence: Old Insular North Frisian vs W/E Frisian (p. 84, n. 24)
- uncertainty: dating rests on the INF dialect argument (systemic +
  dialectal)

**F6 — i-umlaut (incl. *au)**
- input: *au (and back vowels generally)
- output: rounded open-mid front vowel œ̄ → later /ē/ (unrounding)
- conditioning: following *i/*j
- scope: Frisian (parallel to nonWS OE)
- stage: after the raising chain had begun (/ɒ̄/ "half-way to /ō/")
- ordering: after F5 onset
- evidence: *baugijan > *bǣγ'an > bēia (= OE bīeġan); *bræm(b)el* dating
  (notes 20, 22)
- uncertainty: systemic, with lexical anchors
