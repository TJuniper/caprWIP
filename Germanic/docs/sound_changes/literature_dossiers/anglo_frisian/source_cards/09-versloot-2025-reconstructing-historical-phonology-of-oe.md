# Versloot 2025, “Reconstructing the historical phonology of Old English”

Research only. Comparative conclusions belong to
[`../anglo-frisian-chronology.synthesis.md`](../anglo-frisian-chronology.synthesis.md).
Neither a better spelling prediction nor a shorter analysis demonstrates an
inherited Anglo-Frisian innovation. No executable change is authorized here.

## 2. Author's own thesis

Versloot proposes conditional early fronting and rounding, followed by
i-mutation, then relatively late breaking, instead of nearly general
fronting followed by breaking and subsequent reversals (pp. 103, 106–107,
134–135). His “Anglo-Frisian chronology” is a reconstruction tested against
early English spelling, informed by Frisian and North Germanic comparisons,
not a independently established common ancestor (pp. 106–107, 121–123).
He argues that late breaking allows retraction, combinative breaking and
Anglian Smoothing to be dispensed with **for this short-*a* analysis**,
and favors his analysis partly through Occam's Razor (pp. 134–135).
He preserves two alternatives for the input to breaking: lengthened
previously unfronted *a, or selectively fronted *æ (pp. 126–128, 134–135).
Neither alternative is resolved by the paper's preference for its broader
chronology (pp. 127–128).

His use of “simpler” is an author's methodological preference, not this
dossier's criterion for choosing history (pp. 124, 135).

## 4. Languages and evidence actually used

- **Épinal and Erfurt glossaries:** primary English dataset; 336 potentially
  relevant words, 303 judged etymologically usable; root/suffix uncertainty
  excludes others (pp. 110, 122). He treats manuscript-level variation
  statistically rather than deleting unusual forms beforehand (p. 109).
- **Early Northumbrian:** Cædmon's Hymn, Bede's Death Song, Leiden Riddle,
  Franks Casket and partly Ruthwell; interpretation difficulties exclude
  many Ruthwell words (p. 108). The Northumbrian comparison is small and
  cannot certainly distinguish chronology from dialect or chance (p. 134).
- **Old Frisian:** a comparative reconstruction of restricted fronting,
  vowel lengthening before clusters, and differently conditioned breaking;
  not a parallel exhaustive Frisian experiment (pp. 111–115, 126, 135).
- **Modern/late Frisian and Faroese:** phonetic plausibility comparisons,
  especially lengthened versus unlengthened *a (pp. 115–116). These are
  **later parallels**, not surviving evidence of one inherited OE–Frisian
  breaking event.
- **Onomastic English material:** seventh-century names and later
  manuscript variants, largely through Van der Schee; spelling
  interpretation remains contested (pp. 113–114).

## 5. Proposed relative chronology

`<` means precedes; it does not convert a hypothesis into an observation.

### English

**His proposed chain:**

```text
nasal rounding / conditional *a-fronting
    < i-mutation of unbroken vowels
    < breaking in restricted rC contexts
    < beginning of back mutation
```

- `fronting < i-mutation`: inputs to the i-mutation cline distinguish
  previously fronted *æ from *a/[ɑ]; explicit three-step lists
  (pp. 120, 123, 125).
- `i-mutation < breaking`: explicit proposed order (pp. 123, 125,
  127–128); *feormat* can be breaking of already mutated *e < *a
  (p. 131). This is an argued alternative, not an independently observed
  sequence.
- **Variant L:** `retained *a < phonetic lengthening < diphthongization`.
  The proposed lengthening need not be phonemic; *[aˑ] is not a proven
  intermediate (pp. 114–116, 123).
- **Variant F:** `selective *a > æ < i-mutation < breaking of remaining æ`.
  Restricted early fronting before rC removes the need for lengthening as
  the input explanation, though lengthening might still participate
  (pp. 126–127).
- `breaking < back mutation`: concluding reconstructed list, not a
  separately demonstrated universal edge (p. 135); some exceptional early
  forms are already interpreted as back mutation (pp. 121, 129–130).
- **Residual smoothing:** the proposed elimination does not abolish
  smoothing of old long diphthongs; the author explicitly preserves that
  possibility (p. 118 n. 24).

**Standard Theory as the author reports it, not his chain:**

```text
rounding < broad fronting < breaking < restoration
    < i-mutation < Anglian Smoothing
```

This is the relevant contrast in his table and operational account, with
dialectal exclusions and exceptions; palatal diphthongization is not
implemented for his Mercian comparison (pp. 104, 122–123).

### Frisian

```text
restricted fronting / rounding < i-mutation < Frisian breaking
```

This is the comparative framework he reports, not a newly demonstrated
Frisian total order (pp. 105–106, 111–112, 133–135). Old East Frisian
lengthening offers an analogy for English rC contexts (p. 114).
His modern West Frisian `namia > neame` example belongs to a much later
lengthening/fronting/diphthongization history, not the pre-OFris chain
(p. 115). Do not insert it into the inherited stem.

### Shared / undifferentiated stem

The author says the chronologies run **nearly** parallel, explicitly
locating differences at fronting, breaking and back mutation (p. 135).
He supplies no new split point and no complete exclusive shared chain
(pp. 106–107, 135). Similar conditioning supports a comparison; it does
not by itself place an event before separation.

| “(Phonologisation of) i-mutation” no later than 600 | Author's reconstructed boundary; allophonic mutation and phonologization are not interchangeable (p. 135). |

| Back mutation beginning eighth century | Concluding reconstructed date; exceptions interpreted as early back mutation remain (pp. 129–130, 135). |

The citation to Waxenberger in p. 135 n. 33 does not directly date breaking:
Versloot notes that Waxenberger2019 does not mention it there.

## 7. Proposed shared innovations

He compares nasal rounding, restricted fronting and i-mutation as
closely overlapping processes in English and Frisian (pp. 133–135).
He does **not** provide a new proof of their single inherited identity.
His “Anglo-Frisian” hypothesis is an explanatory framework built from
Frisian parallels (pp. 106–107).
The proposed common demographic basis and cultural contacts are his
interpretation of external research, not relative-chronology edges
(p. 106). The paper does not settle shared *ai, *au or velar
palatalization, which it explicitly leaves outside its principal test
(p. 107).

## 8. Proposed independent developments

English rC breaking has no direct Old Frisian counterpart; Frisian /e/
breaking has its own conditioning; back mutation is characterized here
as exclusively English (p. 135).
Anglian *x = [ç] permitting fronting contrasts with Frisian *x = [x]
blocking it in his proposed explanation (pp. 117–118).
Modern Frisian and Faroese breaking-like histories are comparison
cases, not an author's claim of an inherited innovation shared with OE
(pp. 115–116).
Palatal diphthongization is weak in Mercian and only incipient in his
Early Northumbrian sample, with back mutation a competing explanation
for some tokens (pp. 120–121).

**Quantitative evidence, not a tree verdict:** categorical predictions are
ST 237/282 (84%) versus AF 232/282 (82%) in Épinal, and ST 195/274 (71%)
versus AF 197/274 (72%) in Erfurt (pp. 124–125). Adding probabilistic
i-mutation raises AF to 244/282 (87%) and 218/274 (80%) respectively
(pp. 124–126). Both predict the same outcome in 92% and 91% of tokens
(p. 128). The appendix model has 199 cases, four predictors and reported
87% correctness (pp. 139–141). Different categorical/probabilistic
representations and manuscript choices matter: do not call these numbers
proof of chronology or inheritance.

## 10. Tree-test implications

**Governing clarification:** CAPR requires a strict tree with an
Anglo-Frisian ancestral node. This card tests assignment of the author's
proposed events to its stem or daughters and the state/conditioning
reconstructable at that node; it does not test whether CAPR should remove
the node. The author's demographic or subgrouping interpretation remains
distinct from that modelling requirement.

**CAPR analysis:** restricted fronting and mutation can be compatible with
a shared stem if independently supported split and daughter edges permit
it; their overlapping English/Frisian conditions alone do not prove it
(premises: pp. 106–107, 135).
Putting mutation before breaking in both daughters removes an alleged
reversed-order obstacle, but **does not identify the two breakings**:
the author expressly says English breaking has no direct Frisian parallel
(p. 135).

No result here decides SC030/SC043 identity or the inherited status of
palatalization: *au and velars are deferred (p. 107). CAPR must not use
the author's shared demographic/contact narrative (p. 106) as a
wave/diffusion escape hatch from the ordered-tree test. Nor may it adopt
his simplicity preference (p. 135) instead of evidence.

## 11. Uncertainty grading

- **Observation-strength:** manuscript distributions and the reported
  prediction tables, subject to etymology and reading choices (pp. 109–110,
  124–128). These are spellings, not directly observed phonemes.
- **Preferred reconstruction:** restricted fronting, late breaking and
  probabilistic i-mutation; lengthening supplies one phonetic account
  (pp. 116–117, 123–128, 135).
- **Plausible, non-unique:** *ae may reflect [æ], [æˑ] or [æː]; the modern
  parallels; late opacity in *ferwit-* (pp. 115–116, 127, 132).
- **Explicitly unresolved:** lengthening versus early-fronting variants;
  chronological versus dialectal variation; phonemic status of broken
  *ea; Middle English continuation; broad *au and palatalization history
  (pp. 107, 116, 127–128, 134).
