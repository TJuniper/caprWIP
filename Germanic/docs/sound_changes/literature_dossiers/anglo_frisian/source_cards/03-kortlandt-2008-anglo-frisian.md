- **BibTeX key:** Kortlandt2008

- **Pages actually used for this card:** 265–276 (argument + chronologies); 277–278 (reference list only)
- **Genre / venue character:** Short research article / polemical intervention in a specialist Frisian-studies venue. It is a *rejoinder* to Stiles 1995 and Fulk 1998: it advances Kortlandt's own numbered relative chronology and defends the reality of a common Anglo-Frisian stage. It is assertive and compressed — most edges of the numbered chronology are stated, not separately argued, and it leans heavily on Kortlandt's own earlier papers. Independent evidential weight is therefore concentrated in a few diagnostics (§9), not in the chronology as a whole.

## 2. Author's own thesis

Kortlandt argues that there genuinely was "a period of common development not shared by the German dialects" (pp. 274–275) — i.e. an Anglo-Frisian stage — and he answers Stiles's objection by actually constructing the exclusive common relative chronology Stiles said was impossible: "The stages AF 1–10 can appropriately be called Anglo-Frisian" (p. 271). His organizing claim is structural: Proto-Germanic had an asymmetry in the low vowels between long front *ǣ and short back *a, which could be resolved either by fronting *a to æ (Anglo-Frisian) or by backing *ǣ to ā (the other languages, except Gothic where *ǣ was raised to ē early); if *ǣ had already been retracted to ā in West Germanic, "the Anglo-Frisian fronting would be entirely unmotivated" (p. 266). Hence his signature thesis that **West Saxon ǣ (< *ē1) is an archaism, not an innovation** (pp. 265, 266; his 1986, 1999, 2006b). He adopts Krupatkin's principle that long-vowel shifts drive parallel short-vowel shifts (pp. 266, 268–269) and uses it to replace Fulk's "unmotivated" alternation of backing and fronting with a single directional "gradually widening scope of developments" (p. 275). He insists early Anglo-Frisian palatalization (AF 7) must be distinguished from a later Old English palatalization (E 14) (pp. 267, 273). Historically he ties the chronology to two migrations: a 5th-century 'Saxon' migration (at which West Saxon separates) and a 6th-century 'Anglian' migration (at which Anglo-Frisian breaks up), with Kentish siding with Anglian (p. 271).

**Does he accept an Anglo-Frisian node?** Yes, in substance: he affirms a real common Anglo-Frisian development (pp. 271, 274–275). But he frames it in wave/isogloss terms rather than as a clean Stammbaum node — "every development gave rise to at least one isogloss (between groups of speakers who were and who were not affected)" (pp. 271–272), and he prefers "a gradually widening scope of developments that affected progressively more instances under various conditions" (p. 275) over "sweeping developments which affected all possible instances at a stroke."

## 3. Positions reported, not held

- **Stiles 1995** (the target): reported as arguing *against* reconstructing an Anglo-Frisian proto-language "because it is not possible to construct the exclusive common relative chronology that is necessary in order to be able to establish a node on a family tree" (Stiles 1995:211, quoted p. 265). Stiles's list of 10 shared OE/OFris features is reproduced (p. 265). Stiles's own chronology (Kortlandt labels it PS 1–9) is referenced and mapped, not quoted in full (pp. 271–273).
- **Fulk 1998**: his 11-stage relative chronology for the Northumbrian dialect (RF 1–11) is reproduced verbatim (p. 266). Kortlandt reports it in order to criticize the "unmotivated character of the sound changes" — the alternation backing(1)/fronting(3)/backing(5)/fronting(6)/backing(7)/fronting(9)/backing(11) (p. 266) — and Fulk's split between early palatalization (RF 4) and late phonemicization (RF 10), which Kortlandt calls "an unfortunate split" (p. 267).
- **H.F. Nielsen 2001**: his 5-stage Old Frisian chronology (N 1–5) is reproduced (p. 273) and then **rejected** on three grounds (p. 274); Kortlandt says this "in fact eliminates Nielsen's chronology as a possibility."
- **Hogg 1979**: reported (and attacked) as claiming "the various types of palatalization are prime candidates for simultaneous application" (Hogg 1979:108, quoted p. 267).
- **Krupatkin 1970**: his observation that long-vowel shifts trigger parallel short-vowel shifts (1970:63) is quoted and *adopted* as a working principle (pp. 266, 269).
- **H.F. Nielsen 1984** (u-diphthong second elements), **K.M. Nielsen 1961** (Scandinavian breaking, incl. Sparlösa/Rök runic forms), **van Haeringen 1920**, **Campbell 1939**, **Heuser 1903**, **Boutkan 1998**, **Hofmann 1964**, **Klein 1990**: reported supportively as sources of specific data or diagnostics (pp. 267–274).
- **Milroy & Milroy 1985**: weak-tie social-network theory of change is reported approvingly and applied to the invaders (p. 276).

## 4. Languages and evidence actually used

- **Old English, by dialect** — the dialect split does real work: West Saxon (the ǣ-archaism claim; WS 9–12 branch), Anglian/Northumbrian (North. fēll, fēng, eorm; A 11–12), Kentish (K 11–12; ǣ>ē raising as a possible local development), West Mercia (second fronting, mentioned) (pp. 266–271).
- **Old Frisian** — carries the decisive palatalization diagnostics (tsetel, tziake vs kēi/kāi); modern Frisian dialects preserving ē < *ǣ < *ē1 vs ǣ < *ai; Insular North Frisian as the dialect *not* reached by AF 10 (pp. 268, 273–274).
- **Old High German / Old Saxon** — comparanda (tag, tāt, māno, lant, brāhta); OS "is not a homogeneous linguistic entity" and shares some early AF developments (pp. 265, 269–270, 274).
- **North Germanic / Scandinavian** — breaking comparanda, incl. runic Sparlösa, Rök, biaurn/biurn (via K.M. Nielsen 1961) (p. 268).
- **Gothic** — mentioned once (early raising of *ǣ to ē) (p. 266).
- **Latin / onomastic** — Caesar's *Suēbi* (not \*\*Suābi) as evidence for an early front vowel in OHG (p. 266); Angeln, Alemannia analogy (p. 275).
- **Historical / archaeological** (non-linguistic, §6) — Ptolemy, Bede, Jackson 1953, Alcock 1971, the *Gododdin* (pp. 275–276).

## 5. Proposed relative chronology

Kortlandt gives ONE integrated numbered chronology with branch prefixes (pp. 270–271). It is reproduced literally first, then rendered as ordered edges. Prefixes: **WG** West Germanic (all); **AF** Anglo-Frisian common stem; **WS** West Saxon (splits off at the 'Saxon' migration, after AF 8); **AF 9–10** the remaining ("non-Saxon") Anglo-Frisian dialects; **A** Anglian, **K** Kentish, **F** Frisian (branches after the 'Anglian' migration); **E** developments common to the English dialects but *post-dating* the AF break-up.

## 6. Absolute chronology

Kept separate from §5. All dates below are historical/archaeological and stand several inferential steps away from any sound change; Kortlandt uses them to *locate* the two migrations, not to date individual changes.

- **1st c. BC** — Caesar's *Suēbi* (not \*\*Suābi): used to argue a front vowel must be reconstructed for an early stage of OHG (p. 266). Inferential distance from a sound change: large (spelling → vowel quality → *ǣ not retracted).
- **2nd c. AD** — Ptolemy places the Saxons in present-day Holstein (p. 275).
- **mid-3rd c.** — Saxons "in control of the whole region between the Elbe and the Weser"; **4th c.** — they reach the Netherlands (Nielsen 1981:265, p. 275).
- **'Saxon' migration to Britain = 5th c.** — West Saxon separates from Anglo-Frisian; the 'Saxon' fronting + breaking dated "after the migration to Britain in the 5th century but before the monophthongization of *ea to *ē2" (pp. 269, 271, 273). Vortigern (~450) to the battle of Mount Badon (~500), then ~half a century of peace; 'Saxons' settled in Kent and Sussex (p. 275).
- **'Anglian' migration = 6th c.** — break-up of Anglo-Frisian; subjugation of the north from the mid-6th c.; battle of Catterick ~600 (*Gododdin*) (pp. 269, 271, 275–276). Kentish invasion identified chronologically with the 'Anglian' invasion (p. 268 "second invasion in Kent in the sixth century"; p. 276).
- Sound changes themselves are **not** given calendar dates except by this relative-to-migration anchoring.

## 7. Proposed shared innovations

Kortlandt's explicit inventory of Anglo-Frisian (English ∩ Frisian, *not* shared with German incl. OS) is at pp. 274–275, mapping to AF 1–8:

- **Centralization of stressed vowels before nasals, with loss of nasalization before fricatives and rounding** (AF 1–3) — OE/OFris mōna, lond, brōhte, tōth/tōþ, gōs (pp. 269, 274).
- **Monophthongization of *ai to ā** (AF 4) (p. 274).
- **The limited retraction of *ǣ to ā** (AF 5) — "before w and before g plus back vowel" (pp. 270, 274).
- **Fronting of non-breaking *a to æ, blocked by a back vowel in the following syllable** (AF 6) (p. 274).
- **Palatalization of *k and *g by non-broken front vowels** (AF 7) (p. 274).
- **Analogical restoration of the back vowel** in 2sg *farist, 3sg *fariþ, *slahist, *slahiþ (AF 8) (pp. 273, 274).

He concludes: "there can be little doubt about a period of common development not shared by the German dialects" (pp. 274–275). Additionally **AF 9–10** (monophthongization of *ea > ē; raising of *ǣ > ē) are shared by **Anglian + Frisian** (continental contact) but explicitly **not** by West Saxon (pp. 268, 271, 273 RF 6 = AF 10) — a *sub*-shared innovation, not an English∩Frisian one.

## 8. Proposed independent developments

- **Breaking** — "It is well-known that breaking … took place under different conditions in English and Frisian" (p. 265). In the chronology, breaking is dispersed across separate post-split stages: WS 9/11/12, A 11, K 11 (early, before umlaut) vs **F 14 (late, after i-mutation F 13)** (pp. 270–271, 273). Hence parallel, not a single inherited event. (He does treat *a-breaking* vs *u-breaking* as partly shared innovations of subsets: a-breaking in West Saxon + Scandinavian, u-breaking in Anglian + Frisian, p. 269 — but still not a unitary English∩Frisian breaking.)
- **Palatalization (the late layer)** — the "second English palatalization" E 14 (and phonemicization) is distinct from the early Anglo-Frisian AF 7 and has **no Frisian counterpart** (pp. 267, 271, 273). "We have to distinguish between an early Anglo-Frisian development and a later Old English innovation" (pp. 267, 273).
- **Second/continued fronting** — the 'Saxon' second fronting (WS 11) and Anglian fronting (A 11) affected the *remaining* instances of *a after the AF 6 stage, dialect by dialect (pp. 267, 270–271, 272) — parallel extensions, not one event.
- **i-mutation** — appears as two separate late stages, **E 16** (English) and **F 13** (Frisian) (p. 271; RF 9 = E 16 + F 13, p. 273) — parallel.
- **Kentish raising of *ǣ to ē** — "was probably a local development, perhaps under the influence of a second invasion in Kent in the sixth century" (p. 268) — independent of the Anglian/Frisian AF 10 raising.
- **Uniform plural verbal ending** (Stiles's feature 10) — "fully shared by Old Saxon, is quite irrelevant … and must be kept apart" (p. 265) — not Anglo-Frisian-diagnostic at all.

## 10. Tree-test implications

**(CAPR analysis — not Kortlandt's own framing.)** Applying the criterion in `../README.md` to Kortlandt's chronology as stated:

**Can occupy a shared ancestral (English∩Frisian) node — candidate `H`:** AF 1–8, if the chronology holds, all precede the first split (the 5th-c. 'Saxon' migration, after AF 8). So they are placeable on the common stem without violating a later daughter-specific edge:
- nasal centralization + loss of nasalization + rounding (AF 1–3);
- *ai > ā monophthongization (AF 4);
- limited retraction *ǣ > ā (AF 5);
- fronting of non-breaking *a > æ (AF 6);
- **early** palatalization of *k, *g before front vowels (AF 7);
- analogical back-vowel restoration (AF 8).
These are exactly the changes Kortlandt lists as the common period (§7), and his internal edges AF 4 < AF 6 < AF 7 < AF 8 are *demonstrated*, which strengthens the `H` reading for the fronting/palatalization complex specifically.

**Must be independent parallel changes under a tree model — forced `P`:**
- **Breaking.** This is the decisive configuration from `../README.md`. In Frisian, breaking (F 14) must *follow* i-mutation (F 13); in English, breaking (WS 9/11–12, A 11, K 11) *precedes* i-mutation (E 16). A daughter-specific edge (F 13 < F 14) that English does not share forces "breaking" to be at least two historically distinct events. Surface OE/OFris breaking correspondences are therefore `P`, not `H`. (Kortlandt agrees in substance — p. 265 — though he never states it as a tree test.)
- **Second/late palatalization.** E 14 has no Frisian counterpart and post-dates the break-up; any OE↔OFris palatalization identity beyond AF 7 is `P`.
- **i-mutation.** E 16 vs F 13 are separate post-split stages ⇒ `P` for the OE/OFris identity, even though both are "the same" umlaut typologically.
- **AF 9–10 (monophthong. *ea>ē; raising *ǣ>ē).** These are shared by Anglian + Frisian but **not** West Saxon. Under a tree in which West Saxon is a coordinate English branch, they cannot sit on an English∩Frisian node; they belong to a *non-Saxon* subnode (or are `P` if one insists on an English node). Kentish ǣ>ē is separately flagged as possibly independent (p. 268).

**Where the author substitutes wave/continuum for an ordering edge (records absence of a tree-usable edge, not error):**
- "every development gave rise to at least one isogloss … and the chronology only holds for the dialects which have actually come down to us" (pp. 271–272);
- "a gradually widening scope of developments that affected progressively more instances under various conditions" (p. 275);
- Milroy & Milroy weak-tie mobility model applied to the 5th–6th-c. invaders (p. 276).
At these points Kortlandt is explicitly modelling Anglo-Frisian as diffusion across a mobile continuum; they supply narrative cohesion but no additional tree-usable edges beyond those already in §5.

## 11. Uncertainty grading

**Firmly demonstrated (a diagnostic compels the edge) — mark `[demonstrated]` in §5:**
- *ai > ā (AF 4) precedes fronting (AF 6) and palatalization (AF 7): Frisian kēi/kāi unpalatalized vs tsetel/tziake palatalized (pp. 268, 274).
- *ē1/*a fronting older than *ai fronting: higher modern-dialect reflex of *ē1 (p. 274).
- AF 6 < AF 7 (palatalization targets the *newly fronted* vowels; OFris palatalizes before e<*æ, ē<*ǣ, not before ǣ<*ai) (p. 268).
- AF 7 (early palatalization) distinct from and earlier than E 14/E 16 (palatal/velar layering under restoration + second fronting + umlaut) (pp. 273–274).
- F 11 (*ā<*ai > ǣ) precedes F 12 (*au > ā), because au-ā is un-fronted until umlaut (p. 274).

**Author's preferred reconstruction (coherent, but he knows alternatives exist):**
- **West Saxon ǣ is an archaism, not an innovation** — argued from the low-vowel asymmetry + Caesar Suēbi (pp. 266, 272), but this reverses the mainstream reading and rests on structural motivation plus one onomastic form.
- The two-migration ('Saxon' 5th c. / 'Anglian' 6th c.) historical scaffold and the identification of the first breaking with the 'Saxon' fronting (pp. 267, 269, 271, 275–276).
- *ē2 from disyllabic *ea (pp. 268–269) — flagged with "I think", relies on his 1991/1994/2006b.
- The AF 6 blocking conditions (l, r, h + C; open-syllable back vowel), adopted specifically "if we want to avoid the assumption that fronted æ was again retracted to a" (pp. 267, 272; cf. Heuser 1903:1) — a theory-internal motivation, not an independent proof.

**Asserted only (part of the numbered list, not separately argued in this paper) — mark `[asserted]` in §5:**
- Internal order AF 1 < AF 2 < AF 3 (the nasal package) (p. 270).
- Placement of AF 5 (retraction of *ǣ before w / g+back vowel) relative to AF 4/AF 6 (p. 270).
- Essentially all fine-grained edges inside WS 9–12, A 11–12, K 11–12, E 13–16, and F 11–14 — the branch chronologies are presented as a finished reconstruction with little per-edge argument. In particular **F 13 < F 14 (Frisian breaking after i-mutation)** is asserted here (though it agrees with Nielsen's N 4 < N 5, p. 273, and Boutkan 1998).

**Explicitly hedged / unresolved (do not harden):**
- "it is natural to assume that *ai had been monophthongized … and that *au had remained unchanged" (p. 267) — "natural to assume."
- Kentish raising *ǣ>ē "was **probably** a local development, **perhaps** under the influence of a second invasion" (p. 268).
- Phonetics of the central vowel: "*ə **may have been** [ʌ] or [ʌ̃]" (p. 269).
- "**possibly** 'the second element of the broken reflex of æ was also -u at first'" (Nielsen quoted, p. 268).

**Transcription limits:** the schwa/macron encoding defects in the initial
extract have been repaired in the committed reference. The author's
"e < *ä" (p. 268) is retained as printed; any implementation must establish
its intended phonological value rather than silently replace the symbol.
