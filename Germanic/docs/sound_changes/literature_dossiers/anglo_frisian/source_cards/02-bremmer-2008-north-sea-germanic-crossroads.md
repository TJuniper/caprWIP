## 2. Author's own thesis

Bremmer's stated purpose is "critically to review the present state of
research and to present an alternative to the confusing ideas and
terminology which currently prevail" about how far Frisian was once spoken
along the coast south-west of the modern province of Friesland, i.e. in
Holland (p. 280; the problem restated p. 285). His method is to **order the
Proto-Old Frisian sound changes into a relative chronology** and then use
them as a diagnostic checklist to detect a Frisian (as opposed to
Franconian/coastal) substratum in Hollandish place-names and appellatives
(pp. 286, 288). He gives "primacy to the phonological criteria" (p. 286).

Crucially for this area, Bremmer's **own genetic framework is not
"Anglo-Frisian" but "Ingvaeonic / North Sea Germanic"**, conceived as a
*coastal dialect continuum* spoken ca. 200–400 "from Calais in the south to
Husum in the north which was fairly homogeneous or rather, which formed a
continuum in which the transitions from south to north were very gradual"
(p. 284). Old English is one offshoot that "detached from their continental
roots" after 400 (p. 284); Frisian, Low German and Hollandish are the
continental offshoots. He explicitly frames the community-of-forms
explanation in terms of the **Wellentheorie** (wave theory): the coastal
dialect "was neither Franconian nor Frisian, but Ingvaeonic (also called
North Sea Germanic)" (pp. 292–293). He locates the point where Ingvaeonic
becomes specifically Proto-Frisian at **breaking (change 1.8)**: "In my
opinion, this point of departure is breaking (1.8)" (p. 293). The term
"Anglo-Frisian" is **never used by Bremmer in his own argument** — it
appears in the paper only as the title of Stiles 1995 in the bibliography
(p. 307). Bremmer neither defends nor attacks a two-way OE+OFris genetic
node; he subsumes the shared OE/OFris features under the wider Ingvaeonic
grouping (p. 288).

## 3. Positions reported, not held

- **Stiles 1995** — Bremmer *adopts* (does not merely report) Stiles's
  relative chronology: "I follow the relative chronology for Proto-Old
  Frisian as established by Patrick Stiles (1995:199–200) and followed, on
  the whole, by Hans Frede Nielsen (2001)" (p. 286). He also quotes Stiles
  for method: "as Stiles (1995:182) has put it, 'phonology is the most
  closed, and structured subsystem of languages and least susceptible to
  accept borrowed material. This is less true of morphology, syntax and
  lexis, in ascending order'" (p. 286). **See §13 and the strong caveat in
  §9/§10: Bremmer's numbered list 1.1–1.9 is his presentation of what he
  attributes to Stiles's ordering; it is NOT Stiles's own text and must
  never be cited as Stiles's evidence.**
- **Nielsen 2001** — reported as following Stiles's Proto-Old Frisian
  chronology "on the whole" (p. 286); also Nielsen 1985 cited for the
  sociolinguistic generalisation that mixed-origin immigrant communities
  choose less-marked or compromise forms (p. 296).
- **Schönfeld (1970 = van Loey 1970)** — reported as convinced of Frisian
  spread into Holland (p. 284, quoted p. 285); his phonological conditions
  for rounding of *a* are reported and then criticised as "unintelligible
  from a phonetic point of view" (p. 289). Schönfeld is the source of most
  of the coastal-Dutch data in 2.1–2.9.
- **Cor van Bree (1977)** — reported as reticent, limiting secure Frisian
  to the modern province of Friesland; Bremmer quotes and pushes back
  (pp. 285–286).
- **van Coetsem (1987)** — his loan-phonology / two-transfer-types contact
  model is reported and adopted as the mechanism for the Frisian→Franconian
  shift (p. 296).
- **Eric Hoekstra (1993, 1994a/b)** — syntactic/morphological Frisian–North-
  Hollandish parallels reported and critiqued as synchronic and
  non-diachronic (pp. 297–299).
- **Heeroma** — reported (via Hoekstra 1995) as having ideologically tried
  to "argue away the role of Frisian" (p. 300).
- **Wellentheorie tradition** — reported as the successor to the
  nineteenth-century "erstwhile Frisophone population" explanation
  (pp. 292–293).

## 4. Languages and evidence actually used

- **Old Frisian** and **Old Low Franconian (OLFranc) / Middle Dutch /
  Hollandish** are the two poles of every comparison (pp. 287–291). The
  paper's contrast set is **OFris vs OLFranc/Hollandish**, *not* OFris vs
  Old English.
- **Old English** is invoked only to note that most of the numbered changes
  "also apply to Old English" as Ingvaeonic innovations (p. 288); it does
  **no** independent argumentative work and receives no ordered OE chain.
- **Coastal Dutch place-names, field-names, personal names and appellatives**
  (Holland, Zeeland, Flanders) carry the empirical load (pp. 288–294),
  drawn largely from Schönfeld, Blok, van Loon, Rentenaar, Weijnen.
- **Runic material** is used once, for absolute dating (the ~500 futhark
  reform, p. 288).
- **Archaeology, settlement history, sociolinguistics** frame the narrative
  (pp. 280–283, 295–299) but supply no phonological ordering.
- Dialect granularity: within Frisian, West/East/North Frisian are
  distinguished (p. 293); within the coast, North-Holland vs South-Holland,
  down to Flanders (pp. 289–291, 295).

## 5. Proposed relative chronology

> **Caveat (do not skip):** Bremmer presents changes **1.1–1.9** as a
> numbered survey which he states is "the relative chronology for Proto-Old
> Frisian as established by Patrick Stiles (1995:199–200)" (p. 286), and he
> says "I shall stick to the order as given in my relative chronology, i.e.
> 2.1 runs parallel with 1.1, etc." (p. 288). The *numbering order* is thus
> asserted to be chronological, but Bremmer supplies an explicit
> **diagnostic feeding/ordering argument for only a few edges**; the rest
> is an inherited (Stiles-attributed) sequence without per-edge argument in
> this paper. Only the argued edges are tree-usable.

### Frisian
Asserted total order (Bremmer's numbering, attributed to Stiles), with
inputs/outputs summarised (p. 287):
```
1.1 nasal rounding (a,ā > o/ō / _N)
  < 1.2 nasal-spirant loss + comp. lengthening (a > ō / _N+voiceless fric.)
  < 1.3 long ā > /æː/ in closed syllables
  < 1.4 *ai, *au monophthongise (OFris ā/ē, ā)
  < 1.5 short a > æ in closed syllables
  < 1.6 velar palatalisation (k > ts, g > j)
  < 1.7 i-mutation (incl. long vowels)
  < 1.8 breaking (i, e > iu/io before ch, chs/cht)
  < 1.9 unrounding of all i-mutated vowels to e/ē
```
Edges with an explicit diagnostic argument in *this* paper:
- edge `1.1 < 1.2`: (pp. 288–289) explicit feeding: "no 2.2 without 2.1"
  (p. 288), and "we must assume that rounding of *a* took place on a wide
  scale … and that this change preceded 1.2" (p. 289). Rounding before
  nasals must precede nasal-spirant loss for the latter to yield rounded
  `ō` from `a` (*tanþ* > *tōth*).
- edge `1.5 < 1.6`: (pp. 289–290) explicit ordering from *-dei* < *dag*:
  "The form can only be explained by assuming that first the vowel was
  raised and then the velar was palatalised to *j*" (p. 290). Raising of
  *a* precedes velar palatalisation.
- edge `1.7 < 1.9`: (pp. 287, 291) unrounding (1.9) targets exactly "all
  vowels that underwent i-mutation" (p. 287; restated p. 291), so i-mutation
  must precede unrounding.
- Additional (off-list) edge implied at pp. 290–291: **i-mutation precedes
  loss of /w/** — river-name *Swēt* keeps /w/ because the mutated vowel
  blocked *w*-drop, whereas *zoet* < *swōti* shows "unmutated vowel plus
  loss of *w* before /u/" (p. 290). *w*-loss is not in the numbered list.
- Edges `1.2 < 1.3 < 1.4 < 1.5` and `1.6 < 1.7 < 1.8`: asserted by the
  numbering (Stiles-attributed), **no per-edge diagnostic given here.**

### Shared / undifferentiated stem
Bremmer places 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4 (*ai > ā), 1.5, 1.6, 1.7 on the shared
**Ingvaeonic** stem (common to OFris and OE, and — per the 2.x survey —
found also in coastal Dutch): "Practically all of these phenomena are
Ingvaeonic innovations within West Germanic, and most of them also apply to
Old English, viz. 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4 (*ai > ā), 1.5, 1.6 and 1.7" (p. 288).
Breaking (1.8) is placed **after** the split as specifically Frisian
(pp. 293, 295).

## 6. Absolute chronology

Kept strictly apart from §5.

- **Changes 1.1–1.5: before or around 500.** "Apart from relative datings
  for 1.1–5, we also have fairly reliable indications that these changes
  must have taken place before or around 500, because around 500 certain
  innovations were carried through in the runic alphabet" (p. 288). The
  reform: the original *a*-rune was reassigned to the new /æ/, a new "ash"
  rune ⟨A⟩ invented for unchanged *a*, and a mutant ⟨O⟩ for rounded *a*
  before nasals (p. 288). **Inferential distance from dated object to sound
  change is large:** date of the runic-alphabet reform → the phonetic
  values assumed for the reassigned/new runes → the inference that the
  fronting/rounding changes must already have applied. Bremmer himself
  hedges the dating as "fairly reliable indications" (p. 288). Runic
  inscriptions generally dated 400–800 (Bremmer reports Page 2001, p. 286).
- **Ingvaeonic continuum: ca. 200–400** (p. 284) — dates the *continuum*,
  not any specific change.
- **Anglo-Saxon detachment: after 400** (p. 284).
- Political/settlement dates (Frisian expansion to the Scheldt by 7th c.;
  Lex Frisionum ca. 800; first record of "Holland" 1101; annexation of
  West-Friesland 1289) (pp. 279–283, 295, 299) date *history*, not sound
  changes.

## 7. Proposed shared innovations

Developments Bremmer regards as common — but at the **Ingvaeonic (North Sea
Germanic)** level, i.e. shared by OFris *and* OE *and* (per 2.x) coastal
Dutch, **not** a specifically two-way Anglo-Frisian set:

- 1.1 nasal rounding; 1.2 nasal-spirant loss; 1.3 long ā > /æː/; 1.4 *ai >
  ā (the *ai branch specifically); 1.5 short a > æ; 1.6 velar
  palatalisation; 1.7 i-mutation — "most of them also apply to Old English,
  viz. 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4 (*ai > ā), 1.5, 1.6 and 1.7" (p. 288).
- The whole point of the 2.1–2.9 coastal survey is that these features also
  surface in Hollandish/coastal Dutch (pp. 288–294), i.e. they are pan-
  coastal Ingvaeonic, not diagnostic of any narrow node.

## 8. Proposed independent developments

Bremmer's "independent" axis is **Frisian vs Franconian/Hollandish**, not
Frisian vs English. He identifies as specifically Frisian (post-split,
not shared by neighbouring continental Ingvaeonic):

- **Breaking (1.8)** — his declared point of departure for Proto-Frisian;
  the Kennwort *tiuch* / field-name *'t Joch* is "typically seen as a
  Kennwort" for Frisian land-management (p. 293).
- **Initial þ > t** (Frisian *trije*, *tûsen*; place-name *Tillebreg*; MDu
  *tille*), vs Dutch voicing to *d* (p. 293).
- **Medial -þþ-/-dþ- > -tt-** (*Zwet(te)*, *betten*, *botting* < *bod-thing*),
  vs Franconian voiced *bodding* (p. 293).
- **twisken** 'between' with tw- and -sk- (cf. OFris *twiska*), a Frisian
  relict not found beyond the southern border of Holland (pp. 293–294).
- **Unrounding (1.9)** of i-mutation products (pp. 291, 293).

He does **not** argue that any OE/OFris correspondence is an independent
parallel change; the OE side is simply folded into the shared Ingvaeonic
stem (p. 288).

## 10. Tree-test implications

**This is CAPR analysis, per `../README.md`. Bremmer's own model is a
wave/continuum model and is reported faithfully in §2; the following
translates his data into CAPR's strict ordered-tree formalism, which may
NOT use wave/Sprachbund/continuum/linkage as an escape hatch.**

- **If Bremmer's chronology is correct, which alleged Anglo-Frisian
  innovations can still occupy a shared ancestral node?** Bremmer supplies
  no OE-specific ordering edges at all, so he introduces **no new
  daughter-specific edge that would force any OE/OFris correspondence to be
  parallel**. On his account 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4(*ai), 1.5, 1.6, 1.7 sit on
  a shared stem (p. 288) and could, as far as this paper goes, occupy a
  common node. **But the node he assigns them to is Ingvaeonic / North Sea
  Germanic — wider than OE+OFris.** Because the same features also appear
  in coastal Dutch/Hollandish (the entire 2.1–2.9 survey, pp. 288–294),
  Bremmer's evidence tells *against* assigning them to a *specifically*
  two-way Anglo-Frisian node: a feature shared by OE, OFris **and** coastal
  Dutch cannot be a diagnostic Anglo-Frisian innovation. Under CAPR this
  pushes such features toward class **S** (surface isogloss at best for the
  narrow OE–OFris question), with the true shared stem, if any, located
  higher (North Sea Germanic).
- **Which apparent correspondences would necessarily have to be independent
  parallel changes under a tree model?** Bremmer forces **none** for the
  OE–OFris pair. Internally to Frisian, his ordered edges (1.1<1.2,
  1.5<1.6, 1.7<1.9) are tree-usable *within a future Frisian cascade*.
  One CAPR-relevant flag (inference, not Bremmer's claim): Bremmer's *a*
  fronting is stated as "in closed syllables" for both long ā (1.3) and
  short a (1.5) (p. 287), whereas OE first fronting/Anglo-Frisian
  Brightening (SC043) fronts *a* generally outside nasal contexts. If that
  conditioning difference is real and not an artefact of Bremmer's compact
  formulation, it is exactly the "apparent identity is false / different
  conditioning" branch of the CAPR test — a reason to keep the OE and
  Frisian fronting as separate rows pending adjudication. This is **CAPR
  analysis flagged as inference**, not something Bremmer argues.
- **Where does the author invoke wave/continuum/linkage in place of an
  ordering argument?** (Neutral record — these passages supply no
  tree-usable edge.)
  - The founding move: Ingvaeonic as a gradual **continuum** "from Calais …
    to Husum … in which the transitions from south to north were very
    gradual" (p. 284).
  - Explicit **Wellentheorie** for the coastal correspondences: the coastal
    dialect "was neither Franconian nor Frisian, but Ingvaeonic" (pp. 292–
    293).
  - **Contact / language-shift model** (van Coetsem 1987) for the
    Frisian→Hollandish transition, incl. "sound substitution" of the *ā* <
    *au*/*ai* diphthong reflexes (pp. 296–297) — this narrates *replacement
    by contact*, not an ordered internal change.
  - Sociolinguistic/settlement argumentation (prestige of a Frisian trading
    elite, defrisianisation, immigration of mixed-origin colonists)
    (pp. 283, 295–296, 299) — supplies migration/contact history, **no**
    ordering edge.

## 11. Uncertainty grading

- **Firmly demonstrated (data compel):** essentially none is presented as
  compelled. The individual etymologies (e.g. *tōth* < *tanþ*) are secure,
  but every *chronological* and *substratum* conclusion is hedged.
- **Author's preferred reconstruction (consistent, alternatives known):**
  breaking (1.8) as the point of departure for Proto-Frisian — "In my
  opinion, this point of departure is breaking" (p. 293); the Ingvaeonic-
  continuum framework (p. 284); the Stiles-attributed order 1.1–1.9 as a
  whole (p. 286).
- **Plausible but non-unique inference:** that a Frisian substratum
  underlies Hollandish coastal forms (pp. 288–294); the ~500 runic terminus
  for 1.1–1.5, explicitly only "fairly reliable indications" (p. 288); the
  place-name/personal-name diagnostics, which Bremmer himself flags as
  "somewhat risky … because persons can travel" (pp. 289–290).
- **Explicitly unresolved (author says so):** whether the rounded nasal
  form ever became "a fixed phoneme" — "Perhaps, the rounded form had not
  yet become a fixed phoneme" (p. 289); the origin of the Hoekstra
  syntactic parallels — "I take it to be a matter of register" (p. 299);
  whether Frisian was ever spoken as far as Flanders (p. 292); the
  post-Roman continuity of the Frisians (note 1, p. 301).

**Hedges preserved verbatim (do not upgrade):** "I therefore assume as a
working hypothesis" (p. 283); "most likely … spoke an early form of
Frisian" (p. 283); "Theoretically speaking, we must assume" (p. 289);
"could mean … a possible indication therefore" (p. 290); "In my opinion"
(p. 293); "For the time being, then, I take it to be a matter of register"
(p. 299).

## 12. Implementation-ready extracts (Frisian)

Preparation only — **not** an implementation. Inputs/outputs and
conditioning as Bremmer states them (all p. 287 unless noted); ordering
from §5; uncertainty from §11.

- **1.1 Nasal rounding.** input WGmc *a*, *ā*; output OFris *o*, *ō*;
  conditioning `/ _ {n, m, ŋg}` via nasalisation then velarisation; scope
  short and long low vowel; stage: earliest, before ~500 (§6); ordering:
  `1.1 < 1.2` (feeding, p. 288–289); evidence *mon* < *man-*, *mōna* <
  *māno*; uncertainty: firm as an OFris/OLFranc contrast, "fixed phoneme"
  status open (p. 289).
- **1.2 Nasal-spirant loss.** input *V nasal + voiceless fricative (s, f,
  þ)*; output loss of nasal with compensatory lengthening, `a → ō`; scope
  short a/i/u + N + {s,f,þ}; ordering `1.1 < 1.2`; evidence *tōth* < *tanþ*;
  uncertainty: firm.
- **1.3 Long ā raising.** input WGmc *ā*; output OFris /æː/; conditioning
  "in closed syllables"; evidence *skēp* < *skāp*; **flag conditioning vs
  OE (§10).**
- **1.4 Diphthong monophthongisation.** input *ai*, *au*; output OFris
  *ā/ē* (from *ai*) and *ā* (from *au*); evidence *klāth*, *stēn*, *ēk*
  (*ai); *bām* (*au); uncertainty: firm for OFris outcomes.
- **1.5 Short a raising.** input WGmc *a*; output OFris *æ*; conditioning
  "in closed syllables"; ordering `1.5 < 1.6` (p. 290); evidence *stef* <
  *staf*; **flag conditioning vs OE (§10).**
- **1.6 Velar palatalisation.** input *k*, *g*; output *ts*, *j*;
  conditioning "usually before, but sometimes after front vowels";
  ordering `1.5 < 1.6`; evidence *tserl* < *kerl*, *ield* < *geld*.
- **1.7 i-mutation.** input back/round vowels incl. **long** vowels; output
  fronted (later unrounded, 1.9); conditioning i/j in following syllable;
  ordering `1.7 < 1.9` and `1.7 < w-loss` (pp. 287, 290–291); evidence
  *dēma* < *dōmjan*, *Swēt*.
- **1.8 Breaking.** input short *i*, *e*; output *iu/io* (riucht/riocht);
  conditioning "before ch and chs/cht"; stage: **first specifically
  Frisian change, post-Ingvaeonic split** (p. 293); evidence *riucht* <
  *reht*, *tiuch/'t Joch*; uncertainty: author's preferred point of
  departure.
- **1.9 Unrounding.** input i-mutated round vowels (short & long); output
  *e*, *ē*; ordering `1.7 < 1.9`; evidence *bregge* < *brugja*, *hel* <
  *hulja*, *dēda* < *daudjan*, *tēth* < *tōþi*.

## 13. Dependencies on sources CAPR does not hold

Load-bearing works this card's claims rest on, with current acquisition status
(cross-ref `../missing-direct-sources.md`):

- **Stiles 1995, "Remarks on the 'Anglo-Frisian' Thesis"** (Friesische
  Studien II, 177–220) — **critical, not held.** The *entire* relative
