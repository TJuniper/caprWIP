## 2. Author's own thesis

Bremmer treats English–Frisian proximity as real but its explanation as more complex than either mass Frisian participation in the settlement of Britain or inheritance alone: cultural developments before and after migration also contributed (pp. 127–128). His conclusion refers to shared origin in a *hypothetical* Anglo-Frisian mother dialect, but does not make all parallels single inherited innovations (p. 128). He expressly calls English and Frisian palatalization separate changes and contrasts their breaking chronologies (pp. 31–32, 35). Frisian's distinctive profile is reconstructed by relative order and comparison across its daughter dialects; labio-velar mutation “would seem” especially diagnostic of its differentiation, not a precisely dated boundary of language separation (pp. 22, 36, 41).

## 3. Positions reported, not held

- Bremmer says he follows the chronology outlined by **Stiles 1995**, with **Fulk 1998** and **Nielsen 2001** also cited. This is Bremmer's report/adoption, **not direct consultation of Stiles or Fulk** (p. 22). The palatalization sequence and conventional Frisian breaking date again cite Stiles; the rejection of ordinary breaking in “church” reports Stiles too (pp. 31, 34–35).
- Nielsen's statement that OE is closer to OFris than to any other Germanic language is quoted as Nielsen's conclusion (p. 126); Löfstedt's counts of exclusive lexical parallels are reported findings, not independently counted here (p. 126).
- The old explanation through large-scale Frisian migration to Britain is reported and challenged by Bremmer's limited historical and archaeological evidence; a counterproposal involving Anglo-Saxons remaining in Frisia is only reported (pp. 127–128).
- The explanation through retention of North Sea Germanic features is introduced as the “general opinion today”; the resistance to Frankish innovations is explicitly hypothetical, and the cultural account is then incorporated in Bremmer's conclusion (p. 128).
- Sjölin's chronological rather than dialectal classification and its criticism by Munske are reported; Bremmer warns that manuscripts contain texts of differing provenance and dates (pp. 17–18). De Haan's and Versloot's periodizations are competing analyses, not automatic sound-change dates (pp. 122–125).
- The later “illness” diphthongs may be contamination of two words, attributed to Århammar, rather than continued breaking; analogical explanations of medial palatal variants are attributed to Laker (pp. 32, 35).

## 4. Languages and evidence actually used

- OE/OFris comparisons do chronological work in *slā/slēan*, the “purchase” loan, and initial versus medial palatalization; Kentish unrounding and Northumbrian final-n loss are **dialect-specific parallels**, not pan-English outcomes (pp. 30–32, 126).
- OFris plus modern West, East and North Frisian support distributional reconstructions; Island North Frisian's mutated-u reflexes constrain completion of unrounding, while Old Weser/Rüstring, Old Ems and Old West Frisian retain different vowel and “church” histories (pp. 33–36, 109–114).
- Dutch, German, Old Saxon and other Germanic comparanda identify inherited vowels, nasal loss, gemination and exceptions; Latin loans test fronting/palatalization, though reborrowing and Low German/Dutch influence are acknowledged (pp. 23–28, 31).
- Personal-name **Briocht**, Rüstring plural **gerso**, and verbal **siucht** are relative-order witnesses, not just vocabulary examples (pp. 34–35, 40).
- Runic character differentiation is evidence of cultural/orthographic similarity. No individual dated inscription is supplied here to demonstrate the whole chronological chain or its genealogy (pp. 127–128). Medieval sources are late: scant runic material c.500–800, manuscript glosses c.1200, the oldest full law manuscripts around 1300 (p. 6).

## 5. Proposed relative chronology

Notation: `<` = precedes; **E** = explicit author edge; **C** = conditional or qualified inference; **R** = received chronology reported/adopted. These labels grade argument status, not mechanical certainty. **No total order is reconstructed.**

### English

```text
English breaking < English palatalization
short-a fronting < retraction before a following back vowel
```

- First edge: **E/R**, the exact clause is “palatalization is demonstrably later than breaking as a phonemic change.” The attachment of “as a phonemic change” is syntactically ambiguous; this card records the stated order without assigning that qualifier exclusively to breaking or reconstructing separate phonetic/phonemic-layer orders. Bremmer suggests breaking might consequently be the first unambiguously English change. He supplies no English derivation demonstrating that edge on this page (p. 35).
- Second edge: **E**, p. 30 describes retraction of the fronting product: **stæf : stafas**, contrasted with Frisian absence of this retraction. This is an ordered process description, not proof that the antecedent fronting itself was English-only (p. 30).
- English “purchase” has palatalization absent in Frisian; Bremmer says each palatalization followed changes unique to its language, but does **not name and formally order the English precursor** in this passage. Do not invent an English au-fronting edge from this comparison alone (p. 31).

**Readable subchain, not a completed chain:**

```text
long-ā / short-a fronting < palatalization < ai-monophthongization < i-mutation < breaking
long-ā fronting < au-monophthongization < i-mutation
```

The first line selects §44's stronger pre-mutation reconstruction, not its weaker alternative, and retains the unresolved §42a tension. Neither line establishes palatalization versus au-monophthongization, breaking versus labio-velar mutation, or an absolute date (pp. 29–36).

### Shared / undifferentiated stem

WGmc gemination is explicitly common West Germanic, not specifically Anglo-Frisian (p. 23). The nasalization/fronting relationships are presented in the North Sea Germanic discussion, with wider OE/OS evidence for plural leveling (pp. 24–27). That classification and distribution support earlier/wider scope, but **do not prove a uniquely Anglo-Frisian stem event**. The parallel inventory at p. 126 is not itself an ancestral reconstruction; p. 128 expressly allows cultural contributions to the similarities.

### Internal tensions — checked, not silently reconciled

1. **Actual textual tension: palatalization versus ai-monophthongization.** §42a says the initial change occurs before front vowels as they existed after both fronting and monophthongization of ai/au; §44 explicitly places palatalization **before ai**, and Remark 1 gives a multiword non-palatal inventory (pp. 30–32). Having checked §43, §44, both Remarks, §38 and §47, the contradiction in the stated ai order remains. The au portion is not an independently established reverse edge in §44. Preserve both passages; do not silently correct the printed author's prose or invent a split chronology to cure it (pp. 29–33).
2. **Distinct events, not necessarily contradiction: mutation versus medial palatalization.** §47 restricts its chronological argument to **initial** velars. Medial i/j palatalization, varying nominal outcomes and strong participles are separately discussed. Those passages do not license exporting *kening*'s initial-stop order to all positions, nor grammatical conditioning as the phonological law (pp. 30–33, 111).
3. **Qualified completion:** §45's general e outcome and §71's “all” ten changes before island settlement must be read with §47's explicit Island North Frisian i exception and incompletely finished unrounding; core mutation is not identical with completion of every unrounding/merger (pp. 32–33, 41). The history chapter allows settlement in the late seventh or early eighth century, rather than an exact uniform cutoff (p. 6).
4. **Breaking's “illness” exception:** §49's nonvelar ti-stem explanation is explicitly cross-referenced to §50 Remark 2, which considers prolonged activity **or** contamination. This is unresolved evidence, not license for a second unconstrained breaking law (pp. 34–35).
5. **Weakening versus deletion:** §50 explicitly places breaking before intervocalic χ-to-h weakening, while Remark 1 argues that breaking is unlikely to predate medial-h loss, favoring **h-loss < breaking**. §56 distinguishes the closed-syllable breaking environment from the h-loss discussion. Preserve the different event descriptions, environments and confidence levels; do not collapse them into one “h-change,” reverse Remark 1, or silently complete a global weakening/deletion chain (pp. 35, 37).

## 6. Absolute chronology

- **Island settlement:** late seventh/early eighth century in the historical narrative; early eighth century in the unrounding remark; “in the course of the eighth century” in the summary. Dates concern migration, then geographic reflexes are projected back to pre-departure changes. This requires the historical settlement inference plus inherited, rather than later separately developed, dialect reflexes; incomplete unrounding is explicitly allowed (pp. 6, 33, 41).
- **Runes around 500:** the author associates differentiation of inherited-a sounds with common English/Frisian rune variants. This dates his historical/orthographic reconstruction, not a separately demonstrated date for each phonological step; he leaves the place of rune invention unresolved (pp. 127–128). The general inscription range is c.500–800, not the dates of all innovations (p. 6).
- **Rüstring Christianization around 800:** **Ondreus** supports late treatment of a loan by nasal rounding. Conversion is not the exact date of borrowing or a terminus for the whole earlier inherited-vocabulary change (p. 25).
- **Attestation:** medieval manuscripts mostly give evidence several centuries later; East Frisian manuscripts c.1300–1450 and major West Frisian manuscripts c.1450–1525 are not synchronous dialect samples (pp. 6, 114). Late open-syllable lengthening belongs to the later Middle Ages in this account, without a precise year (pp. 115–116).

## 7. Proposed shared innovations

Bremmer lists nasal rounding/loss, long-ā fronting, ai > ā, short-a fronting, palatalization and breaking as phonological **parallels**, not a proven package of single common-stem innovations (p. 126). His earlier treatment places gemination in common WGmc and nasal developments/long-ā fronting in North Sea Germanic discussion, but explicitly separates palatalization and contrasts breaking (pp. 23–27, 31–35).

The shared rune differentiation is a common cultural innovation in representation of changed sounds; direction/place of invention remains unknown. It cannot turn different daughter phonologies into one ancestral phonological law (pp. 127–128).

## 8. Proposed independent developments

- **Palatalization:** explicitly separate, with OE **ċēap** versus OFris **kāp** from Latin *caupo*; medial environments differ too, as OFris **dīk, rīke** contrast with OE **dīċ, rīċe** (pp. 31–32).
- **Breaking:** English breaking precedes palatalization, Frisian breaking follows mutation. Bremmer treats English breaking as potentially the first unambiguously English change; Frisian has its own narrower velar-cluster process (pp. 33–35).
- **Retraction:** English fronted æ is retracted before a following back vowel; Frisian lacks that retraction. This is daughter-specific treatment of a potentially shared antecedent, not proof of independent initial fronting (p. 30).
- **Later “illness”:** independent contamination in West and Island North Frisian is a reported possibility, not Bremmer's demonstrated law (p. 35).
- **au:** Frisian unexceptional monophthongization to ā is distinct from English outcomes; this book does not supply an English *æu* intermediate or demonstrate its identity with short-a fronting (pp. 27–28, 31).

## 10. Tree-test implications

**CAPR premise:** strict tree and the Anglo-Frisian node are fixed. This review tests **contents and placement of innovations**, not whether the node exists.

- Shared nasal histories and fronting remain **compatible** with ancestral placement where no required daughter-only precursor is demonstrated. Wider North Sea Germanic distribution may instead locate a change above the Anglo-Frisian node; compatibility is not proof of either placement (pp. 23–27, 126–128).
- Under Bremmer's explicit **daughter-specific precursor < palatalization** premise, a single inherited shared palatalization is excluded. His *ċēap/kāp* comparison and medial-domain distinctions support separate daughter developments, but the book leaves the exact English precursor unnamed. Exclusion is conditional on that premise; it is **not exclusion of the Anglo-Frisian node** (pp. 31–32).
- Under his English-specific breaking and separate Frisian chronology/domain premises, the two breakings are daughter developments, not one inherited breaking event. The order reversal alone does not formally prove this without the daughter-placement premises; the ambiguous “as a phonemic change” clause does not independently settle the layer at which either change is ordered (pp. 33–36).
- **SC030/SC043:** the local identifiers refer respectively to English au brightening and plain-a brightening; they are mentioned only to route future research. Bremmer's Frisian **au > ā** and its chronology do not establish an English *au > æu* intermediate, shared au brightening, or the identity/simultaneity of that event with plain-a fronting. His plain-a conditioning and English-only retraction supply evidence for comparing formulations of SC043, **not a verdict on its current stage or implementation** (pp. 27–30). A difference in au outcomes cannot by itself disprove common plain-a fronting, and no new SC030/043 edge follows merely from treating both as “fronting” (pp. 27–33).
- Bremmer describes North Sea Germanic as a dialect continuum, and later trade, cultural identity and resistance to Frankish/Saxon influence contribute to his explanation (pp. 22, 128). Record this faithfully; it supplies **no tree-usable chronology edge or CAPR wave/diffusion escape hatch**. Selective retention of forms/words in his cultural account is not a Neogrammarian phonological conditioning rule (p. 128).

## 11. Uncertainty grading

- **Firmly demonstrated in this review:** the printed text distinguishes inherited long ā, short a and secondary ai/au products; expressly leaves ai conditioning unresolved; states separate palatalization; and contains both opposed ai-order passages. These are verified author claims, not a claim that the whole historical reconstruction is compelled (pp. 27–33).
- **Author's preferred reconstruction:** the §44 palatalization sequence, closed-syllable breaking account, and common Frisian changes projected from geographical distributions (pp. 31–36, 41). Within the assumed reconstructions, *siucht*, *Briocht* and *gerso* furnish actual interaction arguments, rather than headings alone (pp. 35, 40).
- **Plausible but non-unique inference:** pre-i-mutation versus pre-unrounding initial palatalization; migration-based termini; marginal “church” monophthongs as relics; reborrowed *pāl*; analogical medial variants (pp. 6, 27, 31–35).
- **Explicitly unresolved:** ai split; several rC-fronting exceptions; alternatives for “six” and “illness”; geographic origin of island settlers and of rune invention (pp. 6, 27–29, 34–35, 128). These are local limits, not grounds for blocking every other conclusion.
