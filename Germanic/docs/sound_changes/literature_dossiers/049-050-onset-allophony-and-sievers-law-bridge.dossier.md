## Historical problem

SC049-SC050 sits immediately before the already promoted SC051 and SC052 reports, so both changes need explicit prose while the assembled sound-change half stays strictly chronological. The problem is that the two rules do not read like a natural textbook chapter. SC049 is narrow and mostly allophonic: the live chronology evidence is compound-specific (`rainbow`), and the literature treats `/b/ ~ [beta]` as distributional phonology or orthographic reflex rather than as a large standalone OE sound law. SC050, by contrast, has real historical literature because Sievers-law reflexes matter for heavy/light stem alternation, weak verbs, and the lead-in to later palatal contexts.

That leaves SC049-SC050 looking more like a practical adjacent bridge than a historically unified pair. The eventual prose therefore has two jobs at once: state both ordinary FST changes explicitly, and avoid overclaiming unity, scope, or chronology where the cards do not justify it. The main chapter-shape question is whether this should later become one short adjacent bridge report or two even shorter implementation notes kept in chronological order.

## Source dossier

### Hogg 1992

- **source_key:** `HoggPhonology1992`
- **locator:** pp. 101-102
- **terminology:** `/b/` as stop vs. bilabial fricative allophone in Old English
- **short quotation:** "Elsewhere it was realised as the bilabial fricative [beta]."
- **paraphrase:** Hogg presents the alternation as part of the ordinary OE consonant system: `/b/` is a stop initially, after a nasal, and in gemination, but elsewhere surfaces as a voiced bilabial fricative [@HoggPhonology1992, p. 108].
- **conditioning:** initial position; post-nasal position; geminate position versus non-initial, non-geminate environments
- **chronology:** This is framed as system-internal OE distribution, not as a separately narrated late historical chapter.
- **examples:** `habban` and related stop/fricative alternations in the OE consonant inventory discussion
- **cautions:** Useful for mapping SC049 onto handbook phonology, but it does not by itself justify turning SC049 into a broad historical episode, and it says nothing about pairing SC049 with SC050.

### Ringe and Taylor 2014

- **source_key:** `RingeTaylor2014`
- **locator:** p. 121
- **terminology:** fricative and stop allophones of Proto-West-Germanic `*b` and `*g`
- **short quotation:** "`*b` and `*g` had fricative and stop allophones..."
- **paraphrase:** Ringe and Taylor explicitly treat `*b` and `*g` as having stop/fricative allophony in Proto-West Germanic, with stop realizations in initial position and after nasals and fricative realizations elsewhere [@RingeTaylor2014, p. 121].
- **conditioning:** initial and post-nasal stops versus non-initial fricatives
- **chronology:** This supports SC049 as inherited or continuing allophonic structure rather than as a large self-standing OE innovation.
- **examples:** general PWGmc voiced-obstruent distribution rather than a dedicated `rainbow` discussion
- **cautions:** The passage is typological and comparative; it supports the model's allophony but not a strong SC049 chapter tradition, and it does not support treating SC049 and SC050 as a natural pair.

### Luick 1914

- **source_key:** `Luick1914`
- **locator:** p. 107
- **terminology:** `labialen stimmhaften Reibelaut`
- **short quotation:** "der labialen stimmhaften Reibelaut"
- **paraphrase:** Luick notes that the voiced labial fricative can appear in OE spelling with `b` and later sometimes `f`, which is useful as orthographic corroboration for the fricative side of the `/b/` alternation [@Luick1914, p. 107].
- **conditioning:** chiefly medial or non-initial fricative environments as reflected in spelling practice
- **chronology:** Orthographic evidence helps show that the alternation was real in OE usage, but it does not provide a positive relative chronology beyond that.
- **examples:** manuscript spelling behavior for the voiced labial fricative
- **cautions:** This is supportive background for SC049, not evidence that SC049 is a large chronological hinge.

### Adamczyk 2001

- **source_key:** `Adamczyk2001`
- **locator:** article discussion of introductory chronology and weak-verb reflexes; article begins at p. 61
- **terminology:** Old English reflexes of Sievers' Law; heavy versus light stems; weak-verb evidence
- **short quotation:** "geminated verbs ... can be traced back to the non-syllabic alternant"
- **paraphrase:** Adamczyk argues that Old English preserves reflexes of Sievers' Law in both nominal/adjectival material and especially weak verbs, with geminated forms tied to one alternant and nongeminated forms to the other [@Adamczyk2001]. That makes SC050 legible as more than a local FST convenience.
- **conditioning:** heavy/light stem structure; consonant-plus-`j` environments; later weak-verb reflexes
- **chronology:** Adamczyk treats Sievers-law reflexes as early enough to feed later OE developments; that fits CAPR's use of SC050 before SC052 but does not require making SC050 part of the SC052 chapter.
- **examples:** `fremman`, `biddan`, `sellan`, `nerian`
- **cautions:** The article is about the wider reflex system, not about the single `stretch` witness on the chronology card, so the eventual report should keep the bridge modest and local.

### Fulk 2018

- **source_key:** `Fulk2018`
- **locator:** p. 28; section index at sec. 6.15
- **terminology:** West Germanic gemination and the weak-verb patterns relevant to Sievers-law reflexes
- **short quotation:** "Light stems ending in a consonant other than `r` show the effect of WGmc. gemination..."
- **paraphrase:** Fulk's comparative grammar offers the compact handbook cross-check that gemination and weak-verb shape interact differently in `biddan`, `fremman`, `sellan`, and `nerian`, which is the traditional territory in which Sievers-law reflexes are discussed [@Fulk2018, p. 28, sec. 6.15].
- **conditioning:** light versus heavy stems; consonant-final stems; gemination before `j`
- **chronology:** This backs the claim that SC050 belongs to real historical morphology/phonology, even if the chronology card's local positive evidence is only the later SC052 boundary.
- **examples:** `biddan`, `fremman`, `sellan`, `nerian`
- **cautions:** Fulk supports the general phenomenon, not a specific SC049-SC050 bridge or the exact CAPR rule wording.

## CAPR formulation versus literature

CAPR's `PGmcBAllophony` maps reasonably well onto the handbook description of `/b/` as stop initially or after nasals and as a fricative elsewhere. The live rule text is narrow: it maps `*b` to `*beta` after vowels and liquids, and then restores stop `*b` before geminate `*bb`. That is much closer to an implementation rule or phonetic-distribution bridge than to an independently narrated historical sound change. The literature helps justify the alternation itself [@HoggPhonology1992, p. 108; @RingeTaylor2014, p. 121; @Luick1914, p. 107], but it does not produce a strong source tradition for SC049 as a standalone chapter. In practice, SC049 currently looks like a model-internal bridge needed to preserve compound outcomes such as `rainbow`.

`SieversLawSyncope`, by contrast, maps onto a real source tradition. CAPR deletes `*i` before `*j` after a consonant or palatal consonant, in the same broad area where the literature discusses the alternation between light and heavy stems, gemination, and weak-verb reflexes [@Adamczyk2001; @Fulk2018, p. 28, sec. 6.15]. That makes SC050 historically more legible than SC049. Even so, the local CAPR role is still modest: the chronology card's live positive relation is outward, with SC050 feeding SC052 via `stretch`. The report therefore should discuss SC050 as context for SC052 without pulling it out of chronological order or turning SC050-SC052 into a non-contiguous chapter.

No source tradition found here argues for treating SC049 and SC050 together as a natural historical pair. The best current interpretation is that the grouping is practical and chronological: they are adjacent ordinary FST changes that both need explicit prose, and they sit just before the promoted palatalization reports, but their evidence and source traditions point in different directions.
