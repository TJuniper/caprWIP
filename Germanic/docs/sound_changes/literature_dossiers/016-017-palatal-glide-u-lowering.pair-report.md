## 1. Search coverage summary

The search was conducted in two layers:

1. a wide term sweep across `docs/references/` using the rule names, alternate descriptive terminology, and lexeme/reconstruction probes supplied in the task;
2. a separate sweep across `Germanic/docs/` and `Germanic/docs/sound_changes/` for CAPR-facing notes, chronology cards, and export metadata.

The result was asymmetrical but usable:

1. **SC016** produced several real witnesses, but mostly as discussions of West Saxon rising diphthongs, `geo-/gio-/iu-` spellings, and reflexes of initial `j`, not as a standard named sound change.
2. **SC017** produced a stronger and more standard literature trail, with repeated rule statements and broadly stable conditioning.
3. **Pair chronology** produced no explicit literature statement saying that SC016 must precede SC017. The local `yoke` boundary remains primarily a CAPR chronology result, though literature evidence clearly supports the phonological ingredients.

## 4. Strongest quotations

1. **SC016 / Campbell 1959:** "the graphs ea, eo, io are used not for the usual OE falling diphthongs, but for rising diphthongs, which were formed when palatal glides developed before back vowels ... geoc yoke"
2. **SC016 / Kaluza 1900:** "Urg. j im Anlaut = ae. g, ge, gi, i ... geoc, gioc Joch ... geong, giung, iung jung, geogud Jugend"
3. **SC017 / Campbell 1959:** "`u > o before mid and low vowels. In OE forms this change occurs with considerable regularity ... dohtor ... gold ... geoc ... coren, boren, holpen`"
4. **SC017 / Fulk 2018:** "when u stood before a mid or low vowel in the next syllable ... it was lowered to o"
5. **SC017 / Fulk 2018:** "This lowering is prevented before a tautosyllabic nasal consonant ... Lowering is prevented also when j preceded the non-high vowel conditioning the change"

## 5. Points of agreement

1. The `geoc / geong / geoguþ` cluster is a real and traditional object of discussion; SC016 is not invented ex nihilo.
2. The lowering of `u` to `o` before a following non-high vowel is a standard historical development and not merely a CAPR convenience.
3. `Geoc` is a shared bridge lexeme: it appears in the SC016-style discussions and in the SC017-style rule statements.
4. Nasal blocking is a major part of the SC017 story across the handbooks.

## 6. Points of disagreement or silence

1. **Terminology mismatch:** SC016 is rarely treated as a named sound change; the literature usually describes spellings, rising diphthongs, or initial-`j` reflexes instead.
2. **Variation within SC016:** older grammars stress competing forms such as `iung / giung / geong` and `iuc / gioc / geoc`, which makes the phenomenon real but not maximally neat.
3. **Variation within SC017:** Luick's `u/o` doublets show that lexical and analogical fluctuation complicate the simple rule.
4. **Chronology silence:** no productive source recovered here explicitly states the SC016-before-SC017 order or the SC017-before-SC019 order in CAPR's terms.

## 7. How well each dossier supports eventual book prose

1. **SC016:** partial support only. The literature supports the phenomenon, but not the exact CAPR stage name. Any prose chapter would need to say explicitly that the literature usually discusses West Saxon palatal-glide/rising-diphthong behavior rather than a standard sound law called "OE Ws Palatal Glide."
2. **SC017:** strong support. The literature gives repeated rule statements, useful quotations, clear conditioning, and enough caution material to keep the prose honest.

## 8. How should the pair be treated in the book?

The present evidence favors **a three-part early corridor with SC019**, not either of the narrower alternatives.

Reasons:

1. SC016 by itself is too narrow and too weakly literature-labeled to stand comfortably as a fully independent mini-chapter.
2. SC017 is stronger, but its right-hand chronology and several of its most narratable CAPR examples (`nose`, `shovel`, `sorrow`) immediately run into SC019.
3. The early corridor from SC016 to SC019 is where the real explanatory value lies: `yoke` on the left, `nose / shovel / sorrow` on the right.

So the best current book architecture is:

1. a short SC016-focused subsection on West Saxon palatal-glide/rising-diphthong behavior;
2. a larger SC017 subsection on standard Northwest Germanic `u`-lowering;
3. a linked SC019 subsection that completes the local corridor.

## 9. Must SC019 receive a literature dossier before any SC016/SC017 book prose is drafted?

Yes.

SC017's later computational boundary is already reciprocal with SC019 and is supported by a richer failure set than the SC016/SC017 side. Drafting book prose on SC016/SC017 alone would therefore leave the early corridor unfinished and risk over-weighting the `yoke` boundary simply because it is the left-hand reciprocal pair. A serious prose pass should wait until SC019 has the same literature discipline now given to SC016 and SC017.
