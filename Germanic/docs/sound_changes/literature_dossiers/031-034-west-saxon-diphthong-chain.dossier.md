## Historical problem

`SC031-SC034` is a plausible West Saxon diphthongal region, but the source
tradition does not automatically guarantee that it should become one unchanged
production chapter. The structural review already showed why the chronology
cards feel uneven: `SC031` and `SC034` form the strongest local reciprocal
core, while `SC032` and `SC033` are genuinely connected to the same region but
map less neatly onto the handbooks.

The source question is therefore not merely whether each individual change is
historically real. It is whether the handbooks describe:

1. one coherent West Saxon diphthong chain;
2. one strong `SC031`/`SC034` core plus additional bridge material;
3. or several partly overlapping phenomena that CAPR has segmented more finely
   than the traditional grammars do.

The resulting dossier needs to hold together four different kinds of evidence
without overstating any one of them:

1. local chronology-card constraints, especially the reciprocal `SC031`/`SC034`
   pair;
2. handbook descriptions of inherited and secondary long diphthongs;
3. handbook descriptions of palatal diphthongization and later smoothing or
   monophthongization;
4. CAPR-specific cautions, especially the no-output earlier failures for
   `SC032` and the supplementary-only expanded-PWGmc notes for `SC031` and
   `SC033`.

## Source dossier

### Campbell 1959

- source_key: Campbell1959
- locator: §120; §§132--136; §§170--176; §§185, 222--227
- terminology: West Germanic diphthongs; influence of initial palatal consonants; smoothing
- short quotation: "This process is termed smoothing."
- paraphrase: Campbell does not present one four-change "West Saxon diphthong chain" chapter. Instead he treats several connected developments: West Germanic diphthongs and `jj/ww` sequences, the OE outcomes of `ai`, `au`, and `eu`, the new diphthongs created by initial palatals, and later Anglian smoothing. That makes him very useful for the region as a whole, but also a warning against reading CAPR's four rules as four textbook law names. [@Campbell1959, §120; @Campbell1959, §§132--136; @Campbell1959, §§170--176; @Campbell1959, §§185, 222--227]
- conditioning: `ww` sequences yield forms such as `dēaw`, `hēawan`, and `scēawian`; inherited `ai` and `au` develop to OE `ā` and `ēa`; initial palatal consonants then create new diphthongs in forms such as `scieran`, `giefan`, `sceaft`, `sceap`, `ceaster`, and `geaf`; Anglian smoothing later reduces diphthongs before back consonants such as `c`, `g`, and `h`. [@Campbell1959, §120; @Campbell1959, §§135--136; @Campbell1959, §§170, 185, 222]
- chronology: Campbell's arrangement makes the strongest source-based case for the dossier's structural caution. The `ww` and inherited-diphthong material is earlier; palatal diphthongization is a distinct later zone; smoothing is later again and dialectal. That makes `SC031`/`SC034` historically natural as a core, but it leaves `SC032` as a later leveling or smoothing-side bridge rather than a plainly coequal chapter center. [@Campbell1959, §120; @Campbell1959, §§170--176, 185, 222--227]
- examples: `dēaw`; `hēawan`; `scēawian`; `dēaþ`; `ēage`; `fēh`; `hēh`; `weorc`; `scieran`; `giefan`; `sceaft`; `sceap`; `ceaster`; `geaf`. [@Campbell1959, §120; @Campbell1959, §§135, 170, 185, 222--225]
- cautions: Campbell is excellent evidence that the region is real, but he does not isolate `SC031`, `SC032`, `SC033`, and `SC034` as four separate historical laws. `SC032` especially looks more like a CAPR grouping over material that Campbell distributes across smoothing and later diphthong developments.

### Hogg 1992

- source_key: HoggPhonology1992
- locator: pp. 107--108; pp. 111--112
- terminology: palatalisation; palatal diphthongisation
- short quotation: "Even if one accepts ... the reality of palatal diphthongisation of front vowels, there is no need to accept that a parallel change affecting back vowels ... was ever anything more than an orthographic variation."
- paraphrase: Hogg is particularly useful for chapter weighting. He treats the palatal side of the region as real but uneven: initial `sk` and other palatals trigger diphthongized outputs such as `sceap`, but the exact status of some back-vowel spellings is much less secure. This helps explain why `SC034` looks historically stronger than a fully expanded four-change corridor and why `SC033` or `SC032` might later need subordinate treatment rather than equal chapter billing. [@HoggPhonology1992, p. 112; @HoggPhonology1992, pp. 112--113]
- conditioning: palatalization applies broadly to initial `sk`; following front vowels after palatals produce forms such as `giefan`, `giet`, `sceap`, and `ceaf`; the back-vowel side is more doubtful and sometimes may be orthographic rather than a full phonological rule. [@HoggPhonology1992, p. 112; @HoggPhonology1992, pp. 112--113]
- chronology: Hogg places palatalization before the palatal-diphthongization discussion and treats the latter as a separate, partly controversial process. That does not map directly to `SC031-SC034`, but it strongly supports the dossier's claim that the handbooks do not hand us one ready-made four-step chapter. [@HoggPhonology1992, p. 112; @HoggPhonology1992, pp. 112--113]
- examples: `waste`; `ascad`; `scip`; `disc`; `sceap`; `ceaf`; `giefan`; `giet`. [@HoggPhonology1992, p. 112; @HoggPhonology1992, pp. 112--113]
- cautions: Hogg is better for the palatal-diphthong side of the dossier than for `SC031`'s `ww` background or `SC032`'s leveling profile. He reinforces the idea that some parts of the region are strong and others less stable, but he does not by himself settle the final chapter shape.

### Ringe and Taylor 2014

- source_key: RingeTaylor2014
- locator: §6.1.2; §6.5.1; §6.10.1
- terminology: development of inherited diphthongs; West Saxon diphthongization by initial palatals; developments of the OE diphthongs
- short quotation: "After initial velars and *sk had been palatalized, any following stressed non-high front vowel was diphthongized in WS"
- paraphrase: Ringe and Taylor are especially useful because they split the region into historical layers very clearly. In one place they discuss inherited and early Germanic diphthong developments; later they treat West Saxon diphthongization by initial palatals; later still they discuss mergers and later developments of OE diphthongs by dialect. This is almost the inverse of a ready-made SC031-SC034 chapter: it is excellent evidence that the four CAPR rules live in one neighborhood, but also good evidence that the handbook tradition does not naturally package them as one single law-chain. [@RingeTaylor2014, §6.1.2; @RingeTaylor2014, §6.5.1; @RingeTaylor2014, §6.10.1]
- conditioning: inherited `ai` and `au` follow their own early development path; initial palatals later trigger West Saxon diphthongization in forms such as `ceaster`, `geaf`, and `scieran`; later OE diphthongs merge or vary by dialect. [@RingeTaylor2014, §6.1.2; @RingeTaylor2014, §6.5.1; @RingeTaylor2014, §6.10.1]
- chronology: Ringe and Taylor strongly support the idea that `SC034` has a real forward relation into the `SC043`/`SC044` brightening-breaking zone, but they also suggest that `SC033` and especially `SC032` may not belong in exactly the same narrative layer as the `SC031`/`SC034` core. [@RingeTaylor2014, §6.5.1; @RingeTaylor2014, §6.10.1]
- examples: `hlāf`; `ceaster`; `geaf`; `gēafon`; `scieran`; `gīe`; later diphthongal variation in `ie`, `eo`, and `io` by dialect. [@RingeTaylor2014, §6.1.2; @RingeTaylor2014, §6.5.1; @RingeTaylor2014, §6.10.1]
- cautions: Ringe and Taylor are best for architecture and chronology distinction, not for the exact CAPR witness sets `dew`, `hew`, `head`, `chew`, or `four`. The dossier should therefore use them as a framing source rather than force every card example into their prose.

### Luick 1914

- source_key: Luick1914
- locator: §§115--119; §§168--176; §§182--183; §195
- terminology: Aufhellung; ältere Diphthongierung durch Palatale; Palatalisierungsdiphthonge; i-Umlaut
- short quotation: "Der wichtigste Fall von palataler Beeinflussung ... war die Veränderung der urenglischen Vokale durch i oder j der Folgesilbe"
- paraphrase: Luick offers perhaps the best historical-grammar framing for the whole region. He explicitly treats the early English diphthong history as a set of connected but non-identical processes: the earlier brightening of `a`, the development of diphthongs with `a` as first component, the older palatal diphthongization, the "second brightening," and finally i-umlaut and the umlaut of palatalization diphthongs. This makes him an excellent witness for the region's reality and an equally strong witness that its internal segmentation is historically complicated. [@Luick1914, §§115--119; @Luick1914, §§168--176; @Luick1914, §§182--183; @Luick1914, §195]
- conditioning: Luick explicitly connects `ww` and related sequences to forms such as `deaw`, `glēaw`, `hēawan`, and `scēawian`; he then treats the later diphthongization caused by initial palatals separately and also notes that these palatalization diphthongs themselves participate in later umlaut developments. [@Luick1914, §§115--119; @Luick1914, §§168--176; @Luick1914, §195]
- chronology: Luick is especially valuable for the dossier's open questions. He states that palatal diphthongization is younger than breaking and older than a later brightening process, while i-umlaut is one of the broadest later palatal influences. That means SC033's broad right relation to `SC044` is historically intelligible, but it also means the whole region cannot be flattened into one simple chronological sentence. [@Luick1914, §§176, 181--183, 195]
- examples: `deaw`; `glēaw`; `hēawan`; `scēawian`; `ziest`; `scieppan`; `zincra`; related umlauted palatal-diphthong forms. [@Luick1914, §§115--119; @Luick1914, §§171--172; @Luick1914, §195]
- cautions: Luick is highly useful, but some of his evidence comes from broad historical framing rather than from exactly the same micro-questions CAPR asks. He supports the dossier's caution that `SC032` and `SC033` may need subordinate or residual treatment even if the region remains one scaffold for now.

### Fulk 2018

- source_key: Fulk2018
- locator: §4.7; §4.13
- terminology: front mutation; diphthongization by initial palatal consonants; smoothing
- short quotation: "diphthongization by initial palatal consonants (which precedes front umlaut but not breaking)"
- paraphrase: Fulk is especially useful because he compresses the later part of the region into a few clear chapter-level cautions. He treats diphthongization after initial palatals as a real Old English process, but one that stands in a specific relation to breaking and front mutation, and he also notes later smoothing in the Anglian dialects before palatal `c`, `g`, and `h`. This is exactly the kind of comparative framing the dossier needs for `SC032` and `SC033`: they correspond to real handbook material, but not to one neatly named standalone law. [@Fulk2018, §4.7; @Fulk2018, §4.13]
- conditioning: initial palatal consonants trigger forms such as `ceaster`, `geaf`, `giefan`, and `giest`; later Anglian smoothing yields outcomes such as `werc` beside West Saxon `weorc`, with similar monophthongization in other diphthongal environments. [@Fulk2018, §4.7; @Fulk2018, §4.13]
- chronology: Fulk's sequencing is valuable because it prevents overreading. Palatal diphthongization precedes front umlaut but not breaking; smoothing is later and dialectal. That supports using `SC034` as a forward link into the already promoted `SC043` brightening chapter, while cautioning that `SC032` should not be narrated as if its no-output card boundary were itself a classical handbook chronology claim. [@Fulk2018, §4.7; @Fulk2018, §4.13]
- examples: `giest`; `be-sciered`; `ceaster`; `geaf`; `weorc` / `werc`; `fēol` / `fīl`. [@Fulk2018, §4.7; @Fulk2018, §4.13]
- cautions: Fulk is concise and highly useful for orientation, but less detailed than Campbell or Luick on the `ww` and inherited-diphthong side. He sharpens the source tradition's internal chronology rather than directly endorsing CAPR's present four-rule segmentation.

## CAPR formulation versus literature

The strongest alignment between CAPR and the handbooks lies in the claim that
`SC031-SC034` really is a diphthong-heavy region. The weakest alignment lies in
the claim that the handbooks already divide that region into the same four units.

The sources most naturally support the following mapping:

1. `SC031` and `SC034` together correspond to a historically natural
   development zone involving `ww`-based sequences and `ēaw`-type outcomes.
   The handbooks usually discuss those developments as one connected history,
   not as two independently named textbook rules.
2. `SC033` corresponds more loosely to the inherited/long-diphthong side of
   the region. It is real enough, but its right edge points into the later
   brightening/breaking neighborhood rather than back into a tight local pair.
3. `SC032` corresponds least neatly to a single handbook law. The source
   tradition discusses smoothing, later monophthongization, diphthongal
   regularization, and later analogical leveling in a distributed way. That
   makes CAPR's `OEDiphthongLeveling` analytically useful, but more
   model-internal than the other three rules.

That leads to five concrete dossier cautions:

1. `SC031`/`SC034` is historically natural enough to remain a serious future
   subchapter candidate.
2. The whole `SC031-SC034` corridor is still historically real as a region.
3. `SC032`'s earlier failures must be described as failed derivations or
   no-output results, not as ordinary alternate surface forms.
4. The expanded-PWGmc notes on `SC031` and `SC033` remain supplementary only;
   the default bundled-profile cards still define the ordinary chronology claims.
5. The already promoted `SC043` brightening report should be treated as the
   right-hand contextual anchor for `SC034`, not as evidence that the whole
   four-change corridor must remain unchanged.

## Chronology implications

The literature and the chronology cards align best when the region is described
as a corridor with one strong local core and two structurally weaker flanks.

1. `SC031 < SC034`, and `SC034`'s earlier side across `SC031`, is the strongest
   local reciprocal relation. `dew` and `hew` are historically natural witnesses
   because the handbook tradition already treats `ww`-based developments such as
   `dēaw` and `hēawan` as a connected history.
2. `SC032` really does belong in the same general diphthongal zone: it follows
   `SC030` and precedes `SC040` in the present model. But the source tradition
   does not hand us one neat textbook law called "diphthong leveling" that
   corresponds exactly to the card.
3. `SC033 < SC044` is historically intelligible, but broad/far. The source
   tradition's treatment of inherited and later-diphthong developments helps
   explain why `SC033` might eventually need cross-reference to the later
   breaking context without being moved there outright.
4. `SC034 < SC043` gives the region a genuine forward link into the already
   promoted brightening report. That supports dossiering the region now, but it
   does not decide whether the eventual production chapter should remain a
   four-change unit.
5. The unresolved architectural question remains real: whole-chain report
   versus narrower `SC031`/`SC034` report with subordinate or residual
   treatment for `SC032` and `SC033`.
