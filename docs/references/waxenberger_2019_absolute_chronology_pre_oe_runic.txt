         This article presents an absolute chronology of the early major sound
         changes reflected in the Pre-Old English (Pre-OE) runic inscriptions. These
         inscriptions were written in the Pre-fuþorc, which was still close to the
         Common Germanic fuþark (= Older fuþark) of 24 characters but was
         extended and modified during the Pre-OE period (ca. AD 425–610/650).
         The Caistor-by-Norwich Brooch (ca. AD 610–650) marks the beginning of
         Old English (OE) because the new and modified runes – ᚩ o, ᚪ a, ᛟ œ, ᚨ æ –
         with their new phonemic sound-values appear together for the first time.
         All the relevant sound-changes were completed by then. The Pre-OE and
         also the Pre-Old Frisian (Pre-OFris.) data reveal four phases of develop-
         ment. In my proposed chronology phases one and four are straightforward,
         while phases two and three are more complex, with two scenarios being
         possible.

2.2 The Pre-OFrisRC: Ca. AD 5th Century to AD 650

Inscriptions of the period ca. AD 5th c.–900 show what are called the diagnostic
Anglo-Frisian runes: the new runes ᚩ o and ᚪ a and also the rune ᚫ with the new

the fifth cent. and AD 650, the new diagnostic rune ᚩ o is only attested in the later

     3.   Methodological preliminaries

     3.1 The ‘Anglo-Frisian unity’

     Since the academic discussion on the question of whether or not there was ever
     an ‘Anglo-Frisian unity’ has been rekindled (see Nielsen 1994 for an overview and
     also Hines 2017), it seemed methodologically justified to build on the findings for
     the Pre-OERC and see to what extent the so-called Frisian corpus shows congru-
     ence with it. To this end, I used the sound-changes of Pre-OE as a framework and
     examined how the Frisian runes and the sound-values they represent fit into that
     frame (see Waxenberger 2017). For the purpose at hand I only took the inscrip-
     tions into account which are relevant for the early sound changes.


     3.2 Find-Spot vs. provenance

     Three of the four relevant inscriptions of the Pre-OFrisRC were not found in
     the modern-day provinces of Friesland and Groningen in the Netherlands, but in
     England (the skanomodu solidus), in Belgium (the Amay Bone Comb) and Ost-
     friesland, Germany (the Schweindorf solidus; Looijenga 2003: 317, 325).13 However,
     the uncertainty of their Pre-OFris. provenance (see also 2.2) is not relevant with
     regard to the absolute chronology of the early sound changes. The relevant ques-
     tion here is not where but when the sound changes took place.

4.     The sound-changes and their reflections in Pre-OE

Because of the sound changes discussed below, the grapheme inventory of the
Common Gmc fuþark had to be enlarged and modified. The new runes ōs ᚩ /ɔ̃:/
and āc ᚪ were added while the old rune ansuz ᚨfuþark adapted the sound value /æ(:)/
and changed its position (no. 4 to no. 26: see Figures 1 and 3). Rune ōþil ᛟ (no. 24)
was affected by i-umlaut and became /œ(:)/ but stayed in slot no. 24 (see Figure 3).
This intermediary phase marks the transition of the Common Gmc fuþark to the
OE fuþorc. Complex processes must have been at work until the OE vowel system
was established and the runes reshuffled. By the middle of the seventh century,
however, the runic data reveals two new (ᚩ = /o(:)/, ᚪ = /a(:)/) and two reassigned
(ᚨ = /æ(:)/, ᛟ = /œ(:)/) runes.

5.     The absolute chronology of the early sound changes: Two scenarios

Returning to the long vowels: as I have pointed out in 4.1.2, the lack of certainty
regarding the date of the monophthongization of Gmc */aɪ/ is the reason for
my two scenarios. Phase no. 1, the emergence of the new phoneme /ɔ̃:/ rendered
by the new rune ᚩ is the starting point in both scenarios (see Table 5 no. 1). If
the monophthongization of Gmc */aɪ/ took place ca. AD 500 (Bammesberger
1996: 21), two different long ā’s should then be posited for the period ca. AD
500–575/610 (= skanomodu solidus and the earliest date given for the Caistor-
by-Norwich Brooch): these are the Pre-OE /a:/1 < WGmc /a:/ and Pre-OE /a:/2
(< Gmc */aɪ/: see Table 5, nos. 2a, 2b). While the old rune ᚫfuþark was still used to
denote Pre-OE /a:/1 (and also for Pre-OE /a/), the new rune āc ᚪ was created for
Pre-OE /a:/2 (see Table 5, no. 2a). Things changed ca. AD 575–610. I would like
to point out that the first date (ca. AD 575) marks the earliest point in the date
range of the skanomodu solidus, ca. AD 610 marking both the latest point in time
for this solidus and the earliest date of the date range for the Caistor-by-Norwich
Brooch. I do not consider the latest date for the Caistor-by-Norwich Brooch (= ca.
AD 650), because the appearance of Pre-OE short /a/ in an unstressed position

     6.    Summary and conclusion

     I have attempted to shed more light on the sequence of early sound changes and
     also on the time when the individual sound changes may have happened. A lack
     of runic data for the time of the monophthongization of Gmc */aɪ/ makes two
     scenarios possible, which differ in Phases nos. 2 and 3. As can be inferred from
     Table 5, Bammesberger’s suggestion that the monophthongization occurred ca.
     AD 500 (= Scenario 1) is possible. However, this early date calls for the assump-
     tion that two long ā’s of different qualities existed side by side until they partially
     merged, the result from that point onwards being exclusively represented by the
     new rune āc ᚪ a. On the other hand, late monophthongization of Gmc */aɪ/
     (shortly prior to AD 575/610 = Scenario 2) would mean a development with tem-
     porarily closely interlinked sound changes. This seems more probable to me.
         However, one thing can be said in favour of both scenarios. They suggest
     that, at least for the Pre-OE side, the phonemicization of Pre-OE /a/ (< Gmc
     */a/) happened, if not parallel to, then at least immediately after the restructuring
     processes of the Pre-OE reflexes of WGmc /a:/ (see Table 5 and Waxenberger
     forthc: chapter 3). This is an important observation with regard to the principle
     of sound changes: when the first change was carried through, the whole system
     began to move, and once set in motion, the individual changes within this chain
     reaction followed within a relatively short time aiming at quickly overcoming the
     temporary imbalance of the system and returning to a stable but partially modi-
     fied system.20
