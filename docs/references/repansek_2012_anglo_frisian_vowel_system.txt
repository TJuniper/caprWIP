ENCODING REPAIR
---------------
The embedded text layer was NOT reliable as extracted. The John Benjamins
house font emits a combining macron as a trailing U+00A4 CURRENCY SIGN, so
every long vowel in the raw extract came out as e.g. "o¤" for "ō". In an
article about vowel history that is a loss of exactly the content-bearing
information, so it was repaired before committing.

The repair is deterministic and context-free — a fixed one-to-one glyph
substitution followed by NFC composition — not a guess or a reconstruction:

  U+00A4 after a vowel   ->  combining macron U+0304 on that vowel

It was verified against forms whose value is fixed by the surrounding prose,
so the check is independent of the substitution itself: `ǣ`, `ō`, `[ʌ]` (printed with a wedge glyph, read independently as
`√` by pdftotext and `^` by Vision — two different renderings of the same
wedge, agreeing on the shape).

         REMARKS ON THE DEVELOPMENT OF
          'ANGLO-FRISIAN' VOWEL SYSTEM



The remarkable degree of similarity between the phonological devel-
opments that characterize Old Frisian and Old English has long been
recognized as indirect/secondary rather than as the result of a period
of common development undergone by the respective linguistic sys-
tems. The older view, which put forward the idea of the existence of
an 'Anglo-Frisian' mother dialect within West Germanic, from which
Old Frisian and Old English supposedly branched out, has convinc-
ingly been contested by numerous authors.1 In recent discussions, a
discernible trend towards minimizing the number of similarities of
indisputably common provenance can be followed. The most effective
methods adopted to achieve this minimizaton involve a comparison of
the relative chronologies arrived at by internal reconstruction and a
reassessment of the conditioned sound changes according to typology
and the probability of universal occurrence. Regarding the first method,
no phonological development transgressing the line that terminates
the exactly parallel chronological ordering of the respective sound
changes can be said to be concomitant (Stiles 1995:181). In essence,
the chronological criterion eliminates change Y from the list of parallel
developments if in language A change X is older than Y; the reverse is
true of language B. With respect to typology, it must be borne in mind
that the typological universality of a sound change in itself cannot be
a stringent and objective criterion in deciding whether we are dealing
with a common or parallel development. Rather, an additional range
of eligibility criteria for elimination of analogues should be applied,
such as:

–    Sporadicalness (vs regularity), which encompasses diffuse or ir-
     regular occurrence of a feature within the linguistic system itself
     and/or its limitation to a smaller linguistic area;
–    Differences in the conditioning environment and/or the input;
–    Lack of occurrence in the same set of lexemes;2

The aim of this discussion is not to restate the obvious by enumerating
the most commonly adduced similarities between the developments
characterizing the phonological systems of Old English and Old Frisian;
rather it attempts to track down the source of these similarities, as it
uncovers the genesis of the vowel systems of both languages. Three
crucial points on the relative chronological ordering of the main de-
velopments must be restated, however: (1) The realization of PGmc.
*ē1 as *ǣ (e.g. OE sċēap < *skǣp, OFr. skēp < PGmc. *skē1pan 'sheep')
can safely be assumed to have occurred before the monophthongiza-
tion product /ā/ arose (e.g. OE stān), since the absence of *ā in the
inherited long vowel system is partly responsible for the developments
giving rise to the former sound change (additional support is provided
by Frisian, where the ǣ-product of monophthongization of *ai must
have occurred after PGmc. *ē1 > *ǣ, because the former /æ̅ / fails to
cause palatalization (e.g. *gēr 'spear', kēi 'key')). (2) On the evidence of
Old Frisian, it needs to be concluded that the processes affecting *ă <
PGmc. *ă and the *ă in biphonemic sequences were unconnected, i.e.
fronting of short *a did not affect the a-phoneme in back diphthongs.5
If the reverse were the case, it would be impossible for us to explain the
lack of palatalization before Proto-Frisian /æ̅ / < *ai. (3) Developments
which, chronologically, must occur after fronting of short *a display
divergent behaviour, even within Old English itself. This last point sets
fronting of *a > *æ as the upper most boundary beyond which there is
no possibility of shared developments. If differences in the extent to
which *a was fronted are anything to go by,6 then the terminus may
be lowered and set immediately after *ē1 > *ǣ, but, importantly, there
is no methodology available that would allow for further reduction in
the set of possible common developments. Consequently, the following
five call for consideration:

(1)   Short *a, *i, *u followed by a sequence of a nasal and a voiceless
      dental sibilant *s or a homorganic voiceless fricative *f, *þ, *≈
      yield long /ō/, /ī/, /ū/ followed by /s/, /f/, /þ/, /≈/;
(2)   Short *a in these sequences comes out consistently as /ō/ (e.g.
      PGmc. *gans- 'goose' > OE, OFr. gōs);

With regard to the accented short vowel system, it is probable that
the allophonic split undergone by the inherited short *a vowel and
*ē1 came about at roughly the same time. This extrapolation forces
us to incorporate the early development in the *a vowel as a part
of the innovatory change that applied to both open vowels, i.e. *ē1
(at that time the only existing long low vowel) and *a, in front of
a following nasal. However, in the case of short *a, its allophonic
realization [ɒ]13 failed to meet the conditions for phonemicization.
Fronting of *a must in turn be seen as the result of imitation, since
through this secondarily achieved split, the short vowel system came
to reflect fully the developments that had taken place in its long
counterpart.14 Its secondary nature is proved by the fact that front-
ing of *a before [+ nasal] was not unconditional as the contrast was
neutralized (to varying extents) in a distinctly velar environment
and/or if a back vowel followed in the next syllable in favour of the
non-front variant.
  These developments are neatly reflected in the impetus behind the
creation of the new Anglo-Frisian ) rune (ōs), which was doubtlessly
invented because a new phoneme, viz. the merger product of *ā and
*ē1N, had entered the vowel inventory, cf. RunFr. C)bu 'a comb', prob-
ably for [kɒmbu] < *kambaz. However, since the majority of cases with
old *a remained unaffected by the change, the acrophonic principle
was not followed (in the reverse case, the ōs rune would remain to be
written with the old rune-character a) and the old *ansuz rune was
renamed, apparently to *ask(az), which, after the split undergone by
the short inherited a-vowel to match the developments in the long
vowel system, rendered *æsk(az), the new æ-value being attached to
the old a (æsc) rune. These modifications in turn eventually required
the making of a new rune with a-value, viz. 0 (*ak-), i.e. the later ā c
rune (Stiles 1995:185-86).
  As argued above, the dialects that later converged to form Primitive
English must have left the continent after this point in the phonological
developments at the latest; but, importantly, the processes of brighten-
ing15 and fronting could equally well have taken place independently
(after or even before the exodus), seeing that they were systemically
conditioned. Brightening therefore cannot be a reliable terminus a quo
for Anglo-Saxon migration.

   As regards the fact that in Frisian both back diphthongs were
monophthongized, the view adhered to here is that Frisian was caught
up in a wider wave of monophthongizations of *ai and *au, which
also affected Old Saxon and Old Low Franconian (cf. Hofmann 1995).
However, the monophthongization products in Frisian do not match
those in OS and OLF. This is easily explained if we consider the fact
that in OS and OLF the vowel systems were already symmetrical
from the start, which renders the monophthongization process un-
necessary, phonologically speaking. The existing systems reacted to
such an unmotivated sound change accordingly: in OS and OLF the
new monophthongs were utilized to fill the available gaps in the mid/
low front-back series respectively, whereas the systemic pressure in
Frisian must have been strong enough to direct the sound change to
its advantage. Monophthongization can therefore be understood as a
welcome process, which the Proto-Frisian long vowel system was able
to use in the most economic manner possible, i.e. to provide for the
missing /ā/ vowel in the long vowel series. Crucial for further significant
changes affecting the Proto-Frisian long vowel system, however, was
the twofold outcome of monophthongization of *ai (i.e. /ǣ/ ~ /ā/), which
alternates without any so far convincingly established regularity17
(stēn 'stone', ēre 'honour', rāp 'rope', tāne 'toe' etc.). Such unpredictable
patterns in occurrence of one or the other outcome of a single sound
change are typical of transition areas.18 It is very likely, indeed, that
at the time of the operation of the process, the Frisian linguistic ter-
ritory was intersected by two competing monophthongization waves,
so the more natural outcome of the monophthongization of *ai, viz.
/ǣ/, disturbed the realization of the desired, i.e. internally motivated
result. The way for /ǣ/ to penetrate the system could have been paved
by the simultaneous entrance of /ǣ/ from i-umlauted *ai into the long
vowel inventory.19 There was no such model in the case of *au, which
was then free to yield a single outcome.20 It should be borne in mind,
however, that isoglosses seldom run in a parallel fashion, so in the
case of *au the course of the monophthongization wave might not have
matched that of *ai.21

The addition of the new phoneme(s) to the long vowel inventory of
English and Frisian, respectively, resulted in an underdifferentiated
system with overcrowding in the open non-front series in English and
on both sides of the open series in Frisian. This quite naturally trig-

both through the intermediate stages of /ɔ/ and /ɛ̄/,22 and eventually
coalesced them with the old /ō/ < *ō 23 and /ē/ < *ē2 respectively. The
latter development, however, is exclusively Frisian and it appears to
postdate the Proto-Frisian period on the evidence of Old Insular North
Frisian, which coalesced the old and the new /ǣ/ (< *ē1, *ai) as opposed
to West and East Frisian, where the way for the new low front vowel
to step in was cleared24 (Hofmann 1964:172,185). Naturally, this dif-
ference calls for a post Proto-Frisian date for monophthongization,
because the onset of chain-induced movements must be simultaneous
with the entrance of the trigger into the system. However, such a chain
of events does not tally with the lexical distribution of the /ā/ and /ǣ/
reflexes (< *ai ) in Insular North Frisian, which matches the state of
affairs in West and East Frisian and secures the Proto-Frisian date
for monophthongization. This contradiction is easily resolved if we
ascribe the differences to early divergence within the Frisian linguis-
tic territory, which at the time was still more or less homogeneous.
Supra-dialectal areal features (in this case Pan- or Proto-Frisian) can
be concurrent with early diversification.

rect, it is fully impossible to connect close-mid front Anglian /ē/ < *ē1
with the developments that took place in Frisian, because at this stage

Conclusion

The aim of this discussion is to expose the set of phenomena that shaped
the vowel systems of Proto-Frisian and Primitive English to an external
reconstruction and strip it of all developments that could be rendered
redundant in order to establish the creation of an identical state of
affairs. Early areal features that marked the ancestral dialectal base
of both linguistic systems must be efficiently reduced to no more than
two innovations. These two peculiarities, however, were sufficiently
cataclysmic to set off a wave of structurally motivated sound changes
directed by the principle of phonological symmetry. Arguably, the
course of all developments subsequent to the split undergone by the
inherited *ē1 vowel was governed by exactly the same inner-systemic
pressure in both linguistic systems, which renders any contact between
Frisian and English after this point unnecessary.

    date (see below) as they include each and every kind of long /ā/, not just
    /ā/ < *au.

20. Of course, this scenario presupposes the existence of a rounded open-mid
    front vowel as the result of i-umlaut of *au in Frisian prior to unrounding
    to /ē/ (*baugijan > *bǣγ'an > bēia 'bend' = OE bīeġan), exactly parallel
    to nonWS (cf. bēcnan 'beckon' < *bauknijan). As already pointed out by
    Krupatkin (1970:61), the creation of WS /ie̅/ is again systemically moti-
    vated due to the coalescence of /io̅/ and /eo̅/, which created a gap in the
    front-to-back series of diphthongs.
21. Denasalization of Proto-Slavic *ǭ, for example, parallel to that of *ę̄ (cf.
    n. 18), is nearly perpendicular to the former isogloss, so the East Kras
    dialects have /ie/ < *ę̄ but monophthongal /uː/ < *ǭ, while West Kras
    dialects have /iːə/ (< *ie) ~ /aː/ and /uːə/ (< *uo) in the South and /iːə/
    (< *ie), /uːə/ (< *uo) in the centre.
22. At the time of i-umalut, Primitive English /ɒ̄/ was caught half-way to /ō/,
    which can be demonstrated by the æ-spelling in bræm(b)el indicating an
    /ǣ/ (< i-umlauted /ɔ/) rather than an /ø / (< i-umlauted /ō/) vowel (Hogg
    1992:126-27).
23. After the completion of this process, symmetry was disrupted again,
    which called for supplementary readjustments. In Insular North and in
    Ems East Frisian, symmetry was restored by rounding the /ā/ vowel to
    provide a symmetrically opposed pair to its front counterpart (Årham-
    mar 2001:748, Versloot 2001:768-69), which matches the developments
    in Middle English dialects South of the Humber, with the important
    difference, however, that the long /ā/ < short OE /a/ by lengthening was
    not involved in the change and must therefore be seen as chronologically
    later. However, it was a welcome addition to the system suddenly devoid
    of the long /ā/ vowel.
    In West Frisian, on the other hand, new long counterparts of the rounded
    open back vowel and /a/ by later lengthening caused the old /ā/ < *ai, *au
    to front and the old open front vowel to rise in a two-part push chain
    (Hoekstra 2001:722). An analogous development is found in ME dialects
    North of the Humber; however, in this case the new long /ā/ did not oc-
    cupy the open central position but was treated as old /ā/ (MEG §51). The
    prevailing difference between the two systems is, of course, that at the
    time of these changes Anglian possessed no open front vowel, so that
    fronting of /ā/ in this dialect can only be convincingly accounted for if we
    assume that after the reappearance of a rounded open back vowel (by
    lengthening), /ā/ was fronted to provide its counterpart.
    Weser East Frisian coalesced /ǣ/ with /ā/ < *ē2, *ē1 (Versloot 2001:737),
    hereby creating a three-level vowel system.
24. I.e. /ǣ/ (< *ē1 ) ⇒ /ē/; the old /ē/ < *ē2 can either stay in position to coalesce
    with it (as in Old West Frisian and the greater part of Old Insular North
    Frisian), or react by complementing the push chain, i.e. /ē/ ⇒ /ī/ (as is the
    case in Riustring Frisian).
