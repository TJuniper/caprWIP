GERMANIC AI AND AU IN ANGLO-FRISIAN*
by Kurt Gustav Goblirsch - Minneapolis
The development of ai and au in Anglo-Frisian runs counter to the Germanic trend
of monophthongization to the front and back mid vowels ē and ō; in addition it is
not uniform within itself: ai is monophthongized to ā, which sometimes appears as
ē in Frisian, whereas au is maintained as the diphthong ea in Old English and
monophthongized to ā in Old Frisian. Even if it is true that the Anglo-Frisian
system of long vowels motivated the changes ai > ā and au > ēa in Old English, we
are left with the question of au > ā in Old Frisian. Only the idea of stress shift in ea
(ēa > eā > ā) can account for the difference in the development of au in Old Frisian
and Old English. The change ai > ā has been considered common Anglo-Frisian, but
the development au > ēa > ā allows us to view both developments ai > ā and au > ēa
as points of structural unity between Old English and Old Frisian: ēa and ā seem to
have been prosodic variants of the same diphthong (au).
In explaining the development of the falling Germanic diphthongs ai
and au in Old English and Old Frisian, difficulties arise not only when
the problem is put against a Germanic background, but also when one
posits an Anglo-Frisian unity. In Germanic the general trend is to
smoothing of the elements resulting in mid vowels: ē when the second
element is front and ō when the second element is back. This
development is carried out in Low German and East Norse. In these
two contiguous groups of dialects the change may have spread from
one central point of origin. In the northwestern and southern areas the
trend is less strong and less uniform: monophthongization is restricted
to certain positions, while in the majority of cases the first element has
been partially assimilated to the second. In the latter instance the results
are written ei, ou in Old High German (OHG) and ei (œi), au (ou) in
West Norse. Discounting Gothic (Go.), which was isolated by the
Gothic migration and may also have monophthongized ai > ē and au >
ō, only Anglo-Frisian ran counter to the Germanic trend. Old English
(OE) monophthongized ai > ā and maintained au as the diphthong ēa.
Old Frisian (OF) shows not only ā < ai but also ē < ai and the
monophthongization of au > ā. I will attempt to explain this discrepancy
in the diphthongs, arguing Anglo-Frisian unity anew, and investigate
the special development.
In Frisian the normal reflex of au is ā as in āge (Go. augo) 'eye', dād
(Go. daups) 'dead', except in umlaut, when the result is ē as in dēpa
(Go. daupjan) 'baptize', hēra (Go. hausjan) 'hear'. The normal
development of ai is also to ā as in twā (Go. twai) 'two', māra (Go.
maiza) 'more, greater', and to short a before consonantclusters as in
askia (OHG eiscōn) 'demand, ask'. Frisian ē from ai appears not only

There has been much debate over the rules governing the distribution
of ā, ē < ai in Old Frisian, but no consensus has been reached (van
Helten 1890, 21-25; 1897; 1906; Bremer 1890; Walde 1901; Heinertz
1912; Armborst 1977; Nielsen 1983). It is possible, however, that the
change ai > ē is due to the influence of neighboring dialects. The
regular reflexes of Germanic (Gmc.) ai, au in Old Saxon are ē, ō, as in
twē 'two', bēn 'leg', ōga 'eye', dōd 'dead'. Saxon influence could
account for the e-variants in Old Frisian not paralleled by Old English,
allowing us to posit ai > ā for Anglo-Frisian (Karstien 1929; Miedema
1974, 124-25; cf. Morsbach 1897). But the Old Saxon transmission
itself is not uniform in this regard. Aside from 'Heliand' and 'Genesis'
the Old Saxon monuments favor ā as the representative of both Gmc. ai
and au, as does Frisian. In Old Saxon and Old Frisian ē, as well as the
ō-variants in Saxon may be due to Franconian influence (Heeroma
1965, 17; Markey 1981, 20-21). If one assumes the influence of Old
Saxon or Old Franconian on Frisian, the development of ai > ē in
Frisian finds an explanation.
Old English also shows ai > ā as in āgan (Go. aigan) 'to own', twā
(Go. twai) 'two'. It cannot be ascertained whether this reflex is
ascribable to smoothing (Sweet 1888, 22) or to a more complicated
process of fronting, dissimilation and regressive assimilation (ai > æe >
æ > æa > ā; Luick 1921, 133). In any case Frisian seems to have
shared the same development. But we can perhaps reconstruct the
details of the change au > ēa as in ēage (Go. augo) 'eye', dēad (Go.
daups) 'dead'. The evolution of au > ēa, isolated in Germanic, could be
the result of the Anglo-Frisian fronting of ā > ǣ and lowering of the
second element. This lowering is paralleled to some extent by the
development of Gmc. eu > ēo. Sometimes the stages are repeated in a
different order, but the basic process is: au > ao > æo > æa (cf. Luick
1921, 130-31). The change of æ > e evident in later orthography may
not reflect any phonetic change.
Various attempts have been made to explain the divergent
developments of ai and au within Anglo-Frisian and its conditions that
are at variance with the rest of Germanic. Siebs (1889, 255-56, 274)
viewed the Frisian transmission as being closer to the Anglo-Frisian
state and posited ai > æ, au > ā for the common development.
Subsequently, he thought, Frisian shifted æ > ē in certain cases (see
above) and > a otherwise, and English shifted a > ā. For au, he
thought, Frisian maintained ā in every case, while English shifted ā>
ēa. Campbell (1939, 89-91), on the other hand, believed that relative
chronology would explain the divergent history of the two languages.
He posited the shift of ā > ǣ before ai > ā in English, a > æ after it,
while a > æ took place before ai > æi > ē in Frisian. He thus allowed

ā < au are left unreconciled.
It has also been proposed, however, that au was a monophthong in
Old English. This view reflects a certain understanding of the OE short
and long diphthongs, which were the reflexes of breaking and velar
umlaut. Hockett (1959; cf. Antonsen 1967) asserted that in the
Vespasian glosses (mid ninth century) ea, eo, io represented long and
short phonemes, distinct from the front vowels /æ/, /e/, /i/. He thought
that just as the digraphs ae, oe, ui had been used to designate umlauts,
so too had the spellings ea, eo, io represented both lip and tongue
position, consecutively, in the unrounded back vowels /a/, //, //. These
vowels would then merely have been retracted rather than
diphthongized. Hockett also believed that the same symbols were used
for corresponding long vowels from the WGmc. diphthongs in words
such as dead 'dead', deop 'deep', liode 'people', which he presumed to
be monophthongized at the time of the Vespasian documents or not
long thereafter. According to this reconstruction, the diphthongs ea, eo,
io, phonetically [aw], [ew], [iw] in Pre-Old English, were affected by
a sort of internal velar umlaut when the first element of each diphthong
was retracted before the second element [w], which then became
unrounded and allowed the monophthongization of the sequence. The
digraphs ea, eo, io would thus represent monophthongs in all cases.
If Hockett's view of the value of the OE long and short digraphs were
correct, we would have another point of structural unity in Anglo-
Frisian. Old English would share the monophthongization of both
Gmc. ai and au with Old Frisian, but not the coalescense of the two.

Attention has also been drawn to the similarity of Old English
breaking and velar umlaut to the process of Old Norse (ON) breaking,
which undoubtedly shows diphthongs with stress shift (Dyvik 1978;
cf. Nielsen 1984). ON e > ea, eo before a, u, respectively, then ea, eo
> ja, jo̟ as in djarfr 'bold', jofurr 'prince'. Stress shift is also the rule in
the corresponding long diphthongs from Gmc. eu: éu > eú > jú, jó as
in djúpr 'deep', bjóða 'offer'. Though the relationship of Old Norse
breaking to the events in Old English cannot be proven, the similarity of
the two processes is undeniable.
It thus seems that the digraphs, including ea, designated diphthongs
in Old English. A further examination of stress shift may provide us
with the clue to understanding the divergent developments of Gmc. au
in Anglo-Frisian. In Old Kentish (OKt.) the diphthong ea underwent a
shift of stress to become the rising diphthong ja. A subsequent

The development of diphthongs is, however, but one of the factors
considered in determining Anglo-Frisian unity. In attempting to prove
such a linguistic unity it is customary to produce a list of common
innovations. Of such changes morphological and phonological ones
seem to be the most reliable. In describing the North Sea Germanic
group of dialects (Old English, Old Frisian, and Old Saxon), we would
consider the following list of parallels: 1. Merger of first and second
person accusative and dative personal pronouns; 2. Merger of plural
verb forms; 3. Loss of r (< z) in personal pronouns; 4. Loss of nasals
before f, s, þ; 5. Fronting of a > æ, e and WGmc. ā (< ē¹) > ā, ē ; 6.
Retraction of a > o and WGmc. ā > ō before nasals. This common
development lasted from the sixth to the tenth century, with the center
of innovation on both sides of the North Sea in southern England and
western Frisian territory (Kuhn 1956, 23ff.). If we thus posit a closer
relationship between Old English and Old Frisian, we are supported by
the following list of correspondences: 1. Unrounding of ö, ü> e, i (e);
2. Change of eg (ag) > ei (ai) word-finally and before consonants; 3.
Lengthening of vowels before mb, nd, ld, rd, rn ; 4.
Monophthongization of ai > ā. Based on the foregoing arguments, the
change of au > ea must be added to this list, making allowance for the
subsequent monophthongization of ēa > ā in Old Frisian as a result of
Akzentumsprung.
