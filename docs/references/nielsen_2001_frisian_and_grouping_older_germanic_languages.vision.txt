A Brief Survey of Research History
1.1. The Place of Frisian within the
Macrogroups of Germanic
In the major models proposed to illustrate
the earliest dialectal splits within Ger-
manic, Frisian has been classified as a
West Gmc. language. This holds true of
Schleicher's family-tree diagram from
1860 where, in Streitberg's terminology
(1896), the Proto-Gmc. trunk branched
out into East Gmc. (Goth.), West Gmc.
(OHG, OS, OE, OFr. and ODu.) and
North Gmc. (Nordic). The inspiration for
this model was clearly a geographical one
in view of the distribution of the earliest
attested Gmc. languages. Another hypo-
thesis, which took Gothic emigration from
present-day Sweden (cf. place-names like
Göt(a)land and Gotland) as its point of
departure, posited a Gotho-Nordic
branch of Gmc. prior to the beginning of
our era, contrasting with what in our first
model was labelled as WGmc. Adherents
of this theory were Lehmann (1966) and
especially Schwarz in a book from 1951.
Much in this hypothesis came to depend
on the assumption that the Goths really
did come from Scandinavia, which is not
at all certain. A third model was proposed
by Kuhn (1955) in reaction to Schwarz's
book. Kuhn thought that on their depar-
ture from northern Europe, the Goths still
spoke Proto-Gmc., and that the remaining
Nordic-West Gmc. area stayed linguisti-
cally uniform till the mid-5th century AD,
after which a split between North and
West Gmc. occurred owing to the depar-
ture of the Anglo-Saxons from the Conti-
nent and the Danish colonization of Jut-
land. By virtue of its linguistic arguments,
Kuhn's model (now known as the North-
West Gmc. theory) has won the approval
of many present-day historical linguists.
III.1. Vor- und Frühgeschichte des Friesischen
However, the concept of WGmc. has
not been accepted in all quarters. Thus
Maurer (1952), by including evidence pro-
vided by archaeology and the classical his-
torians, replaced WGmc. with as many as
three primary groups: North Sea Gmc.
(the predecessor of Fr., Saxon and Eng.),
Weser-Rhine Gmc. (which developed into
Franconian etc.) and Elbe Gmc. (which
later became Upper Ger.). Despite its du-
bious methodology, Maurer's model has
had tremendous impact on the handbooks
of Ger. historical linguistics.
For an overview of the research history
of Gmc. dialect grouping, see Nielsen
(1989, 67–107).
1.2. Anglo-Frisian and
North Sea Germanic
A knotty problem in early Gmc. dialect
geography is the subdivision of WGmc.
(cf. Nielsen 1985, 40-65). It is beyond
question that because of the High Ger-
man Consonant Shift, OHG should be
placed at one end of the WGmc. linguistic
continuum and OFr. and OE (with shared
features of their own) at the other. But
does this entitle us to operate with a
specifically Fr.-Eng. subgroup or does OS,
another northern dialect, join in, cf. the
languages listed by Maurer as descending
from North Sea Gmc.?
Although long before the latter half of
the 19th century scholars considered Fr.
and Eng. to be particularly closely related
(cf. Bremmer 1982, 79-81; 1989), there is
some justification in regarding Sweet as
the father of the Anglo-Frisian hypothesis
(cf. Sjölin 1973, 329), because Sweet
(1877, 162) was the first linguist to claim
that Anglo-Fr. was the common ancestor
of Eng. and Fr., and of the idiom spoken
both by the Anglo-Saxon invaders of Brit-
ain and by those who stayed behind.
Sweet received support in the Low Coun-
tries from Sipma (1913, 1-3) and in Ger-
many from Bremer (1900, 809f), who had
no hesitation in operating with an Anglo-
Fr. language unity in view of all the lin-
guistic features shared by OE and OFr.
(1900, 843). However, Siebs dissociated
himself from Sweet's term, preferring in-

back-vowel phonemes (u, a, cf. OE sunu

The earliest OE system has quadrangu-

group of texts, the most prominent mem-

and a (cf. OFr. and OE), where other
texts, e.g. the Cotton Heliand-manuscript

Heliand S n-stem forms nom.sg.f. thiorne
‘maiden, virgin' and nom.sg.m. uuillia
'will' as opposed to Heliand C thiorna and
uuilleo illustrate this dichotomy.

largely from Westphalia. According to
Klein (1977, 529), the phonological sys-

and /-i, -a, -o, -u/ respectively, which in
Klein's view are both later reflections of
an early OS system consisting of the four
phonemes /-i, -æ, -ɔ, -u/. This system is

great deal of orthographic variation be-

for this variation should be attributed to
the fact that in the OHG mother tongue
of the scribes there were five unaccented
short vowels/-i, -e, -a, -o, -u/ as against
four in the OS language which the scribes
were supposed to put down in writing
(Klein 1977, 390f).
Decisive for this difference in the
number of unaccented (short) vowels be-
tween OHG and OS was the North Sea
Gmc. fronting of -a to -æ, whereby the
vowel merged with -e /-æ/ < Gmc. *-ai, cf.
dat.sg.m. a-stem OHG kuninge, OE cyn-
inge, OFr. (R1) kininge ‘king', the -e suf-
fixes of which derive from *-ai (Gmc.
*kuningai). The merger between the re-
flexes of Gmc. *-ai and *-a took place in
all of the OS speech area, the merged
phoneme being rendered chiefly as -e in
the -e, -a texts and as -a in the -a, -o group,
cf. the variation between OS dat.sg.m.
cuninge (Heliand S) and cuninga (Heliand
C 696) ‘king' corresponding roughly to
that between thiorne and thiorna ‘maiden,
virgin'.
In the -e, -a group, however, the front-
ing of Gmc. *-a was followed by a shift
of -o to -a, giving this brand of OS the
same type of unaccented vocalism as that
of early OE and pre-OFr., cf. above. It is
thus possible to posit a common North
Sea Gmc. unaccented vowel system, but it
is clear that such a system could not have
come about until the fronted reflex of *-a
had coalesced with the monophthongized
descendant of *-ai. The fronting process
must have followed in the wake of the
fronting of long and short a in accented
syllables which, as we saw in 2.1., is likely
to have occurred independently in the
various North Sea Gmc. dialects. As for

The coalescence of the reflexes of Gmc.

2.3. Consonants
In OFr., the number of consonant pho-
nemes was increased through the palatali-
sation and affrication of the Gmc. velar
stops /k/ and /g/: /k/ became /ts/ before
original front vowels and j, cf. tsiāsa 'to
choose', lētza ‘doctor'; and /g/ changed
to /d²/ when geminated before j, cf. ledza
'to lay', or when occurring between n
and i, cf. keninze (< *kuningi- ) ‘king'. /k/
was not palatalized and affricated before
i-mutated vowels, cf. kenn (< Gmc.
*kunja-) ‘kin', which shows that i-umlaut
postdated the palatalisation and affrica-
tion of /k/ (and /g/). Another important
change pertains to the allophonic varia-
tion among fricatives in OFr. Except in
the case of /s/ # /z/ voice was not a dis-
tinctive feature as regards the Proto-Gmc.
fricatives, *[b, ð, g] being allophones of
*/b, d, g/ (± */f, 0, x/). In West Gmc., [ð]
coalesced with /d/, and in OFr., [b]
merged with the voiceless fricative /f/,
which like the other voiceless fricatives
became voiced in medial position ([v]).
/f/, /0/ and /s/ have the following allo-
phonic distribution in OFr.: [f] fisk ‘fish',
gref ‘grave', [v] greva 'to dig'; [0] thonkia

medial position in OFr., perhaps as a fur-

In OE, palatalized and affricated stops

the absence of affricated stop phonemes

OFr. and OE, cf. OS tiohan 'to pull'. The

treated in the same way as the OFr. and
OE ones, e.g. [f] fisk ‘fish', graf ‘grave', [v]

3. Further Chronological Deliberations
Unlike Dal and others (cf. 1.2.), Kuhn be-
lieved partly for ethnographic and ar-
chaeological reasons (1955, 41f; 26) – that
North Sea Gmc. came into being only at
the same time as or after the 5th-century
Anglo-Saxon emigration to Britain. The
linguistic innovations that came to pre-
III.1. Vor- und Frühgeschichte des Friesischen
vail, originated along the shores of the
southern North Sea region, from where
they spread into the LG and Low Franco-
nian hinterland (1955, 36–44; 46). Later
investigators have tended to accept that a
number of the North Sea Gmc. innova-
tions postdate the emigration, but main-
tain that the earliest correspondences
arose on the Continent. This holds true
of Nielsen (1985, 102-154); of Markey
(1976, 36-71) whose inventory of 36 »typ-
ically Ingvæonic« (1976, 44) parallels con-
tained three features thought to have de-
veloped before 450: (1) loss of nasals be-
fore fricatives, (2) uniform verbal pl.
endings and (3) loss of -r in monosyllabic
pronouns; and of Stiles who, in addition
to the development of a back timbre and
nasalisation in long and short a (1995,
198f, cf. also 2.1.), and the fronting of
WGmc. long ā (cf. 1.2., 2.1.) accepted
Markey's items (1) and (2) as pre-invasion
shifts.
Except for the fronting of ā, I have no
problems in assigning the contrast between
(what Krupatkin (cf. 2.1.) designated as) ǎ
and an, the loss of nasals before fricatives
(whereby a" occurring before nasals
merged with the reflexes of Gmc. a in the
sequences *anh, *ans, *anf, *anþ, cf. 2.1.),
and the uniform 1./2./3. pres. pl. endings
in, e.g., OFr. nemat(h) ‘take', OE drīfaþ,
OS drībath/drībad 'drive' (which had as
their starting point the loss of -n- in
3. pres. pl. *-anþi) to the period preceding
the Anglo-Saxon exodus (cf. also Markey
1976, 48f; 70; Krogh 1996, 331–336; Niel-
sen 1985, 118f; 149-151; for a different
view, see Kuhn 1955, 35). I agree, too, that
the loss of -r in monosyllabic pronouns
such as OFr./OS hi/he '‘he', wi ‘we', mi
‘me', OLF hě, ui, mi, OE hě, wě, mě, is a
pre-invasion change in view of the loss of
Gmc. *-z in unaccented position in
WGmc., which led to variation between
unaccented monosyllabic pronouns exhib-
iting consonantal loss and accented ones
retaining -r, the accented reflex of *-z.
Subsequent levellings brought about a
generalization of - forms in OHG (cf. er,
wir, mir) and forms without a final conso-
nant in North Sea Gmc. Further, I believe
that the addition of h- to the nom.sg.m.
