     North-Sea Germanic at the Cross-Roads:
    The Emergence of Frisian and Hollandish



Once the dust thrown up by the feet, hooves and carts of migrating
Germanic tribes and their livestock had finally settled around the year
500, the area now occupied by the Netherlands appeared to be home
to subgroups of three major tribal unities: Frisians along the coast,
Saxons in the east and Franks in the south. When written sources
begin to inform us about the area in the course of the seventh century,
Frisians appear even to have expanded the territory which they had
held in Roman times. According to Roman historians their homeland
then stretched from the Ems in the North to the Rhine in the south, i.e.
what is now called the Old Rhine, running from Utrecht to Leiden and
from there to the sea. But the late seventh-century Life of St Eloy and
Bede's early eighth-century Ecclesiastical History of the Anglo-Saxons
inform us that the Frisians had moved their borders down to the river
Scheldt (see Fig. 1) (TeBrake 1985:117-120; Blok 1969; Bakker 1962).
This information is confirmed by the fact that the southern limits of
the bishopric of Utrecht, founded by the English missionary Willibrord
in the first half of the eighth century, included both banks along the
mouth of the Scheldt (Fritze 1971; Gysseling 1962:4). Around 800, after
the Frankish kings had definitively subjected the Frisians, a capitulary
was drafted at the instigation of Charlemagne which described the
main legal rules that the Frisians were to adhere to. According to this
Lex Frisionum the Frisian territory is subdivided into three districts,
the borders of which concur with rivers: from the 'Sincfal' (a branch

From Ingvaeonic to Frisian

Not only does the lack of documentation of the early medieval language
present problems in reconstructing the linguistic past of Holland, even
the question whether there was a recognisable form of Frisian around
700 has hardly been answered.6 Frisian, just like Low Franconian, is
a West Germanic dialect, together with English, German (High Fran-
conian and Alemanic) and Low German (Saxon). However, as clearly
recognisable as these languages are today they have not always been
so. The further we go back in time, the more they approach each other,
also geographically. In any case, between ca. 200 and ca. 400, a language
must have been spoken along the coast from Calais in the south to
Husum in the north which was fairly homogeneous or rather, which
formed a continuum in which the transitions from south to north were
very gradual. This coastal language continuum is traditionally known
as 'Ingvaeonic' or, more recently, also as 'North Sea Germanic'.
   Meanwhile, soon after their arrival in Britain after 400 and detached
from their continental roots the Anglo-Saxon invading tribes started
to develop their own varieties of Ingvaeonic/North Sea Germanic,
which together are known as Old English. When, and along what
lines, the precursors of Low German, Frisian and Hollandish7 started
to evolve into recognisable linguistic entities is less clear than it is for
Old English which enjoyed the advantage of a clear historical break.
Concerning the history of the Dutch language in particular, the cur-
rent handbooks never give a check list by which we can decide whether
a given form must be Frisian rather than Franconian; their authors
tend to restrict themselves to presenting a delimitation of Frisian in
geographical terms. Even in this respect their voices do not always
sing in tune as a small sampling may demonstrate.

of Frisian into Holland. Indicative for this presence was not that Hol-

by Patrick Stiles (1995:199-200) and followed, on the whole, by Hans

1.1 Before nasals (/n, m, ηg/), WGmc a, short and long, was first
nasalised and then rounded to o by way of velarisation, e.g. *man- be-
 came OFris mon as opposed to OLFranc man; WGmc *māno became
 OFris mōna, but remained māno in OLFranc.

1.2 In the cluster of 'short vowel (= a, i, u) + nasal + voiceless fricative
(= s, f, þ), the nasal was lost with compensatory lengthening of the
preceding; if the vowel was originally an a, it ultimately became ō: e.g.
*tanþ 'tooth' became OFris tōth but remained OLFranc tanth.

1.3 In closed syllables, long ā was spontaneously raised to /æ:/, e.g.
WGmc *skāp 'sheep' became OFris skēp /´:/ but remained OLFranc
skāp.

1.4 The WGmc diphthongs ai and au became monophthongs, ā/ē and
ā in Old Frisian, and ē / ei and ō in Old Low Franconian, respectively.
E.g. *klaiþ- 'cloth' became OFris klāth but OLFranc klēd; *stain 'stone'
became OFris and OLFranc stēn; *aik 'oak' became OFris ēk and
OLFranc ēk / eik; *baum became OFris bām but OLFranc bōm.

1.5 Short a was spontaneously raised to æ in closed syllables; e.g. *staf
became OFris stef but remained OLFranc staf.

1.6 Usually before, but sometimes after front vowels, k > ts and g > j.
E.g.: *kerl 'free man' became OFris tserl but remained OLFranc kerl;
*geld 'payment' became OFris ield but remained OLFranc geld.

1.7 I-mutation. We have no indication whatsoever that long vowels
in Old Low Franconian underwent i-mutation, this in contrast to Old
Frisian, e.g. dēma 'to judge' as against OLFranc dōman.13

1.8 In (Proto-)Old Frisian short i and e were broken or diphthongised
before ≈ and ≈s / ≈t. E.g. WGmc *reht 'right' becomes OFris riucht/
riocht but remains OLFranc recht.

i-mutation were unrounded to e or ē; *kuning- 'king' > OLFranc kun-

the adjacent Ingvaeonic dialects, whilst on the other hand, Frisian
retained certain linguistic phenomena which disappeared elsewhere

 and retentions which has given Frisian its independent phonological

 and to what extent the Old Frisian situation is to be found in Holland

Discussion

For the forms in 2.1-9 from the coastal areas down to Flanders, which
correspond with Old Frisian, it is possible to recognise two main streams
of explanation in the literature. According to nineteenth-century and
early twentieth-century historical linguists these forms are traces of an
erstwhile Frisophone population. However, because a number of such
forms have been found as far south as Flanders, such an explanation
would imply that Frisian had also once been spoken there. Because such
a hypothesis cannot be backed up by extra-linguistic evidence, it was
gradually given up in favour of the Wellentheorie: the dialect spoken

along the coast was neither Franconian nor Frisian,18 but Ingvaeonic
(also called North Sea Germanic). This dialect shared many similarities
with Frisian, but was nonetheless a little different from it.
   In order to find the point where this Ingvaeonic dialect transformed
into Proto-Frisian and Proto-Hollandish, one would have to find the
earliest forms which are Frisian but not shared by any other con
tinental dialect descending from Ingvaeonic. In my opinion, this point
of departure is breaking (1.8). The fact that this sound change has
been preserved in the field-name 't Joch speaks volumes, because the
Old Frisian term tiuch is typically seen as a Kennwort, since it refers
to a certain form of land management that was practised only in the
Frisian areas, West, East and North (cf. Faltings 1984). Only on the
basis of this one word, therefore, the conclusion would be justified
that Frisians once played a significant part, also languagewise, in the
Old Rhine estuary. If this conclusion is right, then a number of still
younger developments in the chronology of Frisian sound changes can
be explained the more easily.
   Amongst such younger developments I count the transition of initial
þ to t. This is the regular development for Frisian, e.g. MoFris trije
'three', tûsen 'thousand'. In Dutch, on the other hand, the initial frica-
tive first became voiced, somewhere in the twelfth century, and then
lost its friction, resulting in d: drie, duizend. Instances of the 'Frisian'
development are the place-name Tillebreg near Hillegom, consisting
of til 'wooden bridge' (< WGmc *þelu; cf. Du deel 'plank', but Fris til)
(Blok 1959:21 [9]). Outside place-names the form is preserved in MDu
tille 'loft' as recorded from Delft and nearby sources (see MNW s.v.).
   The medial clusters -þþ- and -dþ- developed to -tt- in Frisian, some-
thing we also find in occasional forms along the coast of Holland,
e.g. in the place-name Zwet(te), cf. OFris sweththe 'border, boundary'
(Blok 1959:21 [8]), in the verb betten 'to wet' from *beþþan < *baþjan
'to bathe' (first attested 1460 in Holland), and perhaps in smitte /
smette (the latter form down to Flanders) 'smithy' from *smiþþe, cf.
MoFris. smidte (Schönfeld 1970:§50, Opm. 2). When it is the result
of compounds, the voiceless variant points to Frisian influence, e.g.
botting from bod-thing 'ordered court session'; the Franconian variant
was voiced: bodding (Blok 1991:63).

Provisional Conclusion

So what can we conclude from the survey of sound changes? Along the
coast we can distinguish between an Ingvaeonic area all the way down

                                          294

=== page 295 ===
               the emergence of frisian and hollandish



to Calais and a more limited area in which we also find a concentration
of linguistic features which are typically Frisian, including breaking.
This area stretched down as far as the Old Rhine, and perhaps a little
further, down to the Maas. Such features allow us to assume that in
the inhabitable parts of Holland between the coast, the Maas and
the IJssel, an early form of Frisian was spoken, with a concentration
on either side of the mouth of the Old Rhine. This Frisian speak-
ing area was gradually rolled back to the north. For North-Holland
a Frisian substratum is much more easily to be identified than for
South-Holland20 – not just because of typically Frisian phonological
features, but also on account of certain syntactic similarities with
Modern Frisian. This fact is indicative for a defrisianising movement in
a northerly direction which must have halted for a considerable period
of time at the northern boundary of the Rijnland. Recent archaeological
excavations confirm that the Frisian element in the northern half of
Holland is much stronger than in the southern half (Besteman 1990;
Taayke and Knol 1996: passim). The last Frisian stronghold in Hol-
land, what is called West-Friesland today, was definitively annexed
by Floris V, count of Holland, in 1289. Only then was Franconian
introduced there as the language of court and administration, and
gradually extended its influence from urban centres such as Alkmaar
and Enkhuizen to the smaller Frisian speaking communities in the
country side and on the coast. On the other hand, the proximity of
the Frisian speaking heartland a few miles away at the other side of
the mouth of the Zuyder Sea may have contributed to the preserva-
tion of certain Frisian characteristics in the local dialect of Northern
Holland until the present day.


From Frisian to Hollandish

The process of Franconisation/Francification started in South-Holland
as early as the eighth century. Important in this process were the
Frankish key-points, especially the domains which belonged to the
Frankish kings and emperors, known as koningsland or keisersland.
Gradually, the local aristocracy began to speak Franconian and will
have been followed by their servants. In addition, the clearing of
the vast peat bogs of Holland, which started in the tenth century,


                                   295

=== page 296 ===
                           rolf h. bremmer jr



brought about enormous demographic changes. These reclamations
were organised especially by the counts of Holland and the bishops
of Utrecht, and the engineers, diggers and, eventually, the colonists
often came from outside Holland, each group with its own dialect.21
It has been demonstrated that a community of immigrants with a
variegated origin often chooses the less marked linguistic forms or
opts for compromises (Nielsen 1985:84-85). This development, together
with the early urbanisation of South-Holland will have contributed to
the disappearance of the originally Frisian element in the language.
Finally, the written language in Holland, which was strongly influ-
enced by the literary language of Flanders and Brabant, will have
contributed to a situation in which very few local dialect forms have
come to the surface.22
   Along what lines did the transition from Frisian to Franconian take
place? The current handbooks on the history of the Dutch language
neither raise nor answer this question; but modern insights, based on
shift of language induced by language contact, appear to offer helpful
models, especially one that was developed by van Coetsem (1987). This
model has already been successfully applied in the context of Frisian
and Dutch dialectology by Hoekstra (1993, 1994) on the present-day
dialect situation in North-Holland and by van Bree (1994, 1997) on
the rise of Urban Frisian as it developed in the sixteenth century. Van
Coetsem's basic thesis is that in situations of language change through
contact speakers of the socially inferior language will first of all adapt
their own language phonologically to the one of the superior group.
They also adopt many words which do not occur in their own language
or dialect. On the other hand, they accommodate their morphology and
syntax much less to the new language.
   Because the indigenous Frisian dialect and the new Franconian
dialect were rather closely related – both were West Germanic lan-
guages – I assume that the indigenous population adapted their speech
especially by the application of sound substitution. This hypothesis
can be supported by the fate of the Germanic diphthongs au and ai
(1.3). The locals must have soon perceived that e.g. bām 'tree' and bān
'bean' (with ā from *au) were pronounced by the new rulers as bōm and
bōn. This correspondence was carried through with great regularity.
Words with ā from *ai, such as hām 'home', twā 'two' and klāþ 'cloth',
inadvertently became hēm, twē and klēth. Occasionally, however, they

were off the target and substituted words with ā from ai as if they
concerned words with ā from au: rāp 'rope' became rōp alongside with
the 'truly' Franconian form rēp.23
    The replacement of unrounded i-mutated coastal vowels by the
inland, Franconian ones can also be explained by way of a more
or less conscious sound substitution. In his excellent introduction
to the historical phonology of Dutch, van Loon (1986:66) assumes
on the basis of the distribution pattern of this replacement that
substitution took place word by word. Whereas the transition from
Frisian to Dutch in the Frisian towns in the sixteenth century took
 place within two generations, according to van Bree (1994), we have
 to reckon with a much longer period for the replacement of Frisian
by Franconian. The reason is that the compact urban situation in
the province of Friesland in the sixteenth century was much more
conducive to change than the early medieval situation in which
people lived scattered in often isolated communities along the coast
 of Holland.24 The gradual shift also appears from the rather confused
geographical distribution patterns of Frisian relicts in the southern
part of Holland.
    Eric Hoekstra's analysis of the Frisian substratum in the modern
 dialect of North-Holland is not wholly unproblematic. Whereas I
 have tried to demonstrate the Frisian relicts in Holland from a dia-
 chronic point of view, particularly on account of phonological criteria,
 Hoekstra based himself on a number of syntactic and morphological
 criteria from a synchronic, present-day point of view. Hoekstra does
 not consider the diachronic, historical aspect of the matter. Curiously,
 a number of the parallels between the dialect of northern Holland
 and Frisian (whether West Frisian, East Frisian or North Frisian)
 cannot be retrieved from Old Frisian, at least not in as far as is
 known to me.
    For example, Hoekstra (1994a:86-90; cf. Hoekstra 1994b) adduces
 a number of infinitival constructions in which North-Hollandish sides

     Ik gaan te heerknippen NH                ['I go to hair cut']

The near-absence in the Old Frisian corpus of instances of the construc-
tions adduced by Hoekstra can be explained in various ways, I think.
Firstly, the corpus of Old Frisian is too limited in size and genre to
contain such compounds and infinitival constructions. Secondly, the
register of the genre, predominantly legal texts, is too 'dignified' for
such compounds and infinitival constructions. They did not belong to
the written but to the colloquial language. Thirdly, these construc-
tions came into being only after 1500, when Old Frisian ceased to be
written. The last suggestion is problematic, because it presupposes

 rather intensive language contact between the various Frisian speak-
 ing areas in early modern times, areas which moreover by this time
 were no longer a geographical continuum. For the time being, then, I
 take it to be a matter of register.
    Unlike in North-Holland, where the original Frisophone population
 stopped acquiring the new Franconian language at a certain point
 in time and conventionalised their Franconian,25 in any case in a
 spoken form, the process of defrisianisation continued unabated in
 South-Holland. Apart from the demographic causes which I have
 mentioned, this process is also borne out by the fact that the name of
 Frisia / Fresia – we are still dealing with Latin sources – was given
 up in the course of the twelfth century (Blok 1969). The ruling aristo
 cracy, led by the counts, no longer felt themselves or did not want to
 feel themselves to be Frisians any longer. This break with their past
 is remarkable because the counts ultimately descended from Frisian
 noble stock. The name Radboud is often met with in the house of the
 counts of Holland and suggests a link with the Frisian King Rad-
 boud (d. 719). When Robert, the youngest son of the Flemish Count
Baldwin V, married the widow of Florent I, count of Holland, in 1063,
he was from then on called Robert 'the Frisian'. This epithet shows
how much the concept of Frisia for the coastal area was still alive in
 the eleventh century. In the twelfth century, however, all possible
 ideological resources were applied in a concerted attempt to hide the
 Frisian past of the Counts of Holland,26 a decision that will have been
 fed by their expansionist strategy that intended to subject the Frisians
 in the north of Holland. The success of this policy is evident from the
 fact that the traces of Frisian in the southern half of Holland have
 been erased almost completely.


Conclusion

Evaluating the results of my arguments, we may conclude that there
are still many gaps in the attempts to recover traces of Frisian in
Holland. Also Eric Hoekstra (1993; 1994a,b), like so many of his
predecessors, left it at random samplings. What remains to be done
especially is a minute linguistic analysis of the medieval and early
modern vernacular documents written in Holland. In his survey of the


                                  299

=== page 300 ===
                            rolf h. bremmer jr



medieval dialect features of Holland, van Loey (1968:§§123-25) often
remained superficial and sometimes even imprecise. For example,
he signalled tusken as a North-Holland form but is silent on twisken,
which I have identified as a Frisian relict. So what we want for Dutch
would be a project comparable to the Linguistic Atlas of Late Middle
English. Of course, there is Marijke Mooijaart's atlas of thirteenth-
century Dutch, but her evidence is restricted mainly to Flanders and
Brabant and hardly contains any material from Holland (because so
little of it has been left). More is to be expected of a project at the Free
University of Amsterdam which has compiled an electronic corpus
of fourteenth-century localised and dated charters (Van Reenen and
Mulder 1993). A first important result is an investigation into the
dialect of eight scribes working in the count of Holland's chancery by
Margit Rem (2003).
   The search for the possible presence of Frisian elements in Holland
does not only meet with linguistic problems. There is an ideological
component in this quest as well. Eric Hoekstra (1995) has shown how
especially the influential dialectologist Klaas Heeroma in his many
publications on Ingvaeonic tried to argue away the role of Frisian
from the history of the Dutch language. Heeroma was a propagator of
the ideal of a Greater Netherlands that included Flanders and also a
great advocate of Algemeen Beschaafd Nederlands ('Common Civilised
Dutch') – the counterpart of Received Pronunciation. Whenever he
could, Heeroma sought for new and sometimes conflicting arguments
to explain in a different way phenomena which earlier linguists had
marked as Frisian. Recognising the possibility that Frisian was once
spoken in the heart of Holland also seems to involve the problem of the
ethnic identity of the Dutch. Perhaps now, in this post-modern era in
which all the major ideological narratives have evaporated, the time
is ripe for a renewed search for the Frisian antecedents of the dialect
of Holland.
