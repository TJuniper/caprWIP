OLD ENGLISH PALATALIZATION*
By RICHARD M. HOGG
1 INTRODUCTION
By the time of the earliest recorded texts, the Germanic velar
consonants *g, *k, *h had developed into Old English as the voiced
palatal fricative [ȝ'], the voiceless palatal stop [k'] and the voiceless
palatal fricative [x'] respectively.' These changes are collectively
known as palatalization and they take place whenever an original
velar is, if initial, followed by a front vowel, if final, preceded by a
front vowel, and if medial, preceded and followed by a front vowel;
for further details see Campbell, 1959:§§ 426-9. The purpose of this
paper is to re-examine the nature of the sounds which change and the
relative chronology of the changes under discussion.² In the former
case we shall be most especially concerned with the phonological
status of Germanic *g; in the latter we shall consider the relation of
palatalization to other changes, actual or alleged, during the period in
question, notably breaking, restoration of a, palatal diphthongi-
zation, second fronting and (i-)umlaut. But we shall proceed simply
by considering palatalization in relation to each of these other
changes one by one; the question of the phonological status of the
sounds under discussion will be dealt with only in that context.
Finally, the reader is warned not to expect any major resolution of
problems; indeed, a major purpose of this paper is to suggest that
earlier scholarship has unfortunately glossed over several difficult
issues.

later than restoration of a, as in calan.

statement by Quirk and Wrenn (1957:§ 201) that 'the front

the *g which is palatalized is in fact a velar fricative [ȝ]. Historically *g
has two sources, Indo-European *gh and Indo-European *k by
Verner's Law, and theoretically this should give the velar fricative [ȝ].

the stop [g]. The exact environments are controversial, see Moulton,
1954:1 for a summary and references. Most recent scholars, e.g.
Moulton (1954:24-5), Brunner (1965:§ 206.8), Wagner (1969:163)

a nasal, in gemination and initially. In contrast, Campbell
(1959:§ 50.4) claims that initially the fricative remained. Lass and
Anderson (1975:134) suggest that fricative [ȝ] is found only between
back vowels, which implies an earlier situation where the stop [g]
occurred everywhere, except intervocalically. For the moment we
shall accept Campbell's position, with a minimal distribution of [g],
but we return to the question below, although only with respect to
initial g.

mind that supported / and r cause breaking, we could, in distinctive

voice] segments. But that is no explanation - at best it is a statement of
fact. And even on that score it seems dubious, for there seems to be no
good phonetic reason why (✗) should permit and [ȝ] inhibit the
change.

nevertheless we can surely conclude that for Old English to have had
the phoneme /ȝ/ but not the phoneme /g/ is not at all unexpected
typologically.

3 SECOND FRONTING
Having established, although perhaps not as firmly as one might have
wished, that palatalization was a later change than either breaking or
restoration of a, we can now turn to a consideration of two changes or
alleged changes which palatalization must have preceded, second
fronting and palatal diphthongization. It matters little which we
consider first, since second fronting is exclusively West Mercian, and
palatal diphthongization did not apparently occur in any Mercian
dialect. Therefore the choice of second fronting is purely arbitrary
and without chronological implication.
Second fronting is the name given to a change by which ǎ>ĕ and
ǎ > ǎ in most environments, and the change provides a useful terminus
ad quem just as restoration of a provides a terminus a quo. It will be
recalled that forms such as calan and galan (see § 2) show no
palatalization. Now since the stressed a of these verbs is due to
restoration of a from earlier *æ and since palatalization occurs before
all front vowels including æ, palatalization must be later than
restoration of a, cf. above. Now where a is due to second fronting of a
restored a, e.g. *gætu‘gates', *cæfurtun ‘hall',5 palatalization does not

=== page 97 ===

occur. Therefore palatalization must have occurred before second
fronting shifted a to æ in such forms, in other words, palatalization
must have been earlier than second fronting.
This would be as useful as it first appears to be if we were certain of
the relative chronology of second fronting, but that is a matter which
is clearly sub judice. A sampling of opinion shows that d'Ardenne
(1961) and Ball (1962), on rather different grounds, plead for a very
early dating, certainly no later than restoration of a, whereas Luick
(1964) and Campbell (1959) consider the change to have been
immediately prior to umlaut, and finally Girvan (1931) and Kuhn
(1939) suggest a dating later than umlaut. Elsewhere (Hogg, 1977) I
have argued that the change most probably occurred after umlaut,
and there is no need for me to repeat those arguments here. Clearly, a
precise dating of second fronting would be extremely helpful, but our
present state of knowledge makes that impossible.
4 PALATAL DIPHTHONGIZATION
In West Saxon (and Northumbrian in the case of a), where e or æ
would be expected after a palatal consonant, one finds instead ie or ea
respectively. This change, affecting long and short vowels equally, is
known as palatal diphthongization, and it occurs, inter alia, after
velar consonants which have been palatalized. The standard view is
that the change results in diphthongs identical to those represented
by, in the first case, ie as the umlaut of an earlier diphthong, or, in the
second case, ea as the result of breaking or from Germanic *au.
Although there is justification for this view in the light of later
identical developments thus just as seah 'saw'>seh and heah
'high'>heh, so ġeat 'gate'> ġet and ġear ‘year'>ģēr, cf. Luick
(1964:§§ 278–80), there has been much criticism of this position on
the grounds that it presents nothing more than a spelling rule which
has little phonetic plausibility. Let us therefore firstly examine this
question of phonetic plausibility.
The shift **>te, e.g. *ġetan ‘get'> ġietan, *ģēt ‘yet' > ġiet, presents
no difficulty, for the influence of a palatal fricative on the following
vowel would be to raise that vowel, incomplete assimilation giving a
diphthong, as here. Without considering the precise phonetic values
represented by ie, it is worth mentioning that there is hardly any need

to assume that the second element was [e] or [ɛ]. In fact, given the
general nature of Old English diphthongs one might expect e here to
represent a sound somewhere along or near to the diagonal joining [ǝ]
and [u]. Much greater difficulty arises with the change of *æ>ea. If
this change is to parallel that of *e> ie, then the result must have been
a partial raising of *æ accompanied by diphthongization, the second
element being low central or back. The raising need only have been
partial, since there is good reason to suppose that Old English e was a
fairly close sound, and hence the shift from *e>i would only have
been a small one. But there are two problems connected with the
diphthongization of *æ: (a) why was the result ea rather than eo? (b)
why was ea used both for the result of palatal diphthongization of *æ
and for the result of breaking of *æ?
The answer to the first question is perfectly straightforward and
needs no elaboration; it is that the raising can have been of no greater
order than [æ]>[ɛ], see above, and hence eo would be inappropriate.
The second question is trickier, for we are faced with the puzzle that
assimilation of *a to a following velar segment and assimilation of *æ
to a preceding palatal segment, which are directly opposing shifts,
resulted in the same orthographic answer. Yet we ought to remember
that although the movements were in opposite directions, they were
not great, so that the maximum difference must have been something
like [ɛə] in ġeat against [aa] in seah, with [ɛ] and [a] both being
allophones of /æ/ and the second element in seah being prone to
reduction. Further, the Old English scribes had only a limited number
of orthographic devices and they were in the position, after palatal
diphthongization, of having to decide whether the vocalic nucleus of
ġeat, etc. was better represented by e, æ, eo or ea. Of these, only æ or ea
seem acceptable and it was the latter which was chosen in West Saxon
and Northumbrian. If this argument is satisfactory, I see no phonetic
objection to treating palatal diphthongization as a genuine sound
change.

5 UMLAUT
Umlaut is the name given to a set of changes in Old English by which
back vowels are fronted and short front vowels raised when [i] or [j]
stands in the following syllable. The umlaut occurred regardless of
intervening consonant (although some consonant clusters could
cause failure of the change) and, crucial for our purposes, it occurred
even when no consonant intervened, e.g. dep 'he does' <*dōiþ, cf. fēt
'feet'<*fotiz.
It has regularly been maintained that palatalization must have
preceded umlaut, for *ȝ and *k were not palatalized before front
vowels which were the product of umlaut. This results in minimal
pairs such as cēn ‘torch’<*kēn vs. cēne ‘bold’<*kōnjaz. But the
situation is not quite as clearcut as it first appears to be. The back
vowels subject to umlaut are *ň, *ð, *ǎ and their umlauts are,
respectively, i, o, ǎ. Only at a later stage was ỗ unrounded to ĕ and,
later still, changed to in most cases. The unroundings are distinct
from the umlaut. Thus, in order to show that palatalization was
earlier than umlaut it is insufficient to cite minimal pairs such as ĉēn
and cēne, for that only shows that palatalization preceded unround-

This paper has been primarily concerned with the relative chronology
of Old English palatalization and secondarily concerned with the
nature of the sounds palatalized, yet it is on the latter score that we
have more cause for satisfaction. The most vexatious change is that of
Germanic *g, and we have been able to show that originally this was
most probably the voiced velar fricative [ȝ] and that [ȝ] was
palatalized to [ȝ'] which quickly merged with [j] from Germanic *j.
This, of course, does not hold for geminate *gg or *g after nasals, but
they were of no relevance to our principal concerns.
The relative chronology of palatalization was less happily dealt
with. Four changes during the Primitive Old English period were of
crucial importance: breaking, second fronting, palatal diphthongi-
zation and umlaut. We were able to establish that palatalization was
later than breaking, although in itself that causes problems, since the
failure of breaking to occur before [ȝ] is unexplained. We also
determined that palatalization was earlier than second fronting, but
that was not too helpful since the chronology of second fronting is
itself uncertain. Further, we offered very little new evidence in favour
of or against the difficult change known as palatal diphthongization.
Therefore the onus of establishing a terminus ad quem for palatali-
zation lay on the evidence provided by umlaut. And that evidence was
hopelessly contradictory. This paper, therefore, can claim only to
show that the problems surrounding palatalization still lie at the
forefront of Old English phonological studies.
