p. 232 — the received account, and the five inferred developments
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Opening: "In comparative Germanic grammar it is a commonplace
assumption that IE ē first became ǣ in Proto-Germanic and then
developed into ā in the parent forms of both North and West Germanic."
Bennett quotes Prokosch (Comp. Gmc. Gr. §34; cf. Hirt): "In Gothic, ǣ
reverts to ē by a different process of tensing (raising and fronting);
in North and West Germanic, ā results; this develops secondarily into
ǣ in Old English, and ē in Frisian."

Distribution as Bennett states it: "The Proto-Germanic vowel ǣ appears
as ǣ only in West Saxon Old English; the other Old English dialects,
together with Old Frisian and Gothic, show ē. Old Saxon, Old Low
Franconian, Old High German, and Old Norse have ā, but Old Saxon also
exhibits individual instances of ē, e.g. bērun gēbun beside OHG bārun
gābun, OIcel. bāru gāfu, WS OE bǣron gēafon (< *gǣfon), Go. bērun
gēbun, etc."

The five inferred developments (printed as a table, p. 232):

    ON, OHG, OLFr...   ē > ǣ > ā
    Go..............   ē > ǣ > ē
    OS..............   ē > ǣ > ā (ē)
    WS OE...........   ē > ǣ > ā > ǣ
    Non-WS OE, OFris.  ē > ǣ > ā > ǣ > ē

Bennett's objection: "In comparison with the simple, completely
attested change of IE ā to PGmc. ō, some of these developments appear
to be rather complicated and speculative. As a whole, they represent
five phonological patterns. Old Norse, Old Low Franconian, and Old High
German, to be sure, show a wholly comprehensible tendency to lower ē to
ǣ and then to retract this to ā, but the inferred developments in the
other Old Germanic languages show no such directional trend. So far as
Gothic is concerned, we must assume that ē first became ǣ and then
returned to ē. ... The West Saxon Old English development requires the
supposition that ē became ǣ, then ā, then ǣ again. Finally, for Old
Frisian and the non-West-Saxon dialects of Old English, we must believe
that ē changed to ǣ, then to ā, then to ǣ again, and finally back to ē."

Footnote 1 records that a few scholars, e.g. Noreen, assume IE ē was
already an open ǣ.

p. 233 — no direct attestation; the case against a common WGmc. *ā
--------------------------------------------------------------------

"The ǣ-vowel of Proto-Germanic, unlike the ō-vowel, is not supposed to
be preserved directly in any historic Germanic language, West Saxon OE
ǣ being traced back to WGmc. ā and only indirectly to PGmc. ǣ."

Summary diagram as printed:

    IE ē > PGmc. ǣ >  ┌ ON ā
                      │ Go. ē
                      └ WGmc. ā > ┌ WS OE ǣ
                                  │ Non-WS OE, OFris. ē
                                  │ OS ā, but also ē
                                  └ OHG, OLFr. ā

"The assumed developments merely summarize the historical facts without
attempting to explain them. ... As a result, Germanic appears to show
no one phonological trend; if any tendency is discernible in this
scheme, it is one leading toward an inexplicable diversity."

Against a common parent West Germanic ā, two considerations are
examined and rejected. First, although OS, OLFr and OHG generally show
ā for IE ē, "Old Saxon still preserves instances of ē (gēbun etc.) in
the 9th century. Furthermore, the first foreign records of Old High
German show ē, not ā; Caesar, for example, refers to the Old High
German Swabians as Suēbi, not *Suābi, and their leaders are designated
by such names as Hariomērus, not *-mārus." Second, the parallel with
Latin loans (Lat. nāpus > OE nǣp (Ep.), nēp (Corp.) 'turnip'; Lat.
strāta > OHG strāza, strāzza, WS OE strǣt 'street') is rejected: "we
know that ā occurred in Latin, but this fact gives us no reason to
infer that the 'same sound' occurred in the parent West Germanic speech
or underwent the 'same developments' in the separate West Germanic
languages." Compare parent WGmc. ē, which "remained unchanged in Old
English (*hēr > hēr 'here')", while the same sound in early Latin loans
became ī or i (cēpa > cīpe 'onion', dēnārius > dinor 'denarius').
Conclusion: "the parent WGmc. ā in such assumed developments as ē > ǣ >
ā > ǣ > ē is by no means indispensable."

Footnote 3: "Wright (OE Gr. §113), unlike most scholars, does not
postulate a WGmc. ā in this development but assumes that PGmc. ǣ
remained in WS OE and was raised to ē in Kent. and Angl."

pp. 234–235 — the wave hypothesis
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Bennett proposes a chronological and geographical account. The PGmc
homeland embraced southern Scandinavia, Denmark, and the north German
lowlands between the Elbe and the Oder. The Gothic emigration eastward
occurred in the last few centuries B.C.; South ('West') Germanic
expansion proceeded more slowly up to about 500 A.D.

"It may be worthy of notice that Gothic ('East' Germanic), the first
known Germanic language to be carried from the homeland into a separate
colony, shows no tendency whatever to change ē to ā. In the west, the
same is true of Anglian Old English and Old Frisian ..."

p. 234, foot: "If we consider only the ... 'East' and 'West' Germanic
tribes, the change of ē to ā does not appear at all along the far
eastern or far western periphery. On the other hand, ē regularly became
ā in North Germanic and in those languages that remained relatively
contiguous with it after the first expansions to the east and west."

"The change of ē through ǣ to ā apparently began in the north of the
homeland and spread gradually toward the south. By the time that it had
reached the district originally inhabited by the Goths, Gothic (at
least in the form that we know it) had been carried far to the east and
so was not affected. In northern Germany the change of ē to ā likewise
had no effect on the speech of the Frisians or northern Angles, who had
migrated to the west. Continental Saxon, which remained mostly in the
path of the change, generally shows ā, but insular West Saxon, which
was" [p. 235] "carried to the west before the change was completed,
shows the intermediate stage ǣ. Low Franconian and High German remained
in the path of the change and developed ā; as noted above, the first
foreign records of High German still preserve the old ē."

"This hypothesis (it is nothing more) attempts merely to account for
the facts. It does not alter, of course, the historical correspondences
among the cognate vowels ē, ǣ, and ā in the Old Germanic languages,⁶
nor does it deny that there was a difference between the two
Proto-Germanic vowels that developed from IE ē and ēi. When we are
faced with the necessity of accepting at least five such unproved
suppositions as ē > ǣ > ā > ǣ > ē, with no apparent agreement among the
languages and no discernible phonological trend, a more credible
interpretation of the facts appears to be required. If it is true that
the change of ē to ā began in the north and spread toward the south,
missing or only partly affecting the more peripheral areas, the
development of IE ē in Germanic is much less complicated than has been
supposed."

p. 235 n. 6 — the *w / *g conditioning (verbatim)
--------------------------------------------------------------------

"⁶ The results of secondary combinative changes in the historic
languages remain the same, regardless of the phonological developments
that are postulated for preliterary times. WS Old English, for example,
shows ǣ as a regular isolative development of IE ē but has ā before w
or g plus a back vowel (analogical forms like wǣgas for wāgas 'waves',
sg. wǣg, also occur). If WS ǣ developed from a WGmc. ā, as usually
assumed, the WS ā in such forms is simply a combinative retention of
the older vowel. If ā is the later development, it represents a
combinative change and shows that the shift of ē to ā occurred in
insular as well as continental West Germanic, though only under certain
specific conditions."

Footnote 5 (p. 234) concedes areal mixture: "If our knowledge of the
early tribal movements were more extensive, we should probably find a
number of instances in which ē-, ǣ-, and ā-speakers came into close and
lasting contact. Certainly there were both Frisians and Saxons along the
Litus Saxonicum, and a number of Frisians found their way into England.
... In such instances there may well have been variant pronunciations of
the IE ē-vowel within a given area, with one development or another
finally predominating."
